Classical physics describes forces with fields: a charge fills space with an electric field, and other charges feel it. Quantum theory adds that every field comes in lumps. The lumps of the electromagnetic field are photons, and in the modern picture every fundamental force except gravity has its own carrier particles, all of them **bosons** (particles with whole-number spin).

## The photon and QED

The quantum theory of light and electrons, **quantum electrodynamics** (QED), was completed around 1948 by Richard Feynman, Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga, building on earlier work by Dirac and others. In QED two electrons repel by exchanging photons. Feynman drew each process as a diagram: lines for particles, wiggles for photons, each with a precise mathematical term. QED predicts the electron's magnetic moment to better than one part in a billion, making it the most precisely tested theory in science.

The exchanged photons are called **virtual**: they are terms in a calculation, not particles that could be caught in a detector, and the picture of little balls thrown back and forth should not be taken literally. What is real is the rule that comes out of it, which Yukawa first used: a force carried by a massive particle has a short range.

## The weak force

The weak force causes beta decay, in which a neutron turns into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino. Enrico Fermi wrote the first theory of it in 1934, treating the four particles as meeting at a point. That worked at low energy but gave nonsense, probabilities above 100%, at high energy. A carrier particle would spread the interaction out and cure the problem, but the weak force acts over less than a thousandth of a proton's width, so the carrier must be very heavy.

::: history 1961–1968 · Harvard, Imperial College and MIT
Sheldon Glashow, Abdus Salam and Steven Weinberg built a single **electroweak** theory in which electromagnetism and the weak force are two faces of one interaction. It needed three heavy carriers: W⁺ and W⁻, which change a particle's charge, and a neutral Z⁰. The Z predicted a new kind of weak interaction with no charge exchange, a "neutral current". The Gargamelle bubble chamber at CERN saw it in 1973.
:::

The theory also predicted the W and Z masses. That set the force's range.

::: steps The range of the weak force
$ R \approx \frac{\hbar c}{m c^2}
Yukawa's estimate: a carrier of rest energy mc² gives a force of range roughly ħc divided by that energy.
---
$ m_W c^2 \approx 80.4\,\text{GeV} = 80\,400\,\text{MeV}
The measured W mass, about 86 times the proton's.
---
$ R \approx \frac{197\,\text{MeV fm}}{80\,400\,\text{MeV}} \approx 2.5 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{fm}
That is 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁸ m, a few thousandths of a proton radius.
---
$ m_\gamma = 0 \Rightarrow R \to \infty
The photon is massless, so the same estimate gives no limit at all: electromagnetism has infinite range, falling off as 1/r² for ever.
:::

The weak force is "weak" mainly because of this short range: two particles rarely come close enough to feel it. At energies near 100 GeV, where W and Z can be made directly, it is about as strong as electromagnetism.

::: history 1983 · CERN, Geneva
Carlo Rubbia persuaded CERN to turn its SPS accelerator into a collider of protons and antiprotons, using Simon van der Meer's method of cooling the antiproton beam. In January 1983 the UA1 and UA2 experiments reported W particles, and in May the Z, at the predicted masses (W about 80 GeV, Z about 91 GeV). Rubbia and van der Meer received the Nobel Prize the following year.
:::

## Gluons

The strong force between quarks is carried by **gluons**. Like the photon they are massless, but unlike the photon they carry the charge of their own force, colour, so gluons attract other gluons. There are eight kinds. That self-interaction is what makes the strong force grow with distance and confine quarks, so although gluons are massless the strong force reaches only about 1 fm. In 1979 the PETRA collider at DESY in Hamburg saw electron–positron collisions produce three jets of hadrons instead of two: a quark, an antiquark and a gluon radiated by one of them.

## And gravity?

Gravity is expected to have a carrier too, the **graviton**: massless, since gravity has infinite range, and with spin 2. No one has detected one, and individual gravitons are probably beyond any conceivable detector. More seriously, no one has a working quantum theory of gravity, so the graviton has no place in the Standard Model.

::: note
Each force is carried by bosons. The carrier's mass sets the range: massless photons give infinite range, the 80–91 GeV W and Z give a range of about 10⁻¹⁸ m, and massless gluons are confined by their own colour charge.
:::
