Classical physics describes forces with fields. A charge fills the space around it with an electric field, a magnet with a magnetic field, and another charge placed in that field feels a push. Quantum theory keeps the fields but adds a twist: every field comes in lumps. The energy of the electromagnetic field cannot take any value; it comes in packets called photons. In the modern picture each fundamental force, except gravity, has its own field and so its own particles, the lumps of that field, and all of them are **bosons**, particles whose spin is a whole number (the particles of matter, by contrast, have spin ½). This lesson goes through the forces one at a time, and shows how a single idea, that the mass of the carrier sets the range of the force, ties together electromagnetism, the weak force and, in a surprising way, the strong force.

## How do photons make a force?

The quantum theory of light and electrons is called **quantum electrodynamics**, or QED. It was completed around 1948 by Richard Feynman, Julian Schwinger and Sin-Itiro Tomonaga, working independently and in quite different styles, building on two decades of earlier work by Paul Dirac and others; Freeman Dyson then showed that their three approaches were the same theory. The difficulty they overcame was that earlier calculations gave infinite answers whenever they went beyond the simplest approximation. The three found a consistent way to absorb the infinities into the measured charge and mass of the electron, after which every remaining prediction was finite.

In QED two electrons repel each other by exchanging photons. Feynman found a way to draw each contribution to a process as a small diagram, with straight lines for electrons and wavy lines for photons, and each diagram stands for a precise mathematical term. Adding up the diagrams gives the answer, with more complicated diagrams contributing smaller and smaller corrections. The method works astonishingly well. QED predicts the strength of the electron's magnetism, its magnetic moment, and the prediction agrees with measurement to better than one part in a billion, which makes it one of the most precisely tested theories in all of science.

The photons exchanged between two electrons are called **virtual** photons, and here a careful word is needed. They are terms in a calculation, not particles that could be caught in a detector, and the popular picture of two skaters throwing balls back and forth should not be taken literally; for one thing, it cannot explain attraction. What the exchange picture does capture correctly is a rule that Hideki Yukawa first used in 1935: a force carried by a massless particle has unlimited range, and a force carried by a massive particle has a short range, roughly ħc divided by the carrier's rest energy, where ħ is the reduced Planck constant. The heavier the carrier, the shorter the reach.

## Why is the weak force weak?

The **weak force** is responsible for beta decay, in which a neutron turns into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino. It also drives the first step of fusion in the Sun. Enrico Fermi wrote the first theory of it in 1934, imagining the four particles meeting at a single point and interacting there. At the low energies of radioactive decay the theory worked very well. But at high energies it gave nonsense: probabilities for some processes grew without limit and eventually exceeded 100%, which is impossible.

The cure was to stop the four particles from meeting at a point by having the interaction carried by a particle, which spreads it out over a small distance, as the photon does for electromagnetism. But the weak force acts only over a tiny distance, around a thousandth of a proton's width, so by Yukawa's rule its carrier had to be very heavy. And there was a deeper difficulty. Theories in which force carriers have mass tend to go wrong at high energies in just the way Fermi's had. The way out was to bring electromagnetism into the same theory.

::: history 1961–1971 · Europe and the United States
Sheldon Glashow in 1961, and then Abdus Salam and Steven Weinberg independently in 1967 and 1968, built a single **electroweak** theory in which electromagnetism and the weak force are two faces of one interaction. The theory used the Higgs mechanism, the subject of a later lesson, to give mass to the weak carriers while leaving the photon massless. It needed three heavy carriers: the W⁺ and W⁻, which carry electric charge and so change the charge of the particle that emits them, and a neutral one, the Z⁰. For several years few people took the theory seriously, because no one could show it gave finite answers. In 1971 a Dutch graduate student, Gerard 't Hooft, working with his supervisor Martinus Veltman, proved that it did, and attitudes changed almost overnight. Glashow, Salam and Weinberg shared the 1979 Nobel Prize.
:::

The Z made a sharp prediction. Every weak process known at the time changed the charge of the particles involved: in beta decay a neutral neutron becomes a positive proton. A neutral carrier would allow a new kind of weak interaction in which no charge changes hands, for example a neutrino bouncing off an electron or a nucleus and staying a neutrino. This was called a **neutral current**. In 1973 the Gargamelle bubble chamber at CERN, exposed to a beam of neutrinos, recorded exactly such events. The Z existed, even before it had been seen directly.

The theory also predicted the masses of the W and Z, about 80 and 90 GeV (billion electronvolts), and the mass sets the range.

::: steps The range of the weak force
$ R \approx \frac{\hbar c}{m c^2}
This is Yukawa's estimate: a force carried by a particle of rest energy mc² has a range of roughly ħc divided by that energy. It follows because a massive carrier can exist only briefly in the exchange, and in that time it cannot travel much further than this distance even at nearly the speed of light.
---
$ m_W c^2 \approx 80.4\,\text{GeV} = 80\,400\,\text{MeV}
The measured rest energy of the W is about 80.4 GeV, about 86 times the proton's. Converting it to MeV puts it in the same units as ħc, which is usually quoted in MeV times femtometres.
---
$ R \approx \frac{197\,\text{MeV fm}}{80\,400\,\text{MeV}} \approx 2.5 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{fm}
Divide ħc ≈ 197 MeV·fm by the W's rest energy; the MeV cancels and leaves a distance in femtometres (1 fm is 10⁻¹⁵ m). The range is about 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁸ metres, a few thousandths of a proton's radius.
---
$ m_\gamma = 0 \Rightarrow R \to \infty
Apply the same estimate to the photon, whose mass is zero. Dividing by zero rest energy gives no limit at all. That is why electromagnetism has infinite range, its force falling off as 1/r² for ever, while the weak force dies away almost at once.
:::

This explains the weak force's name. It is "weak" mainly because of its short range: two particles must come extraordinarily close to feel it, and they rarely do, so weak processes are rare and slow. The strength of the interaction itself is not small. At energies near 100 GeV, where particles collide hard enough to make real W and Z particles and probe distances comparable to the range, the weak force is about as strong as electromagnetism. That is the sense in which the two are one force.

::: history 1983 · CERN, Geneva
To make W and Z particles directly needed collisions far more energetic than any machine of the 1970s could produce. Carlo Rubbia persuaded CERN to convert its largest accelerator, the Super Proton Synchrotron, into a collider in which protons and antiprotons, circling in opposite directions in the same ring, would meet head on. The hard part was gathering enough antiprotons into a tight beam, and that was made possible by Simon van der Meer's method of "stochastic cooling", which nudged the antiprotons into line using signals from the beam itself. In January 1983 the UA1 and UA2 experiments announced the W, and in May the Z, at masses of about 80 and 91 GeV, just as predicted. Rubbia and van der Meer received the Nobel Prize the following year.
:::

## Gluons, which carry their own charge

The strong force between quarks is carried by **gluons**, eight kinds of them. Like the photon, gluons are massless, and by Yukawa's rule that should give the strong force an infinite range. Yet it acts only within about a femtometre. The resolution lies in one crucial difference between gluons and photons.

A photon carries no electric charge, so photons do not attract or repel one another; light beams pass straight through each other. A gluon, by contrast, carries **colour**, the charge of the strong force, and so gluons attract other gluons. The field lines between two quarks therefore do not spread out through space, as electric field lines do; the gluons pull one another into a narrow tube joining the quarks. A field squeezed into a tube does not weaken with distance, so the force between the quarks stays constant however far apart they are pulled, until the energy in the tube creates new quarks and the tube breaks. This is the confinement described in the previous lesson. The strong force has a short range not because its carrier is heavy but because colour itself never gets far on its own: only colour-neutral combinations, the hadrons, travel freely.

Gluons were first seen, indirectly, in 1979 at the PETRA collider at DESY in Hamburg. When an electron and a positron annihilate, they usually produce a quark and an antiquark flying apart, which show up as two back-to-back jets of hadrons. Occasionally the PETRA experiments saw three jets instead. The third was a gluon, radiated by one of the quarks and turned into hadrons in the same way.

## And gravity?

Gravity is expected to have a carrier too, called the **graviton**. Since gravity reaches across the universe, the graviton should be massless, and the theory of how gravity couples to energy says its spin should be 2. No one has ever detected one. Gravity is so weak between individual particles that a single graviton would be all but impossible to catch, and estimates suggest no conceivable detector could do it. More seriously, no one has a working quantum theory of gravity: the methods that tamed the infinities of QED fail for gravity. So the graviton is a reasonable expectation, but it has no place in the Standard Model, the theory of the other three forces described in the next lesson.

::: note
Each force is carried by bosons, and the carrier's mass sets the force's range. Massless photons give electromagnetism infinite range, while the heavy W and Z, at 80 and 91 GeV, confine the weak force to about 10⁻¹⁸ metres. Gluons are massless, but because they carry colour charge they bind each other into tubes, and the strong force is confined by its own carriers.
:::
