In 1932 the list of the basic building blocks of matter was short and satisfying. There were protons and neutrons in the nucleus, electrons around it, and photons, the particles of light, carrying energy between them. Everything in the world seemed to be made of three kinds of particle held together by known forces. Thirty years later the list of supposedly elementary particles ran past a hundred, and physicists had begun, only half joking, to call it a zoo. This lesson tells how that happened: how the new particles were found, first in radiation falling from the sky and then in machines built to make them, how their properties were read from the tracks they left, and why their sheer number became a clue that something simpler lay underneath.

## Radiation from the sky

The story starts with a nuisance. An **electroscope**, a pair of thin metal leaves that spring apart when charged, slowly loses its charge even when it is perfectly insulated. The reason is that the air around it is being ionised, its molecules split into charged fragments, which carry the charge away. Around 1900 everyone assumed the ionising radiation came from radioactive elements in the ground. If so, it should fade the higher you went.

::: history 1912 · a balloon over Austria
Victor Hess, an Austrian physicist, tested the idea directly. He carried electroscopes up in a balloon, in a series of flights reaching about 5 kilometres, and measured how fast they discharged. At first the rate fell, as expected. But above a kilometre or so it began to rise, and at the greatest heights it was several times higher than at the ground. He flew at night and during a near-total eclipse of the Sun and found the same, so the Sun was not the source. The radiation was coming from above, from space. Robert Millikan later named it **cosmic rays**. Hess shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1936.
:::

Cosmic rays turned out to be mostly protons, with some heavier nuclei, arriving from space at very high energies. When one strikes a nucleus in the upper atmosphere it shatters it, and the fragments go on to hit more nuclei, producing a shower of secondary particles that rains down to the ground. Here, for the first time, was a natural source of collisions far more violent than anything a laboratory could produce.

To see what was in the showers, physicists used the **cloud chamber**, a box of air saturated with vapour and suddenly cooled, so that the vapour is ready to condense. A charged particle passing through ionises the air along its path, and droplets form on the ions, leaving a visible trail that can be photographed. Place the chamber in a magnetic field and the tracks curve, because a magnetic field pushes a moving charge sideways. The direction of the curve gives the sign of the charge, and its radius gives the particle's momentum: a faster, heavier particle is harder to bend and curves more gently.

## A particle that should not exist, and another nobody wanted

::: history 1932 · Pasadena, California
Carl Anderson, at Caltech, was photographing cosmic-ray tracks in a cloud chamber with a strong magnet. In August 1932 he found a track that curved like an electron's but the wrong way. That could mean either a positive particle moving down or an electron moving up, and the curvature alone cannot tell which. Anderson had placed a lead plate across the middle of his chamber for exactly this reason. A particle crossing the plate loses energy, so it must curve more tightly after crossing than before. The tighter curve was above the plate, so the particle had come up through it, and it was positive. Its track was far too thin and long for a proton. It was a positive particle with the electron's mass: the **positron**, the antiparticle of the electron. Paul Dirac's relativistic equation for the electron, published in 1928, had implied that such a particle must exist, and Dirac had predicted it outright in 1931.
:::

The next surprise was less welcome. Since 1935 the Japanese physicist Hideki Yukawa had been arguing that the force holding the nucleus together must be carried by a new particle, passed back and forth between protons and neutrons. He saw that the mass of such a particle could be estimated from the range of the force, because a heavier carrier would not get far.

::: steps Yukawa's mass estimate
$ \Delta E \, \Delta t \approx \hbar
Quantum mechanics links an uncertainty in energy, ΔE, to a span of time, Δt, through the reduced Planck constant ħ. Read loosely, it says that a particle of rest energy mc² can appear "on loan" for a time of order ħ/(mc²) without violating energy conservation over longer times. This is a heuristic rather than a derivation, and the loan picture should not be taken literally, but the full theory gives the same relation between mass and range.
---
$ R \approx c\,\Delta t \approx \frac{\hbar c}{mc^2}
During that time, even a carrier moving at nearly the speed of light covers only about cΔt. That distance sets the range R of the force: beyond it, the carrier cannot reach. So the heavier the carrier, the shorter the range.
---
$ mc^2 \approx \frac{\hbar c}{R}
Turn the relation around to give the carrier's rest energy in terms of the range. Now a measured range gives a predicted mass, which is what Yukawa needed.
---
$ mc^2 \approx \frac{197\,\text{MeV fm}}{1.4\,\text{fm}} \approx 140\,\text{MeV}
The nuclear force reaches about 1.4 fm (a femtometre, 10⁻¹⁵ m), and ħc is about 197 MeV·fm in nuclear units. The carrier's rest energy comes out at about 140 MeV, roughly 270 times the electron's mass and about a seventh of the proton's.
:::

So the carrier should lie between the electron and the proton in mass. In 1936 Anderson and Seth Neddermeyer found exactly such a particle in cosmic rays, about 207 times the electron's mass, with a rest energy of about 106 MeV. That was close enough to Yukawa's estimate for everyone to assume this was his particle.

It was not. A carrier of the nuclear force must interact strongly with nuclei, and should be swallowed by them almost immediately. In 1947 an experiment in Rome by Marcello Conversi, Ettore Pancini and Oreste Piccioni showed that the new particle, when slowed down in matter, passed by nuclei almost without interacting. It was not a nuclear particle at all but a heavy copy of the electron, with the same charge and the same indifference to the nuclear force. It is now called the **muon**. Nothing in physics called for it, and nobody could say what it was for. "Who ordered that?" asked Isidor Rabi.

The muon did, though, give one of the first everyday tests of relativity. Muons are made in cosmic-ray showers about 15 kilometres up, and at rest a muon lives only about 2.2 microseconds before decaying. Even at nearly the speed of light, that is enough time to travel only about 660 metres. Yet muons reach the ground in large numbers. The reason is that, seen from the ground, a fast-moving muon's internal clock runs slow, so it lives much longer in our frame than at rest.

::: model light-clock
Push the speed toward c and watch the time dilation factor γ grow. At 0.999c it is about 22, so a muon lives about 22 times longer in our frame and can travel over 14 kilometres before it decays.
:::

## Yukawa's particle at last

::: history 1947 · Bristol, England
Cecil Powell's group in Bristol used a different detector: thick photographic emulsions, which record the track of every charged particle that passes through them and can be left out for weeks. They took plates up mountains, to the Pic du Midi in the Pyrenees and Mount Chacaltaya in Bolivia, where cosmic rays are more intense. Scanning the developed plates under microscopes, César Lattes, Giuseppe Occhialini and Powell found tracks of a particle of about 140 MeV that came to a stop and then decayed, producing a muon. This was Yukawa's carrier, now called the **pi meson** or **pion**, and unlike the muon it interacted strongly with nuclei. The muon was simply what it decayed into. Yukawa received the Nobel Prize in 1949, and Powell in 1950.
:::

The puzzle seemed solved, and for a few months the list looked tidy again. The same year, though, George Rochester and Clifford Butler in Manchester found cloud-chamber photographs of forked tracks, like an inverted V, made by neutral particles heavier than the pion decaying into pairs of charged ones. These were the first **kaons**, and more of their relatives followed. They were called **strange particles**, and the name was meant literally. They were produced often in collisions, which meant the strong nuclear force made them, yet they lived about 10⁻¹⁰ seconds before decaying. A particle that the strong force can make should be able to decay through the strong force too, in about 10⁻²³ seconds. These lived about ten trillion times longer, as if something forbade the quick decay. That puzzle would lead, in the next lesson, to a new conserved quantity called strangeness.

## From the sky to the laboratory

Cosmic rays were a generous but erratic source: you could not choose the energy, aim them, or decide when they arrived. To study the new particles systematically, physicists built accelerators powerful enough to make them on demand. The Cosmotron at Brookhaven, on Long Island, reached about 3 GeV (billion electronvolts) in 1953. The Bevatron at Berkeley reached about 6 GeV in 1954, an energy chosen so that it could make a proton and an antiproton together, and in 1955 Owen Chamberlain and Emilio Segrè used it to find the **antiproton**.

Detectors improved as well. In 1952 Donald Glaser invented the **bubble chamber**, a tank of liquid heated just past its boiling point and held under pressure. When the pressure is released, a charged particle passing through leaves a string of tiny bubbles along its path. A liquid is tens to hundreds of times denser than the gas in a cloud chamber, so far more collisions happen inside it, and the chamber can be photographed every time the accelerator fires. Luis Alvarez's group at Berkeley built large bubble chambers filled with liquid hydrogen, whose nuclei are single protons, and analysed millions of photographs with semi-automatic measuring machines.

What they found was a flood. Many new particles, called **resonances**, lived only about 10⁻²³ seconds, far too short to leave a track. They could be detected only indirectly: when physicists added up the energies and momenta of the particles coming out of a collision, certain combinations showed a peak at a particular mass, which revealed a short-lived particle that had formed and immediately decayed. By the early 1960s the count of strongly interacting particles was past a hundred, with names spread across the Greek alphabet. Enrico Fermi is said to have told a student, "If I could remember the names of all these particles, I'd have been a botanist."

::: note
Each new particle was identified by measuring its tracks: the curvature in a magnetic field gives its charge and momentum, and the track's length and decay give its lifetime. The sheer number of strongly interacting particles, more than a hundred by the 1960s, was itself the clue that they could not all be elementary.
:::
