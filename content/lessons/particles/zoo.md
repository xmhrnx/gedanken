By 1932 matter seemed simple: protons and neutrons in the nucleus, electrons around it, and photons carrying light. Thirty years later the list of "elementary" particles ran past a hundred. They came first from the sky, then from machines built to make them.

## Rays from space

::: history 1912 · a balloon over Austria
Victor Hess carried electroscopes, instruments that slowly discharge when ionizing radiation passes, up to about 5 km in a balloon. The discharge rate fell at first and then rose steeply with height, so the radiation was coming from above, not from rocks in the ground. Robert Millikan later named it **cosmic rays**. Hess shared the 1936 Nobel Prize.
:::

Cosmic rays are mostly high-energy protons from space. Each one that hits a nucleus in the upper atmosphere makes a shower of secondary particles. Physicists photographed their tracks in **cloud chambers**, boxes of supersaturated vapour in which a charged particle leaves a trail of droplets. A magnetic field curves the tracks; the direction of curvature gives the sign of the charge and the radius gives the momentum.

## Antimatter and an unwanted cousin

::: history 1932 · Pasadena, California
Carl Anderson saw a track that curved like an electron's but the wrong way. A lead plate across the chamber showed which way the particle was travelling, since it lost energy and curved more tightly after crossing it. It was a positive particle with the electron's mass: the **positron**, the antiparticle Paul Dirac's equation had implied in 1928 and that he had predicted outright in 1931.
:::

In 1936 Anderson and Seth Neddermeyer found another cosmic-ray particle, about 207 times heavier than the electron. At first it looked like the particle Hideki Yukawa had proposed in 1935 to carry the nuclear force, and Yukawa had estimated its mass from the range of that force.

::: steps Yukawa's mass estimate
$ \Delta E \, \Delta t \approx \hbar
A particle of rest energy mc² can exist "on loan" for a time of order ħ/(mc²). This energy–time argument is a heuristic; the full theory gives the same scale without the loan picture.
---
$ R \approx c\,\Delta t \approx \frac{\hbar c}{mc^2}
Even at nearly light speed the carrier travels only about cΔt, which sets the range R of the force.
---
$ mc^2 \approx \frac{\hbar c}{R} = \frac{197\,\text{MeV fm}}{1.4\,\text{fm}} \approx 140\,\text{MeV}
The nuclear force reaches about 1.4 fm (1 fm is 10⁻¹⁵ m), and ħc ≈ 197 MeV·fm. The carrier should weigh roughly 270 electron masses.
:::

The new particle, at 106 MeV, was close enough. But in 1947 an experiment in Rome showed it passed through nuclei almost without interacting, which a carrier of the nuclear force could not do. It was a heavy copy of the electron, now called the **muon**. "Who ordered that?" asked I. I. Rabi.

Muons also gave an early test of relativity. They are made about 15 km up and live only 2.2 microseconds at rest, long enough to travel about 660 m. They reach the ground in large numbers because, to us, their clocks run slow.

Push the speed towards c and watch γ grow; a muon at 0.999c lives about 22 times longer in our frame.
:::

## Yukawa's particle, and then too many

::: history 1947 · Bristol, England
Cecil Powell's group exposed photographic emulsions on mountains in the Pyrenees and Bolivia. In the developed plates César Lattes, Giuseppe Occhialini and Powell found a particle of about 140 MeV that stopped and decayed into a muon. This was Yukawa's carrier, the **pion**, and it did interact strongly with nuclei.
:::

The same year, cloud chambers in Manchester showed forked tracks from neutral and charged particles heavier than the pion. These were the first **kaons** and other "strange" particles, so called because they were produced copiously, as if by the strong force, yet lived about 10⁻¹⁰ s, trillions of times longer than a strong decay takes.

Cosmic rays were too rare and unpredictable for systematic work, so physicists built accelerators. The Cosmotron at Brookhaven reached about 3 GeV in 1953; the Bevatron at Berkeley reached about 6 GeV in 1954 and produced the antiproton in 1955. Donald Glaser's **bubble chamber** (1952), a tank of superheated liquid in which charged particles leave strings of bubbles, made tracks easy to photograph by the million. Luis Alvarez's group at Berkeley built large liquid-hydrogen chambers and found dozens of **resonances**, particles that live about 10⁻²³ s and appear only as bumps in the energy of their decay products.

By the early 1960s the list was past a hundred. Enrico Fermi reportedly told a student, "If I could remember the names of all these particles, I'd have been a botanist."

::: note
Each new particle was found by measuring tracks: curvature gives charge and momentum, length and decay give lifetime. The sheer number of strongly interacting particles was the clue that they could not all be elementary.
:::
