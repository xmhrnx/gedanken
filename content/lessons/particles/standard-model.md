By the late 1970s quarks, leptons and the force carriers had been fitted into one theory, the **Standard Model**. It describes every particle ever seen in an accelerator and every experiment there to date, using seventeen kinds of fundamental particle and a short list of rules.

## The seventeen particles

Matter is made of **fermions**, particles of spin ½ that obey the exclusion principle. They come in two kinds: **quarks**, which feel the strong force, and **leptons**, which do not.

| | Generation 1 | Generation 2 | Generation 3 | Charge |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Quarks | up | charm | top | +⅔ |
| | down | strange | bottom | −⅓ |
| Leptons | electron | muon | tau | −1 |
| | electron neutrino | muon neutrino | tau neutrino | 0 |

Forces are carried by four kinds of boson: the **photon** (electromagnetism), the **gluon** (strong force, eight colour varieties counted as one), and the **W** and **Z** (weak force, with W⁺ and W⁻ counted as one). The seventeenth particle is the **Higgs boson**, which is not a force carrier in the usual sense and gets its own lesson. Twelve fermions, four carriers and the Higgs make seventeen. Each fermion also has an antiparticle, usually not counted separately.

The three columns are **generations**. Each repeats the first with the same charges but heavier masses: the muon is about 207 times the electron's mass, the tau about 3500 times, and the top quark weighs about as much as a gold atom. Everything around us is made of the first generation alone. The heavier ones decay into it within a tiny fraction of a second, and no one knows why there are three.

## The rules

The forces come from symmetries. Each force corresponds to a mathematical symmetry group (written SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1) for strong, weak and electromagnetic), and requiring the equations to keep that symmetry at every point in space forces the carrier particles to exist and fixes how they couple. Within those rules, every process conserves electric charge, energy and momentum, the number of quarks minus antiquarks (baryon number), and the number of leptons minus antileptons (lepton number).

Neutron decay shows the rules at work. At the quark level a down quark turns into an up quark by emitting a W⁻, which then becomes an electron and an antineutrino.

::: steps Checking neutron decay at the quark level
$ d \to u + W^-
Charge: −⅓ on the left, ⅔ − 1 = −⅓ on the right. The quark flavour changes, which only the W can do.
---
$ W^- \to e^- + \bar{\nu}_e
Charge −1 on both sides. Lepton number: 0 on the left, +1 for the electron and −1 for the antineutrino on the right, so 0.
---
$ n\,(udd) \to p\,(uud) + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e
Put the two together and the other two quarks are spectators. The W lives about 10⁻²⁵ s, and it is "virtual" here, since the neutron has far less than the 80 GeV needed to make a real one.
---
$ m_n c^2 - m_p c^2 \approx 1.29\,\text{MeV} > m_e c^2 = 0.511\,\text{MeV}
The decay is allowed because the neutron outweighs the proton plus the electron; the remaining 0.78 MeV goes into motion.
:::

The model has about nineteen free parameters, numbers such as the masses and the strengths of the forces, which must be measured rather than predicted. Once they are measured, everything else follows, and it has passed every accelerator test for half a century. The last missing pieces arrived in sequence: the top quark at Fermilab in 1995, the tau neutrino in 2000, and the Higgs boson at CERN in 2012.

::: history 1989 · CERN and SLAC
How many generations are there? Any neutrino lighter than half the Z mass gives the Z another way to decay, making it shorter-lived and its resonance peak broader. The first measurements of the Z's width at LEP and SLAC, in late 1989, already pointed to three; the combined LEP result is 2.98 ± 0.03 light neutrino types: exactly three generations, unless a fourth has a very heavy neutrino.
:::

## What it leaves out

- **Gravity.** The Standard Model has no graviton, and general relativity has not been made into a consistent quantum theory. At accelerator energies gravity is negligible, but near the Big Bang or inside a black hole it is not.
- **Dark matter.** Galaxies and clusters hold about five times more matter than the ordinary kind, and none of the seventeen particles can be it.
- **Neutrino masses.** In the original model neutrinos are massless. In 1998 Super-Kamiokande in Japan found neutrinos changing type in flight, which requires mass, and the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory in Canada confirmed it in 2001. The masses are under about 1 eV, at least half a million times lighter than the electron, and how they arise is unknown.
- **Dark energy**, which accelerates the expansion of the universe, and the excess of matter over antimatter, which the model's known asymmetries are far too small to explain.

::: note
Seventeen particles and three forces, organised by symmetry, account for every particle-physics experiment so far. The theory is still incomplete: it omits gravity, cannot explain dark matter, and had to be amended for neutrino mass.
:::
