By the late 1970s the pieces of the previous lessons had been fitted together into a single theory. The quarks, the electron and its relatives, and the carriers of the strong, weak and electromagnetic forces all found a place in what physicists, with unusual modesty, call the **Standard Model**. It describes every particle ever seen in an accelerator and the outcome of every experiment done there, using seventeen kinds of fundamental particle and a short set of rules. This lesson lays out what those particles are, where the rules come from, and how they work in a real process, and then turns to what the model leaves out, because a theory that has passed every test for fifty years is also known to be incomplete.

## What is everything made of?

The particles of matter are **fermions**, particles with spin ½ that obey the Pauli exclusion principle, the rule that no two identical fermions can share the same state. That rule is what gives matter its bulk: it keeps electrons in atoms from all piling into the lowest orbit, and so it keeps atoms from collapsing. Fermions come in two kinds. **Quarks** feel the strong force and are always bound inside hadrons. **Leptons**, from the Greek for "light" in the sense of not heavy, do not feel the strong force at all.

| Quarks | up | charm | top | +⅔ |

| Leptons | electron | muon | tau | −1 |

The forces are carried by bosons, particles with whole-number spin. There are four kinds: the **photon**, which carries electromagnetism; the **gluon**, which carries the strong force and comes in eight colour varieties, here counted as one; and the **W** and **Z**, which carry the weak force, with the W⁺ and W⁻ counted as one. The seventeenth particle is the **Higgs boson**, which is not a force carrier in the usual sense and has the next lesson to itself. Twelve fermions, four carriers and the Higgs make seventeen. Each fermion also has an antiparticle with the opposite charge, and these are usually not counted separately.

The columns of the table are called **generations**, and they are one of the model's strangest features. Each generation repeats the first exactly, with the same charges and the same response to every force, but with heavier masses. The muon behaves in every way like an electron about 207 times heavier; the tau like one about 3,500 times heavier. The top quark, the heaviest particle known, weighs about as much as an entire atom of gold. Everything around us, from atoms to stars, is made of the first generation alone, because the heavier particles decay into lighter ones within a tiny fraction of a second. Why nature repeats itself, and why exactly three times, is unknown.

## Where do the forces come from?

The Standard Model does not simply list the forces; it derives them from **symmetries**. A symmetry, in this sense, is a change you can make to the description of a system that leaves the physics unaltered. The model's forces come from requiring the equations to keep certain symmetries not just overall but separately at every point in space and time. That is a demanding condition, and it can be met only by adding new fields that carry the information about the change from place to place. Those fields are the force carriers. The symmetry fixes how many carriers there are and exactly how they couple to matter.

The symmetries are written SU(3) × SU(2) × U(1). SU(3) is the symmetry of the three colours, and it produces the eight gluons of the strong force. SU(2) × U(1) together produce the electroweak force, with four carriers. The Higgs field then mixes these four, leaving three heavy ones, the W⁺, W⁻ and Z, and one massless combination, the photon. So electromagnetism, the most familiar force, is not one of the starting symmetries but the part of the electroweak force that survives the Higgs mechanism unbroken.

Within those rules, every process ever observed conserves certain quantities: electric charge, energy and momentum, **baryon number**, which counts quarks minus antiquarks (each quark counting one third, so that a proton or neutron has baryon number 1), and **lepton number**, which counts leptons minus antileptons. The colour of every observable particle adds up to neutral. Every reaction that obeys these rules can happen, and none that breaks them has been seen.

## The rules at work: neutron decay

Neutron decay is a good test case because it involves almost every part of the model. A free neutron lives about fifteen minutes and then decays into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino. At the level of quarks, one down quark turns into an up quark by emitting a W⁻, and the W⁻ turns into an electron and an antineutrino.

::: steps Checking neutron decay at the quark level
$ d \to u + W^-
A down quark becomes an up quark and emits a W⁻. Check the charge: −1/3 on the left, and +2/3 − 1 = −1/3 on the right, so it balances. The quark's type, or **flavour**, has changed, and only the W can do that; neither photons nor gluons change one kind of quark into another.
---
$ W^- \to e^- + \bar{\nu}_e
The W⁻ then turns into an electron and an electron antineutrino. Charge is −1 on both sides. Lepton number is zero on the left, and on the right the electron counts +1 and the antineutrino −1, so it is zero there too. The rule that leptons are made only in matched pairs like this is exactly what lepton conservation means.
---
$ n\,(udd) \to p\,(uud) + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e
Put the two steps together. The other two quarks in the neutron, one up and one down, take no part; they are spectators. Note that the W here is "virtual": a real W needs 80 GeV of energy to make, and the neutron has nowhere near that. It is a term in the calculation, and it acts only over the less than 10⁻²⁵ seconds and the roughly 10⁻¹⁸ metres that the weak force allows.
---
$ m_n c^2 - m_p c^2 \approx 1.29\,\text{MeV}
Every conservation rule is satisfied, but energy has to balance too. The neutron's rest energy exceeds the proton's by about 1.29 MeV, so there is energy available for the decay.
---
$ 1.29\,\text{MeV} > m_e c^2 = 0.511\,\text{MeV}
That available energy must at least pay for the electron's rest energy, 0.511 MeV; the antineutrino's mass is negligible. It does, so the decay is allowed, and the remaining 0.78 MeV is shared out as kinetic energy among the three products. The same arithmetic shows why a proton cannot decay into a neutron on its own: it is lighter, so the energy is not there.
:::

The model contains about nineteen **free parameters**, numbers that it cannot predict and that must be measured: the masses of the quarks and charged leptons, the strengths of the three forces, the mass of the Higgs, and a few numbers describing how quarks of different generations mix. Once those are measured, every other prediction follows, and the predictions have passed every accelerator test for half a century. The last missing pieces arrived one after another, each where it was expected: the top quark at Fermilab in 1995, the tau neutrino, also at Fermilab, in 2000, and the Higgs boson at CERN in 2012.

::: history 1989 · CERN and SLAC
How many generations are there? The question seemed unanswerable until the Z could be made in large numbers. A Z can decay into any pair of particles light enough, including a neutrino and its antineutrino of each type. Each extra way to decay shortens the Z's life, and by the uncertainty principle a shorter life means a wider spread of masses, so the Z's peak in a graph of collision rate against energy gets broader. Counting the width of the peak therefore counts the neutrinos. The first measurements, from the new LEP collider at CERN and the SLC collider at SLAC in late 1989, already pointed clearly to three. The final combined LEP result is 2.984 ± 0.008 types of light neutrino. Unless a fourth generation has a neutrino heavier than half the Z's mass, about 45 GeV, there are exactly three.
:::

## What does the model leave out?

The most obvious omission is gravity. The Standard Model has no graviton, and nobody has yet turned general relativity into a consistent quantum theory. At accelerator energies gravity between particles is so weak that leaving it out costs nothing, but in the first instants after the Big Bang or at the centre of a black hole it cannot be ignored, and there the model has nothing to say.

The second is **dark matter**. Measurements of how galaxies rotate and how clusters of galaxies bend light show that they contain about five times as much matter as all the ordinary atoms, matter that gives off no light. None of the seventeen particles can account for it. The total amount of matter built from quarks is measured independently, from the light elements made in the Big Bang, and it is far too small; any charged particle would absorb and give off light; and neutrinos move too fast, being so light, to clump into galaxies.

The third is the mass of neutrinos. In the original model neutrinos have no mass at all. In 1998 the Super-Kamiokande detector in Japan found that neutrinos change from one type to another as they travel, and in 2001 the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory in Canada confirmed it with neutrinos from the Sun. Such changes can only happen if the different types have different masses, so at least some neutrinos must have mass. The masses are tiny, below about half an electronvolt, roughly a million times lighter than the electron. The model can be extended to include them, but it does not say how they arise or why they are so small.

Finally, there is **dark energy**, which is causing the expansion of the universe to speed up and which the model does not explain, and the question of why the universe contains matter and almost no antimatter. The model does treat matter and antimatter very slightly differently, but far too slightly to account for what we see.

::: note
Seventeen particles and three forces, with the forces arising from symmetries, account for every particle-physics experiment so far. The theory is still incomplete: it leaves out gravity, has no candidate for dark matter, and had to be extended to give neutrinos mass.
:::
