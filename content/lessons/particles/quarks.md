The hundreds of particles found in the 1950s were mostly **hadrons**, particles that feel the strong nuclear force. When so many objects share properties, they are usually built from fewer parts. Chemistry's periodic table had pointed to electrons and nuclei; the hadron table pointed to quarks.

## A periodic table for hadrons

In 1961 Murray Gell-Mann, and independently Yuval Ne'eman, sorted the hadrons into families of 8 and 10 by charge and **strangeness**, a conserved number that explained why strange particles were made in pairs and decayed slowly. Gell-Mann called the scheme the Eightfold Way. One family of ten had a gap at the bottom: a particle with charge −1, strangeness −3 and a mass of about 1680 MeV. In 1964 a bubble chamber at Brookhaven found it, the **omega-minus** (Ω⁻), at 1672 MeV.

::: history 1964 · Caltech and CERN
Gell-Mann and George Zweig, working separately, saw that the families could be built from three smaller constituents and their antiparticles. Gell-Mann called them **quarks**, from a line in *Finnegans Wake*; Zweig called them aces. Gell-Mann was careful to call them possibly "mathematical"; Zweig argued they were real, and had trouble getting his paper published.
:::

The three quarks were up (u), down (d) and strange (s). Their oddest feature was fractional charge, which no experiment had ever seen.

The Ω⁻ raised a problem. Its three identical s quarks sit in the same state, which the Pauli exclusion principle forbids for particles of spin ½. In 1964–65 Oscar Greenberg, and Moo-Young Han with Yoichiro Nambu, proposed that each quark carries a new kind of charge with three values, later called **colour** (red, green, blue, with no connection to real colour). The three s quarks differ in colour, so the exclusion principle is satisfied.

## Seeing inside the proton

::: history 1968 · Stanford, California
At SLAC a 3 km linear accelerator fired electrons of up to about 20 GeV at protons. A team led by Jerome Friedman, Henry Kendall and Richard Taylor found far more electrons scattered at large angles, with large energy loss, than a smooth, soft proton would allow. They shared the 1990 Nobel Prize.
:::

It was Rutherford's experiment again, a lifetime later. Large-angle scattering meant the proton's charge was concentrated in small, hard points. James Bjorken had predicted a pattern, now called scaling, that such point-like constituents would produce, and the data followed it. Richard Feynman called the points **partons**; by the early 1970s further experiments showed they had spin ½ and the fractional charges of quarks. The rest of the proton's momentum, about half, was carried by something neutral: the gluons that hold quarks together.

## Why no one sees a free quark

Searches for fractional charge in cosmic rays, accelerators and even oil drops found nothing. Quarks are **confined**. The force between two quarks does not fall off with distance as electric force does; beyond about 1 fm it stays roughly constant, at about 1 GeV per femtometre, as if they were joined by a string. Pulling them apart stores more and more energy, and once there is enough to create a new quark–antiquark pair (a pion needs only 0.14 GeV), the string breaks into two hadrons instead of freeing a quark. High-energy collisions therefore produce narrow sprays of hadrons, called **jets**, rather than single quarks.

At short distances the reverse happens: the force weakens, so quarks deep inside a proton behave almost as free particles. This **asymptotic freedom**, found in 1973 by David Gross, Frank Wilczek and David Politzer, explained why the SLAC electrons saw nearly free partons. The theory of colour and gluons became **quantum chromodynamics** (QCD).

Three more quarks followed: charm in 1974, bottom in 1977 and top in 1995, making six.

::: note
Hadrons are not elementary. Their families, charges and the hard scattering inside protons all point to quarks with fractional charge, held together so strongly that they are never seen alone.
:::
