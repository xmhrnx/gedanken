By the early 1960s physicists had found more than a hundred particles, and most of them were **hadrons**, particles that feel the strong nuclear force: protons, neutrons, pions, kaons and their many short-lived relatives. A hundred kinds of fundamental object is not much of a simplification. But physics had been in this position before. In the nineteenth century chemists had dozens of elements whose properties repeated in a pattern, and the periodic table that organised them turned out to reflect something deeper: atoms built from electrons, protons and neutrons. This lesson follows the same path one level down. First came a pattern among the hadrons, then a guess at the parts that would explain it, then an experiment that looked inside the proton and saw them, and finally an explanation of why those parts are never seen on their own.

## A pattern among the hadrons

The first step was to find the right properties to sort by. Mass alone gave no pattern, but there were conserved quantities, numbers that stay the same in every reaction. Electric charge was one. In 1953 Murray Gell-Mann, and independently Kazuhiko Nishijima, had introduced another to explain the strange particles of the previous lesson. They gave each hadron a whole number called **strangeness** and proposed that the strong force always conserves it, while the weak force does not. That explained both halves of the puzzle. Strange particles are always produced in pairs, one with strangeness +1 and one with −1, so that the total stays zero, which the strong force can do quickly and often. But once a strange particle is alone, it can only decay by changing its strangeness, which only the slow weak force can do, so it lives about 10⁻¹⁰ seconds instead of 10⁻²³.

In 1961 Gell-Mann, and independently Yuval Ne'eman, plotted the hadrons by charge and strangeness and found that particles of similar mass and the same spin fell into neat geometric families of 8 and 10. The mathematics behind the families came from a symmetry group called SU(3), and Gell-Mann, borrowing a phrase from Buddhism, called the scheme the Eightfold Way.

The decisive test was a gap. One family of ten had nine known members, arranged in a triangle in which each row down had one more unit of negative strangeness and a mass about 150 MeV higher. The missing tenth would sit alone at the bottom point. It had to have charge −1 and strangeness −3, and from the spacing of the rows its mass should be about 1680 MeV. Gell-Mann announced the prediction at a conference in 1962. In 1964 a bubble chamber at Brookhaven photographed the particle, the **omega-minus** (Ω⁻), at 1672 MeV. A scheme that predicts a new particle's charge, strangeness and mass before it is seen is no longer just a filing system.

::: history 1964 · Caltech and CERN
Why should the families have these shapes? Gell-Mann at Caltech and George Zweig, a young physicist visiting CERN, independently noticed that they could all be built from three smaller constituents and their antiparticles. Gell-Mann called them **quarks**, a nonsense word he had in mind and then found in a line of James Joyce's *Finnegans Wake*, "Three quarks for Muster Mark!"; Zweig called them aces. The two men took different attitudes. Gell-Mann was careful to say the quarks might be only "mathematical", a convenient bookkeeping device, since no one had seen anything like them. Zweig argued they were physically real, and he had trouble getting his paper published at all.
:::

The three quarks were named up (u), down (d) and strange (s). Their oddest feature, and the main reason for doubt, was that their electric charges had to be fractions of the proton's charge. Every charge ever measured was a whole multiple of the electron's, and a third of one would have been unmistakable.

$ q_u = +\tfrac{2}{3}, \quad q_d = -\tfrac{1}{3}, \quad q_s = -\tfrac{1}{3}
These are the quark charges in units of the proton charge e. The rules of the model are that a **baryon** (the proton, the neutron and their heavier relatives) is made of three quarks, and a **meson** (pions, kaons and their relatives) is made of a quark and an antiquark. The strange quark carries strangeness −1, which is how strangeness enters the scheme.

$ p = uud: \quad \tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{2}{3} - \tfrac{1}{3} = +1
The charge of a hadron is just the sum of its quarks' charges. Two up quarks and one down quark give exactly +1, the proton's charge. The fractions combine into a whole number, which is why fractional charges had never been seen in any known particle.
---
$ n = udd: \quad \tfrac{2}{3} - \tfrac{1}{3} - \tfrac{1}{3} = 0
Replace one up quark with a down and the charge drops by one unit, to zero: the neutron. The proton and neutron, so similar in mass, differ only by this one swap, which also hints at what beta decay does at the deepest level.
---
$ \pi^+ = u\bar{d}: \quad \tfrac{2}{3} + \tfrac{1}{3} = +1
A meson pairs a quark with an antiquark, and an antiquark has the opposite charge of its quark. So the anti-down quark, written d̄, contributes +1/3, and with an up quark it makes the positive pion.
---
$ \Omega^- = sss: \quad -\tfrac{1}{3} \times 3 = -1
Three strange quarks give charge −1 and strangeness −3, exactly the properties predicted for the bottom of the family of ten. The Ω⁻ is the one member built from three quarks of a single kind, which is why it sat alone at the corner of the pattern.
:::

The Ω⁻ also raised a serious problem. Quarks have spin ½, the same as electrons, and such particles obey the **Pauli exclusion principle**: no two identical ones can be in exactly the same state. The Ω⁻ has spin 3/2, which means all three of its quark spins point the same way, and in the lowest-energy arrangement all three sit in the same state of motion. Three identical quarks in the same state is exactly what the exclusion principle forbids. In 1964 and 1965 Oscar Greenberg, and Moo-Young Han with Yoichiro Nambu, proposed a way out: each quark carries a new kind of charge with three possible values, later named **colour**, with the values called red, green and blue. The names have nothing to do with real colour; they are labels for three possibilities that combine to neutral, as red, green and blue light combine to white. If the three strange quarks in the Ω⁻ all have different colours, they are no longer identical, and the exclusion principle is satisfied. At the time this looked like an ad hoc patch. It turned out to be the key to the whole strong force.

## Looking inside the proton

::: history 1968 · Stanford, California
At the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center (SLAC), a straight accelerator 3 kilometres long fired electrons with energies up to about 20 GeV (billion electronvolts) at a target of liquid hydrogen, whose nuclei are single protons. Electrons are good probes because they feel no strong force; they interact only electrically, so they report on where the proton's charge is. A team led by Jerome Friedman and Henry Kendall of MIT and Richard Taylor of SLAC measured how many electrons bounced off at large angles while losing a large part of their energy. They found far more than a smooth, spread-out proton would allow. The three shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1990.
:::

This was Rutherford's experiment again, a little over half a century later. In 1911 Rutherford had found alpha particles bouncing back from gold foil and concluded that the atom's positive charge was concentrated in a tiny nucleus, because only a small, dense target can deflect a fast projectile sharply. The same logic applied now. If the proton's charge were spread evenly through it, a fast electron would pass through with only gentle nudges. Hard, large-angle scattering meant the charge was concentrated in small, hard points.

The theorist James Bjorken had predicted a particular pattern, now called **scaling**, that such point-like constituents would produce: the scattering would depend on a single combination of the energy lost and the momentum transferred, rather than on each separately. The data followed it. Richard Feynman, without committing to what the points were, called them **partons**, "parts" of the proton. Over the next few years, experiments with electrons and with neutrinos showed that the partons had spin ½ and the fractional charges of quarks. The quarks were real.

The same experiments carried one more surprise. Adding up the momentum carried by the charged partons gave only about half of the proton's total. The rest had to be carried by something with no electric charge, which the electrons could not see. That something turned out to be the **gluons**, the carriers of the force that holds quarks together.

## Why no one sees a free quark

If quarks are real, it should be possible to knock one out of a proton and see it on its own, with its telltale fractional charge. Physicists searched in cosmic rays, in accelerator collisions, in seawater and meteorites, and even with improved versions of Millikan's oil-drop experiment. They found nothing.

The explanation is that quarks are **confined**. The force between two quarks behaves quite unlike the electric force. The electric force between two charges weakens as they move apart, so a little energy is enough to separate them completely. The force between quarks does not fall off. Beyond about a femtometre (10⁻¹⁵ m) it stays roughly constant, at a strength corresponding to about 1 GeV of energy for every femtometre of separation. It acts somewhat like a string joining them, with one important difference from an ordinary string: it never goes slack, and the tension is the same however far it is stretched.

That means the energy stored between the quarks grows steadily as they are pulled apart. Long before they are separated, there is enough energy to create a new quark and antiquark out of it, by E = mc²; the lightest pair, which forms a pion, needs only about 0.14 GeV. When that happens, the string breaks, and the new quark and antiquark attach to the two ends. Instead of one free quark, you have two hadrons. In high-energy collisions this happens again and again, and a struck quark shows up not as a single particle but as a narrow spray of hadrons all moving in roughly its direction, called a **jet**.

At short distances the reverse happens: the force between quarks becomes weaker, so quarks close together inside a proton behave almost as if they were free. This property, **asymptotic freedom**, was discovered in 1973 by David Gross and Frank Wilczek at Princeton and, independently, by David Politzer at Harvard. It explained why the SLAC electrons, which struck quarks hard and fast, saw them as nearly free particles, even though at larger distances the same quarks are bound for ever. Colour and gluons together became a full theory of the strong force, **quantum chromodynamics** (QCD), the name taken from the Greek word for colour.

The list of quarks did not stop at three. A fourth, charm, revealed itself in 1974, when two laboratories announced the same new particle in the same week. The fifth, bottom, appeared in 1977, and the sixth and heaviest, top, was found at Fermilab in 1995, making six.

::: note
Hadrons are not elementary. Their families, their charges and the hard scattering of electrons inside protons all point to quarks with fractional charges, bound together by gluons. The force between quarks does not weaken with distance, so pulling them apart makes new quarks instead of freeing old ones, and no quark is ever seen alone.
:::
