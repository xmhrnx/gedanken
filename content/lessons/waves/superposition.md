Two stones dropped in a pond send out two sets of ripples, and where the ripples cross they pass straight through each other and continue unchanged. While they overlap, though, the water does something new: in some places it heaves twice as high, in others it lies almost flat. This is **superposition**, and it is the single most characteristic thing waves do.

## Waves add

For most waves of modest amplitude, the rule is simple:

::: equation The principle of superposition
y = y_1 + y_2
:::

When two waves overlap, the displacement at each point is the sum of the displacements each wave would cause alone. Afterwards each wave carries on as if the other had never been there. The rule holds for sound, light, ripples and waves on strings, as long as the medium responds in proportion to the push (a *linear* medium). Very violent waves, such as breaking surf or shock waves, break the rule.

## Constructive and destructive interference

Take two waves of the same frequency and amplitude A. If their crests arrive together, *in phase*, they add to a wave of amplitude 2A: **constructive interference**. If the crests of one arrive with the troughs of the other, half a cycle *out of phase*, they cancel: **destructive interference**. In between, the result has an amplitude somewhere between 0 and 2A.

Whether two waves arrive in or out of phase often depends on how far each has travelled. If two sources vibrate in step, then at a point where the distances to them differ by a whole number of wavelengths the crests coincide; where they differ by a half-number of wavelengths, crest meets trough.

::: equation Conditions for two sources in step (n = 0, 1, 2, …)
\text{constructive: } \Delta &= n\lambda \\ \text{destructive: } \Delta &= \left(n + \tfrac{1}{2}\right)\lambda
:::

Here Δ is the **path difference**, the difference between the two distances. Cancellation does not destroy energy: it moves it. The dark places are matched by bright places with four times the energy of a single wave, since energy goes as amplitude squared.

::: note
Overlapping waves add point by point. Where the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths they reinforce; where it is a half-number they cancel. A pattern of bright and dark bands is the signature of a wave.
:::

## Young's double slit

The most famous use of this is with light. Shine light of one colour on a barrier with two narrow parallel slits a distance d apart, and look at a screen a distance L beyond. Each slit acts as a source, and the two are in step because the same light feeds both. On the screen you see not two bright lines but a row of evenly spaced bright and dark bands, called **fringes**.

::: model interference
Change the separation of the two sources and watch the fringes. Closer sources spread the bright bands farther apart.
:::

::: steps The spacing of the fringes
$ \Delta = d\sin\theta
For a point on the screen at angle θ from the centre line, the paths from the two slits are nearly parallel when L is much larger than d. The farther slit's path is longer by d sin θ.
---
$ d\sin\theta = n\lambda
Bright fringes appear where the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths.
---
$ \sin\theta \approx \tan\theta = \frac{y}{L}
For small angles, sin θ and tan θ are nearly equal, and tan θ is the fringe's height y on the screen divided by the distance L.
---
$ y_n = \frac{n\lambda L}{d}
Combine the two. The n-th bright fringe sits at this height.
---
$ \Delta y = \frac{\lambda L}{d}
Neighbouring bright fringes are separated by this spacing, the same all the way across the central part of the pattern.
:::

For red light of wavelength 650 nm, slits 0.25 mm apart and a screen 1 m away, the fringes are 2.6 mm apart: easy to see. Run the formula backwards and measuring a spacing with a ruler gives the wavelength of light, far too small to measure directly.

::: history 1801–1807 · London
Newton's authority had made light a stream of particles for a century. Thomas Young, a physician who also helped decipher the Rosetta Stone, argued from interference that light is a wave, and in 1803 he showed the Royal Society fringes made by splitting a narrow beam of sunlight with the edge of a thin card; his lectures of 1807 describe the two-slit version. From the fringes he estimated the wavelengths of different colours, correctly, at under a thousandth of a millimetre. British critics, notably Henry Brougham, attacked him savagely in print, and the wave theory won acceptance only after Augustin Fresnel's mathematical treatment in France in the late 1810s.
:::

## Interference in everyday life

Noise-cancelling headphones record incoming sound and play its mirror image, so the two cancel at your ear. The colours on a soap bubble or an oil film come from light reflecting off the film's front and back surfaces: the extra path through the film reinforces some wavelengths and cancels others, depending on the thickness. Two tuning forks of slightly different frequency produce **beats**, a rise and fall in loudness as they drift in and out of step, at a rate equal to the difference in their frequencies; musicians use beats to tune instruments.

Interference needs sources whose phase relation stays fixed. Two light bulbs flicker in phase randomly billions of times a second, so their fringes wash out; that is why Young fed both slits from one source.
