Drop a stone in a pond and rings spread outward. The water does not travel with them: a floating leaf bobs up and down as a ring passes, then settles where it was. A **wave** is a disturbance that travels through a medium, carrying energy from place to place without carrying the medium along.

## Two kinds of wave

In a **transverse** wave the medium moves at right angles to the direction the wave travels. Flick one end of a rope tied to a wall and a hump runs along it while each bit of rope moves only up and down. In a **longitudinal** wave the medium moves back and forth along the direction of travel. Push and pull one end of a stretched Slinky and bands of compression and stretching run along it. Sound in air is longitudinal. Water waves are a mixture: water near the surface moves in roughly circular paths.

## Describing a wave

Picture a steady sine-shaped wave moving along a rope. A snapshot at one instant shows a repeating shape in space; watching a single point shows a repeating motion in time.

- The **amplitude** A is the greatest displacement from the rest position.
- The **wavelength** λ (lambda) is the distance between neighbouring crests, the length after which the shape repeats.
- The **period** T is the time for one point to go through one full cycle, and the **frequency** f = 1/T is the number of cycles per second, in hertz.

Each point on the rope performs simple harmonic motion, as in the previous lessons. The wave is that motion passed along, each point slightly behind the one before.

::: model wave
Change the wavelength and the frequency and watch the speed, which is always their product.
:::

## Speed, frequency and wavelength

::: steps The wave equation v = fλ
$ \Delta x = \lambda \quad\text{in}\quad \Delta t = T
Watch one crest. In one period, the point under it goes through one full cycle and the next crest arrives where the first one was, so the crest has moved forward exactly one wavelength.
---
$ v = \frac{\lambda}{T}
Speed is distance over time.
---
$ v = f\lambda
Use f = 1/T.
:::

::: note
A wave carries energy, not matter. Its speed, frequency and wavelength are tied by v = fλ, and the speed is set by the medium, not by the source.
:::

The source fixes the frequency: a tuning fork vibrating 440 times a second sends out 440 compressions per second. The medium fixes the speed: sound in air at 20 °C travels at 343 m/s, however loud or high-pitched. The wavelength then follows: 343/440 ≈ 0.78 m. A note an octave higher, at 880 Hz, has half the wavelength.

The same relation covers every kind of wave. Light travels at 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s, so green light of wavelength 530 nm has a frequency of about 5.7 × 10¹⁴ Hz, and an FM radio station at 100 MHz broadcasts waves 3 m long.

## What sets the speed

The speed depends on the medium's stiffness, which pulls displaced parts back, and its inertia, which resists their motion. For a stretched string with tension F and mass per unit length μ:

::: equation Wave speed on a string
v = \sqrt{\frac{F}{\mu}}
:::

Tighten the string and waves go faster; use a heavier string and they go slower. Sound follows the same pattern, travelling about 1,480 m/s in water and around 5,000 m/s or more in steel, which are far stiffer than air for their density.

## A wave as a formula

The displacement y of a sine wave moving in the +x direction can be written

::: equation A travelling sine wave
y(x, t) = A\sin\left(\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}x - 2\pi f t\right)
:::

At fixed t this is a sine curve in x repeating every λ; at fixed x, a sine oscillation in t repeating every 1/f. The minus sign makes the pattern move toward +x: as t grows, the same phase is found at larger x.

::: history 1747 · Berlin
Jean le Rond d'Alembert wrote down the equation of motion for a vibrating string and showed that its general solution is any shape travelling to the right at speed v plus any shape travelling to the left: y = f(x − vt) + g(x + vt). A wave, in other words, need not be a sine at all; any pulse keeps its shape as it moves along an ideal string. Euler, Daniel Bernoulli and d'Alembert then argued for decades over whether every such shape could be built from sines, a question settled only in the next century.
:::

Real waves lose amplitude as they spread out and as friction turns their energy into heat, and in many media the speed depends a little on frequency. The ideal picture is still the starting point for sound, light, earthquakes and, much later in this course, the matter waves of quantum mechanics.
