Rub a balloon on your hair and it will cling to a wall. The ancient Greeks knew a version of this: a piece of amber rubbed with cloth attracts bits of straw and dust. The Greek word for amber, *ēlektron*, gave electricity its name. For about two thousand years this was a curiosity, a parlour trick with no theory behind it. Turning it into physics took three discoveries, each of which overturned a natural first guess. There turned out to be two kinds of electric charge, not one. Charge turned out never to be created or destroyed, only moved. And charge turned out to come in fixed, indivisible lumps rather than any amount you like. This lesson follows each of those in turn.

## How many kinds of charge are there?

Start with an experiment anyone could do in the eighteenth century. Rub two glass rods with silk and hang one from a thread. Bring the other near it, and the hanging rod swings away: the two repel. Now rub two rods of amber, or of hard resin, with fur, and do the same. They also repel each other. But bring a rubbed glass rod near a rubbed amber rod and they swing *together*.

If there were only one kind of electrification, every rubbed object would behave the same way toward every other, and this result would be impossible. So there must be two kinds, and the rule is that like kinds repel while unlike kinds attract. Every charged object ever tested falls into one class or the other: it is either repelled by rubbed glass or repelled by rubbed amber, never both.

::: history 1733 · Paris
Charles du Fay was the superintendent of the royal gardens in Paris and spent his spare hours on electricity. He noticed that a piece of gold leaf charged by touching it to rubbed glass would be pushed away by the glass, but pulled toward rubbed resin. Testing many materials, he found they all sorted into the same two groups. He called the two kinds "vitreous" electricity, after the Latin for glass, and "resinous" electricity, after amber and resin, and he pictured them as two separate invisible fluids that could be added to ordinary matter.
:::

Du Fay's two-fluid picture explained attraction and repulsion, but it left a question open. When glass is rubbed with silk, what happens to the silk?

::: history 1747 · Philadelphia
Benjamin Franklin, a printer turned experimenter, found that when glass and silk are rubbed together, both become charged, and with opposite kinds. To him that suggested a different picture: a single electric fluid that every object normally holds in a balanced amount. Rubbing does not create electricity; it moves fluid from one body to the other. The body with too much he called **positive**, and the body with too little **negative**. He had to decide which was which, and he chose to call rubbed glass positive.

The choice was arbitrary, and it turned out to be unlucky. More than a century later, the particles that actually move when an object is charged, and that carry current through metal wires, were identified as electrons, and under Franklin's convention their charge is negative. That is why, in a circuit, the conventional direction of current, from positive to negative, is opposite to the direction in which electrons really flow.
:::

Franklin's names have stuck. More important, so has his central insight: charging an object means moving charge, not making it.

## Can charge be created or destroyed?

When glass is rubbed with silk, the glass ends up positively charged and the silk negatively charged, and careful measurement shows the two amounts are exactly equal. The total charge, counting positive as plus and negative as minus, is zero before the rubbing and zero after. This is the law of **conservation of charge**: the total charge of an isolated system never changes.

It has been tested far beyond rubbed rods, in situations where you might expect it to fail. In particle physics, a high-energy gamma ray, a particle of light with no charge, can turn into an electron and its antiparticle, the positron, when it passes close to an atomic nucleus. Matter appears where there was none. But the electron carries charge −e and the positron +e, which add to zero, exactly the charge of the gamma ray they came from. In every process ever observed, including the decays of unstable particles and the reactions inside stars, charge is created only in equal and opposite amounts, and so the total is never changed.

## What is actually moving?

Today we know what Franklin's fluid really is. Ordinary matter is made of atoms. Each atom has a small, heavy nucleus at its centre containing **protons**, each with charge +e, along with uncharged neutrons. Around the nucleus are **electrons**, each with charge −e, where e is the same size for both. An atom with equal numbers of protons and electrons has zero total charge: it is **neutral**.

Protons are held deep inside the nucleus and do not move from one object to another when you rub them together. Electrons, especially the outermost ones, are held more loosely. When two different materials are rubbed together, a tiny fraction of the electrons at the surface move from one to the other, depending on which material holds them more tightly. Glass holds its electrons less tightly than silk, so some pass from the glass to the silk. The glass, left with more protons than electrons, is positive; the silk, with extra electrons, is negative.

::: model orbital
Hydrogen, the simplest atom: one proton (+e) at the centre, and one electron (−e) spread over a cloud of places where it might be found. The equal and opposite charges make the atom neutral overall.
:::

## Why do some materials hold charge and others let it go?

How easily electrons can move inside a material decides how it behaves when charged. In a metal, some electrons are not tied to any one atom at all. They wander freely through the whole piece. A material like this is called a **conductor**. Put extra charge on a conductor and it spreads out over the whole object almost instantly, because the mobile charges push each other apart and nothing stops them moving.

In glass, plastic, rubber or dry wood, each electron stays bound to its own atom or molecule. These materials are **insulators**. Charge placed on an insulator stays roughly where it was put, because it has no easy way to move. That is why the rubbing experiments work best with insulators: rub a metal rod held in your hand and the charge simply drains away through the metal and through you.

The same ideas explain the balloon at the start of this lesson, which is more puzzling than it looks. A charged balloon sticks to a wall that has no overall charge at all. Why should a neutral object be attracted? The answer is that the wall's charges, though balanced, can shift. The balloon, charged negatively from your hair, pushes the electrons in each atom or molecule of the wall's surface slightly away from it, and pulls the positive nuclei slightly toward it. No charge leaves the wall, but each atom becomes very slightly lopsided, with its positive side facing the balloon. This separation of charge within a neutral body is called **polarization**.

Now the attraction follows from one more fact, which the next lesson makes precise: electric forces weaken with distance. The positive side of each atom is a little closer to the balloon than the negative side, so the attraction to the near side is a little stronger than the repulsion from the far side. The difference is a net pull toward the balloon. The same effect makes a charged comb pick up scraps of paper, and makes a thin stream of water from a tap bend toward it.

## Is there a smallest amount of charge?

Every charge ever measured on an isolated particle, or on any object, turns out to be a whole-number multiple of a single basic amount, the **elementary charge** e. An oil drop can carry a charge of 3e or 47e or −2e, but never 2.5e. Charge is measured in **coulombs** (C), and on that scale the elementary charge is very small.

::: equation Quantization of charge
q = ne, \qquad e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}
:::

Here n is a whole number, positive or negative. This property is called **quantization**: charge comes in discrete steps, like money in cents, rather than as a smoothly variable amount. Since 2019, the international system of units has fixed e at exactly 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C by definition, so a coulomb is now defined as a particular number of elementary charges. How many?

::: steps How many electrons make one coulomb?
$ n = \frac{q}{e}
Start from q = ne, which says any charge is a whole number of elementary charges, and divide both sides by e. That turns the question "how many electrons carry this charge?" into a division.
---
$ n = \frac{1\,\text{C}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}} \approx 6.24 \times 10^{18}
Put in a charge of one coulomb. The units cancel, leaving a pure count. A single coulomb is the charge of about 6.24 × 10¹⁸ electrons, a little over six billion billion.
:::

That number shows why charge looked smooth and continuous to early experimenters. Rubbing a balloon typically moves about a tenth of a microcoulomb, or less. That is still around a hundred billion electrons, so adding or removing one more makes no difference anyone could notice by watching the balloon. Seeing the steps required measuring the charge on objects small enough that each extra electron mattered.

::: history 1909 · Chicago
Robert Millikan and his graduate student Harvey Fletcher did that with drops of oil. They sprayed a fine mist of oil between two horizontal metal plates and watched individual drops through a small telescope. Some drops picked up a few extra electrons from the spray or from ionized air. By adjusting the voltage on the plates, they could make the upward electric force on a charged drop exactly balance its weight, so that it hung still in the air. From the voltage needed and the size of the drop, worked out from how fast it fell with the plates switched off, they could calculate its charge. Drop after drop, the charges came out as whole multiples of one value, about 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Millikan's published figure was slightly low, about 0.6% below the modern value, because he used an inaccurate value for the viscosity of air, which affected the sizes he worked out for the drops.
:::

There is one twist. Protons and neutrons are not fundamental: each is built from three smaller particles called **quarks**, and quarks carry fractional charges. The "up" quark has charge +⅔e and the "down" quark −⅓e. A proton is two ups and a down, giving ⅔ + ⅔ − ⅓ = +1; a neutron is one up and two downs, giving ⅔ − ⅓ − ⅓ = 0. But quarks are never found on their own. They are always bound in groups whose charges add to a whole multiple of e, so no experiment has ever isolated a fractional charge, and the rule that free charges come in whole multiples of e still holds.

::: note
Electric charge comes in two kinds, positive and negative, and is conserved in every process ever observed. It always appears in whole multiples of the elementary charge e, so charging an object never creates charge; it only moves electrons from one place to another.
:::
