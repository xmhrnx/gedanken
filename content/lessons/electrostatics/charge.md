Rub a balloon on your hair and it sticks to the wall. The ancient Greeks knew that rubbed amber attracts straw, and the Greek word for amber, *ēlektron*, gave electricity its name. For two thousand years this was a curiosity. Turning it into physics took three findings: there are two kinds of charge, charge is never created or destroyed, and it comes in fixed lumps.

## Two kinds

Rub two glass rods with silk and they repel each other. Rub two amber rods with fur and they also repel. But a rubbed glass rod *attracts* a rubbed amber rod. So there are two kinds of electrification, and the rule is simple: like charges repel, unlike charges attract.

::: history 1733 · Paris
Charles du Fay, superintendent of the royal gardens, found that charged objects fell into two classes that attracted each other and repelled their own kind. He called them "vitreous" (like glass) and "resinous" (like amber) electricity, and thought of them as two separate fluids.
:::

::: history 1747 · Philadelphia
Benjamin Franklin proposed instead a single electric fluid. An object with too much was **positive**, one with too little **negative**, and rubbing only moved fluid from one body to the other. He labelled rubbed glass positive. The choice was arbitrary, and it turned out unlucky: the particles that actually move in wires, electrons, ended up carrying "negative" charge. That is why the conventional direction of current is opposite to the way electrons flow.
:::

Franklin's names stuck, and so did the most important part of his idea.

## Charge is conserved

When you rub glass with silk, the glass becomes positive and the silk becomes negative by exactly the same amount. Nothing is made; charge is only moved. This holds in every process ever observed, including the creation of particles: when a gamma ray turns into an electron and a positron, the charges −e and +e add to zero.

Today we know what moves. Ordinary matter is made of atoms, each with a positive nucleus (protons, charge +e each) surrounded by electrons (charge −e each). A neutral atom has equal numbers of both. Rubbing transfers a tiny fraction of electrons from one surface to the other.

::: model atom
Protons in the nucleus carry +e and electrons carry −e; a neutral atom has as many of one as the other. The electrons do not really follow neat orbits, as later lessons explain.
:::

## Conductors and insulators

In a metal some electrons are free to wander through the whole piece. Such a material is a **conductor**: charge placed on it spreads out. In glass, plastic or rubber each electron stays bound to its atom. These are **insulators**, and charge stays where it is put, which is why rubbing works best on them.

This also explains why a charged balloon attracts a *neutral* wall. The balloon's negative charge pushes the wall's electrons slightly away and pulls its nuclei slightly closer. The nearer positive side is attracted more strongly than the farther negative side is repelled, so the net force is attraction. This shifting of charge within a neutral body is called **polarization**.

## Charge comes in lumps

Every charge ever measured on an isolated particle is a whole-number multiple of the **elementary charge** e. Charge is measured in **coulombs** (C), and e is tiny on that scale.

::: equation Quantization of charge
q = ne, \qquad e = 1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}
:::

Here n is a whole number, positive or negative. Since 2019 the SI fixes e at exactly 1.602176634 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, so the coulomb is defined by counting elementary charges.

::: steps How many electrons make one coulomb?
$ n = \frac{q}{e}
Rearrange q = ne for the number of elementary charges.
---
$ n = \frac{1\,\text{C}}{1.602 \times 10^{-19}\,\text{C}} \approx 6.24 \times 10^{18}
Divide. A coulomb is about six billion billion electrons' worth of charge.
:::

Rubbing a balloon typically moves well under a millionth of a coulomb, still trillions of electrons.

::: history 1909 · Chicago
Robert Millikan and his student Harvey Fletcher sprayed tiny oil drops between two charged plates and adjusted the voltage until a drop hung still, with the electric force balancing its weight. The charges they inferred always came out as multiples of one value, about 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Millikan's figure was slightly low because he used a wrong value for the viscosity of air.
:::

Quarks, the particles inside protons and neutrons, carry charges of ⅓e and ⅔e. But quarks are always bound in groups whose charges add to whole multiples of e, so no free fractional charge has ever been seen.

::: note
Charge comes in two signs, is conserved in every process, and always appears in whole multiples of e. Charging something never creates charge; it only separates it.
:::
