Within a year of the discovery of fission, physicists in Britain, France, Germany, the Soviet Union, Japan and the United States had all realized a weapon might be possible. Only the United States, working with Britain and Canada, turned the possibility into a working bomb before the war ended. The project grew from a letter into an enterprise employing about 130,000 people at its peak and costing roughly $2 billion of 1940s money.

## The letter

::: history August 1939 · Long Island, New York
Leo Szilard feared that Nazi Germany, where fission had been discovered, would build a bomb first. With Eugene Wigner and later Edward Teller, he visited Albert Einstein at his summer house. Einstein signed a letter to President Franklin Roosevelt, dated 2 August 1939, warning that "extremely powerful bombs of a new type" might be built from uranium and that Germany had stopped sales of uranium from occupied Czechoslovakia. The economist Alexander Sachs delivered it in person on 11 October, after war had broken out in Europe.
:::

Roosevelt set up an Advisory Committee on Uranium. It moved slowly, with small grants, for nearly two years. American physicists were not yet convinced a bomb was practical, because early thinking assumed natural uranium, which needed impossibly large amounts.

## The British push

::: history March 1940 · Birmingham, England
Otto Frisch and Rudolf Peierls, both refugees from Nazi Germany and so barred from Britain's radar work, asked what would happen with *pure* U-235 and fast neutrons. Their two-part memorandum concluded that the critical mass would be small, that a bomb would release energy equal to thousands of tons of explosive, that its radiation would kill people long after the explosion, and that there was no effective defence. They recommended building one before Germany did.
:::

The British government set up the **MAUD Committee** to evaluate the memorandum. Its report of July 1941 concluded that a uranium bomb was feasible and could be built within a few years. The report, and visits by the Australian physicist Mark Oliphant, persuaded American leaders. On 9 October 1941 Roosevelt approved a full effort. The Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor on 7 December brought the United States into the war two months later.

::: note
The decisive insight of 1940 was not new physics but a change of question: from "can natural uranium make a bomb?" (no) to "how much pure U-235 is needed?" (little enough to carry). Everything afterwards was an enormous industrial and engineering effort.
:::

## Proving the chain reaction

::: history 2 December 1942 · Chicago
Under the stands of the University of Chicago's disused Stagg Field, Enrico Fermi's team had stacked a pile of graphite blocks and uranium, several metres across, called **Chicago Pile-1**. With the last control rod slowly withdrawn, the neutron counters climbed steadily without levelling off: the first self-sustaining chain reaction. It ran for about four minutes at half a watt. Arthur Compton telephoned James Conant in code: "The Italian navigator has landed in the new world."
:::

CP-1 had no shielding and no cooling. It showed that k could be pushed above 1 and, just as importantly, that the reaction could be controlled. The large reactors that followed were built to make plutonium.

## Groves and Oppenheimer

In 1942 the Army took over, and the effort was given the cover name Manhattan Engineer District. In September General **Leslie Groves**, who had just overseen construction of the Pentagon, took command. He was forceful, impatient and an exceptional organizer, and he moved quickly: within days he had secured high priority for materials and purchased the site in Tennessee.

Groves chose **J. Robert Oppenheimer**, a theoretical physicist at Berkeley, to lead the weapon design laboratory. The choice surprised many: Oppenheimer had no Nobel Prize, no administrative experience and left-wing associations that worried security officials. He proved to be a brilliant scientific director, able to understand every part of the project and to hold together a laboratory of strong personalities.

## Three secret cities

- **Oak Ridge, Tennessee**: vast plants for separating U-235 from natural uranium. One building was among the largest in the world, and the site drew enormous amounts of electricity.
- **Hanford, Washington**: large reactors on the Columbia River that turned U-238 into plutonium, plus chemical plants to extract it. The first production reactor started in September 1944.
- **Los Alamos, New Mexico**: a laboratory on a remote mesa, opened in 1943, where scientists including Hans Bethe, Richard Feynman, Enrico Fermi, Edward Teller and John von Neumann worked out how to turn the material into a weapon.

Both production routes were pursued at once because no one knew which would work first, and both were needed: uranium for one design, plutonium for the other.

## Trinity

By 1945 the reason for the project had changed. Germany surrendered on 8 May; an American intelligence mission had already found that its program was far behind. The war with Japan continued. Some scientists, notably in the Franck Report of June 1945 and a petition organized by Szilard in July, urged that the bomb be demonstrated or not used on cities. The decision rested with President Harry Truman and his advisers.

::: history 16 July 1945 · Alamogordo, New Mexico
At 5:29 a.m., in a desert valley called the Jornada del Muerto, the first nuclear explosion, codenamed **Trinity**, tested the plutonium implosion design. It produced about 21 kilotons, far more than most at Los Alamos had predicted; a 2021 reanalysis put it nearer 25. Oppenheimer later recalled a line from the Bhagavad Gita: "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds." The test director Kenneth Bainbridge was blunter: "Now we are all sons of bitches."
:::

Truman learned of the success at the Potsdam Conference. On 26 July the Allies issued the Potsdam Declaration demanding Japan's unconditional surrender and warning of "prompt and utter destruction". Two weapons were ready by early August.

::: equation The yield scale, in the units used ever since
1\,\text{kiloton} = 4.184 \times 10^{12}\,\text{J}
:::
