Within a year of the discovery of fission, physicists in Britain, France, Germany, the Soviet Union, Japan and the United States had all realised that a weapon might be possible. Only one of those efforts produced a working bomb before the Second World War ended: the American project, carried out with Britain and Canada, that came to be known as the Manhattan Project. It grew from a letter into an enterprise that employed about 130,000 people at its peak and cost roughly $2 billion in the money of the 1940s. This lesson follows how that happened: the fear that started it, the calculation that made it seem practical, the decisions and people that shaped it, and the test in the New Mexico desert that showed it had worked.

## The letter

The physicists who first worried most about a bomb were refugees. Many had fled Nazi Germany and knew both its scientists and its government at first hand. Fission had been discovered in Berlin. Germany had first-rate physicists, a strong chemical industry, and from March 1939 control of the uranium mines of occupied Czechoslovakia. If a bomb could be built, the refugees reasoned, Germany might build it first, and the consequences of Hitler holding such a weapon were unthinkable.

::: history August 1939 · Long Island, New York
Leo Szilard, who had imagined the chain reaction in 1933, wanted to warn the American government but had no standing to do so. The most famous scientist in the world did. In July, with his fellow Hungarian Eugene Wigner, Szilard drove out to the house on Long Island where Albert Einstein was spending the summer, and explained the danger. Einstein, who had not followed the latest work on chain reactions, grasped it quickly. On a later visit, with Edward Teller at the wheel, they settled the text of a letter to President Franklin Roosevelt, which Einstein signed. Dated 2 August 1939, it warned that "extremely powerful bombs of a new type" might be built from uranium, and pointed out that Germany had stopped the sale of uranium from the Czechoslovak mines it had taken over. Getting the letter to the President took time. The economist Alexander Sachs, who had access to Roosevelt, delivered it in person on 11 October, six weeks after war had broken out in Europe.
:::

Roosevelt responded by setting up an Advisory Committee on Uranium. It moved slowly for nearly two years, handing out small grants. The caution was not just bureaucratic. Most American physicists were not convinced a bomb was practical, because the early thinking assumed natural uranium. In natural uranium, as the chain-reaction lesson explained, U-238 wastes most of the neutrons, and the amounts needed for an explosion came out impossibly large. A reactor seemed achievable; a bomb did not.

## The question that changed everything

The decisive step came from Britain, from two physicists who were, ironically, excluded from the country's most important war research.

::: history March 1940 · Birmingham, England
Otto Frisch and Rudolf Peierls had both fled Nazi Germany. As citizens of an enemy country, they were barred from Britain's secret radar work, so they were free to think about other things. They asked a question nobody had worked through properly: what if the bomb were made not of natural uranium but of *pure* U-235, the isotope that fissions readily, so that fast neutrons could drive the chain without U-238 to absorb them? Their calculation gave a critical mass small enough to fit in a bomb carried by an aircraft. They set out the conclusions in a short two-part memorandum: such a bomb would release energy equal to thousands of tons of high explosive; its radiation would go on killing people after the explosion; there would be no effective defence against it; and since Germany might be working on the same idea, Britain should build one first.
:::

The British government set up a committee of scientists, known as the **MAUD Committee**, to evaluate the memorandum. Its report of July 1941 concluded that a uranium bomb was feasible, that it could probably be built within a few years, and that it would be decisive in the war. The report crossed the Atlantic, and the Australian physicist Mark Oliphant, who worked in Britain, travelled to the United States in the summer of 1941 to press its conclusions on American scientists in person. They were persuaded, and so were the officials above them. On 9 October 1941 Roosevelt approved a full-scale effort. Two months later, on 7 December, Japan attacked the American fleet at Pearl Harbor, and the United States entered the war.

::: note
The decisive insight of 1940 was not new physics but a change of question: from "can natural uranium make a bomb?", to which the answer was no, to "how much pure U-235 is needed?", to which the answer was little enough to carry. Everything afterwards was an enormous industrial and engineering effort to make that material and turn it into a weapon.
:::

## Proving the chain reaction

Before anyone could build a bomb or a plutonium factory, there was a basic fact to establish. Nobody had yet shown that a chain reaction could actually be sustained.

::: history 2 December 1942 · Chicago
The task fell to Enrico Fermi, who had left Italy in 1938, partly because his wife was Jewish and Mussolini's government had adopted anti-Jewish laws. At the University of Chicago his team built a reactor in a squash court under the stands of the disused Stagg Field football stadium. It was a great pile of graphite blocks, several metres across, with lumps of uranium and uranium oxide embedded in a regular pattern, and it became known as **Chicago Pile-1**. Graphite slows the neutrons, so that natural uranium can sustain a chain. Rods of neutron-absorbing material held the pile below criticality. On the afternoon of 2 December, Fermi had the last control rod withdrawn step by step, checking his calculations at each stage. Finally the neutron counters began to climb and did not level off: the first self-sustaining chain reaction. Fermi let it run for about four and a half minutes at roughly half a watt, less power than a torch bulb, and then shut it down. Arthur Compton, who directed the Chicago work, telephoned James Conant in Washington with a coded message: "The Italian navigator has landed in the new world."
:::

Chicago Pile-1 had no shielding and no cooling, and its power was trivial. Its importance lay in what it proved. It showed that k could be pushed above 1 with real materials, and, just as importantly, that the reaction could be controlled. The large reactors that followed were built for a different purpose: to make plutonium.

## Groves and Oppenheimer

By then the Army had taken charge. In 1942 the effort was organised under the Army Corps of Engineers with the cover name Manhattan Engineer District, after the location of its first offices. In September, General **Leslie Groves**, who had just overseen construction of the Pentagon, was put in command. Groves was forceful, impatient and widely disliked, and he was an exceptional organiser. Within days of taking over he had secured the highest national priority for the project's materials and arranged the purchase of a huge site in Tennessee.

His most important appointment was a surprising one. To lead the laboratory that would design the weapon itself, Groves chose **J. Robert Oppenheimer**, a theoretical physicist at the University of California, Berkeley. Many were puzzled. Oppenheimer had no Nobel Prize, had never run anything larger than a research group, and had left-wing associations that alarmed security officials. Groves had judged him well. Oppenheimer proved a brilliant scientific director, able to understand every part of the project in detail, to see which problems mattered most, and to hold together a laboratory full of strong and sometimes difficult personalities.

## Three secret cities

The project's heart was three new communities, built from almost nothing in remote places and kept secret from the public, each with its own task.

At **Oak Ridge, Tennessee**, the job was to separate U-235 from natural uranium. Since the two isotopes are chemically identical and differ in mass by only about 1%, this required enormous plants. One building was among the largest in the world at the time, and the site drew enormous amounts of electricity.

At **Hanford, Washington**, beside the Columbia River, large reactors turned U-238 into plutonium, the route the chain-reaction lesson described, and chemical plants extracted the plutonium from the spent uranium. The river supplied cooling water. The first production reactor started in September 1944.

At **Los Alamos, New Mexico**, on a remote mesa, a laboratory opened in 1943 to work out how to turn the material into a weapon. Oppenheimer gathered an extraordinary group there, including Hans Bethe, Richard Feynman, Enrico Fermi, Edward Teller and John von Neumann, along with many younger scientists and a team sent from Britain.

Both routes to bomb material were pursued at once, at great cost, because no one knew which would succeed first, or whether either would. In the end both were needed: uranium for one design and plutonium for the other, for reasons the next lesson explains.

## A changed reason, and a test

By 1945 the reason the project began had disappeared. Germany surrendered on 8 May. An American intelligence mission that followed the advancing armies had already found that the German program was far behind, never having come close to a working reactor. The war with Japan continued, with heavy fighting on Okinawa and plans for an invasion of the Japanese home islands.

Some of the scientists now argued that the weapon should not be used against cities without warning. In June 1945 a group at Chicago led by James Franck wrote what became known as the Franck Report, urging a demonstration on an uninhabited area first, and warning that surprise use would start an arms race. In July Szilard circulated a petition to the President asking that the bomb not be used without giving Japan a chance to surrender. Neither changed the outcome. The decision rested with President Harry Truman, who had succeeded Roosevelt in April, and his advisers, and they treated the bomb as a weapon to be used.

::: history 16 July 1945 · Alamogordo, New Mexico
The uranium design was considered so certain to work that no test was planned. The plutonium design was far less certain, so it was tested first. At 5:29 in the morning, in a desert valley called the Jornada del Muerto, the first nuclear explosion, codenamed **Trinity**, lit up the mountains brighter than midday. Most at Los Alamos had expected a much smaller yield; the measured figure was about 21 kilotons, and a 2021 reanalysis put it nearer 25. Oppenheimer later recalled a line from the Hindu scripture the Bhagavad Gita: "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds." The test director, Kenneth Bainbridge, put it more bluntly to Oppenheimer: "Now we are all sons of bitches."
:::

Truman learned of the success at the Potsdam Conference near Berlin, where he was meeting the British and Soviet leaders. On 26 July the United States, Britain and China issued the Potsdam Declaration, demanding Japan's unconditional surrender and warning of "prompt and utter destruction" if it refused, without mentioning the new weapon. The Japanese government did not accept. Two weapons were ready by early August.

The scale used to describe them, then and ever since, is the energy of a thousand tonnes of TNT.

::: equation The yield scale, in the units used ever since
1\,\text{kiloton} = 4.184 \times 10^{12}\,\text{J}
:::
