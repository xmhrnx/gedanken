Two nuclear weapons have been used in war, both by the United States against Japan in August 1945. This lesson sets out what happened and the physics of what a nuclear explosion does, drawing on the public record. The numbers describe people: the dead, and the survivors, called *hibakusha*, who carried the injuries and the memory for the rest of their lives.

::: history 6 August 1945 · Hiroshima
At 8:15 a.m. the B-29 bomber *Enola Gay*, piloted by Colonel Paul Tibbets, released Little Boy over Hiroshima, a city of roughly 250,000 to 350,000 people with an army headquarters and port. It exploded about 600 metres above the city centre with a yield of about 15 kilotons (estimates range from 12 to 18).
:::

::: history 9 August 1945 · Nagasaki
The B-29 *Bockscar*, piloted by Major Charles Sweeney, was sent to Kokura, but clouds and smoke hid the target. The crew turned to the secondary target, Nagasaki. At 11:02 a.m. Fat Man exploded about 500 metres above the Urakami valley, more than 2 km from the planned aiming point, with a yield of about 21 kilotons. The surrounding hills confined much of the damage, so despite the larger yield, fewer people died than at Hiroshima.
:::

## Where the energy goes

An air burst like these puts its energy into a few distinct effects. Roughly:

::: equation Approximate energy split for a fission air burst
\text{blast} \approx 50\%, \quad \text{heat} \approx 35\%, \quad \text{nuclear radiation} \approx 15\%
:::

The radiation share is about 5% prompt (released within the first minute) and 10% residual (from radioactive fission products).

## Fireball and heat

Within a millionth of a second the weapon's material reaches tens of millions of degrees. At these temperatures most of the energy comes out as X-rays, which are absorbed in the nearby air within metres and heat it into a glowing ball, the **fireball**. It expands to a few hundred metres across within about a second, with a surface briefly hotter than the Sun's.

The fireball radiates intense light and heat, called **thermal radiation**, travelling at the speed of light. For a few seconds, everything in line of sight was exposed. In both cities, people in the open as far as about 3 km from the **hypocentre** (the point on the ground directly below the burst) suffered burns; the patterns of dark clothing were burned onto skin, and roof tiles near the centre blistered. The heat ignited countless fires. In Hiroshima they merged into a firestorm that burned out about 13 square kilometres.

## Blast

The rapidly expanding hot air drives a **shock wave**, a sharp jump in pressure travelling outward faster than sound, followed by winds of hundreds of kilometres per hour. Damage is governed mainly by **overpressure**, the pressure above normal atmospheric pressure. Like any wave spreading in three dimensions, the shock weakens with distance, so damage falls off in rough rings.

An overpressure of about 35 kilopascals (5 pounds per square inch, about a third of an atmosphere) destroys most ordinary houses. In Hiroshima almost every building within about 1.6 km of the hypocentre was destroyed, apart from a few reinforced concrete structures, such as the Industrial Promotion Hall now preserved as the Atomic Bomb Dome. Flying glass and collapsing buildings caused many deaths and injuries.

## Radiation

**Prompt radiation**, gamma rays and neutrons released in the first minute, penetrated buildings and bodies. Within about 1 km it delivered doses that were lethal to most people who survived the blast and heat. Many died over the following weeks of **acute radiation sickness**: nausea, hair loss, bleeding and infection as the bone marrow failed.

**Fallout** is radioactive material, chiefly fission products, that falls back to the ground. Because both weapons burst high and the fireballs did not touch the ground, little soil was drawn up and local fallout was relatively light. Some fell in rain, the "black rain" that fell on parts of Hiroshima. Long-term studies of survivors, run jointly by Japan and the United States since 1950, found clearly increased rates of leukemia and later of solid cancers among those exposed.

::: note
Most deaths in both cities came from blast and fire in the first moments and days. Radiation was the effect that set nuclear weapons apart, killing some who were otherwise uninjured and raising cancer risk for decades.
:::

## Casualties

Exact figures cannot be known. Population records burned, many military personnel and Korean and other conscripted laborers were not counted, and people kept dying of injuries for months. Estimates also depend on the cutoff date and method.

- **Hiroshima**: commonly cited figures are 90,000 to 166,000 deaths by the end of 1945. The 1946 US Strategic Bombing Survey estimated 70,000 to 80,000; the city of Hiroshima's later estimates are higher.
- **Nagasaki**: commonly cited figures are 60,000 to 80,000 deaths by the end of 1945.

Roughly half of the deaths in each city occurred on the day of the bombing.

## Surrender

On 8 August the Soviet Union declared war on Japan and invaded Manchuria the next morning. After deadlock in the Japanese government, Emperor Hirohito intervened in favour of accepting the Potsdam terms. Japan informed the Allies on 14 August, and on 15 August the Emperor's recorded broadcast told the Japanese people. The formal surrender was signed aboard the USS *Missouri* on 2 September 1945.

Historians continue to debate the decision. One view holds that the bombs ended the war quickly and avoided an invasion that would have cost far more lives on both sides. Others argue that Japan was close to surrender, that Soviet entry into the war was as decisive as the bombs, that a demonstration or changed terms might have sufficed, or that deliberately targeting cities was wrong in itself. The evidence and its interpretation remain contested.
