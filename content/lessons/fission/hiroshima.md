Two nuclear weapons have been used in war, both by the United States against Japan, three days apart in August 1945. This lesson sets out what happened in Hiroshima and Nagasaki, drawing on the public historical record, and explains the physics of what a nuclear explosion does: where its energy goes, and why each of its effects reached as far as it did. The numbers that follow describe people. They are the dead, and the survivors, called *hibakusha* in Japanese, who carried the injuries, the illnesses and the memory for the rest of their lives, and many of whom spent those lives telling the world what they had seen.

::: history 6 August 1945 · Hiroshima
Hiroshima was a city of roughly 250,000 to 350,000 people on the delta of the Ōta River in western Japan. It held an army headquarters and a port, and it had been largely spared the conventional bombing that had devastated other Japanese cities. Early on the morning of 6 August, the B-29 bomber *Enola Gay*, piloted by Colonel Paul Tibbets, flew in over the city in clear weather. At 8:15 a.m., as people were going to work and children were at school or out clearing firebreaks, it released Little Boy. The bomb fell for about three quarters of a minute and exploded about 600 metres above the city centre, with a yield of about 15 kilotons; estimates range from 12 to 18.
:::

::: history 9 August 1945 · Nagasaki
Three days later the B-29 *Bockscar*, piloted by Major Charles Sweeney, carried Fat Man toward its primary target, the city of Kokura. Haze and smoke from a nearby fire-bombed city hid the aiming point, and after several passes, running short of fuel, the crew turned to the secondary target, Nagasaki, a port city with major shipyards and arms factories. Clouds covered it too, until a gap opened. At 11:02 a.m. Fat Man exploded about 500 metres above the Urakami valley in the north of the city, more than 2 km from the planned aiming point, with a yield of about 21 kilotons. The steep hills around the valley shielded other parts of the city from the worst of the blast and heat, so although the yield was larger than at Hiroshima, fewer people died.
:::

## Where the energy goes

A nuclear explosion begins in the same way as the chain reaction described in earlier lessons: in under a microsecond, about a kilogram of material fissions, and its energy is deposited in the bomb's own material. What makes the weapon so destructive is what happens next, as that energy is passed to the surrounding air and then outward. For an air burst like these, high enough above the ground that the fireball does not touch it, the energy divides roughly as follows.

::: equation Approximate energy split for a fission air burst
\text{blast} \approx 50\%, \quad \text{heat} \approx 35\%, \quad \text{nuclear radiation} \approx 15\%
:::

The radiation share itself splits into two parts. About 5% of the total is **prompt radiation**, gamma rays and neutrons released within the first minute. The other 10% is **residual radiation**, given off over the following hours, days and years by the radioactive fission products, the fragments of split nuclei. Each of the three main effects acts in a different way and over a different range, so it helps to follow them in the order in which they arrive.

## Fireball and heat

Within a millionth of a second, the bomb's material reaches temperatures of tens of millions of degrees, far hotter than the centre of the Sun. Matter that hot radiates most of its energy not as visible light but as X-rays. Air absorbs X-rays strongly, so they travel only a short distance before being soaked up, heating a ball of air around the bomb to incandescence. This glowing sphere of hot gas is the **fireball**. It expands rapidly, growing to a few hundred metres across within about a second, and for a brief moment its surface is hotter than the surface of the Sun.

The fireball pours out intense light and heat, called **thermal radiation**. Because it is light, it travels at the speed of light, so it arrives everywhere in the city before the blast wave does, and it lasts only a few seconds. Anything in direct line of sight was exposed; anything in shadow was protected. In both cities, people in the open as far as about 3 km from the **hypocentre**, the point on the ground directly below the burst, suffered burns. Dark fabric absorbs more light than pale, so the patterns of dark clothing were burned onto people's skin. Near the hypocentre, the surfaces of granite stones and ceramic roof tiles were blistered, and in some places a person or an object blocking the light left a pale shadow on a wall or step.

The heat also set fire to wood, paper and fabric across a wide area, and the blast that followed overturned stoves and broke gas lines, starting more. In Hiroshima, a city built largely of wood, the fires merged within about half an hour into a **firestorm**, a fire so large that its rising heat draws in winds from all sides, which feed it further. It burned out roughly 11 to 13 square kilometres, the whole centre of the city. Nagasaki's hills and the shape of its valley prevented a firestorm of the same kind, though fires there were still extensive.

## Blast

As the fireball expands, it shoves the surrounding air outward faster than sound can travel, creating a **shock wave**: a sharp, almost instantaneous jump in pressure moving outward, followed by winds of hundreds of kilometres per hour. What does the damage is mainly the **overpressure**, the amount by which the pressure in the wave exceeds normal atmospheric pressure. A building is pushed on its exposed side by the full overpressure before the pressure can equalise around it, and walls designed to stand against wind are not built for that.

An overpressure of about 35 kilopascals, which is 5 pounds per square inch or roughly a third of an atmosphere, is enough to destroy most ordinary houses. That figure helps make sense of the damage. In Hiroshima almost every building within about 1.6 km of the hypocentre was destroyed, apart from a few reinforced concrete structures. One of these, the Industrial Promotion Hall, stood almost directly beneath the burst; because the blast struck it from above rather than the side, its walls and the frame of its dome survived. It is preserved today as the Atomic Bomb Dome. Collapsing buildings and flying glass and debris caused many deaths and injuries, often among people who had survived the heat.

Because the blast spreads in three dimensions, it weakens steadily with distance, and damage falls off in rough rings around the hypocentre. A simple argument about how a shock spreads shows why the larger Nagasaki weapon did not damage a much wider area.

::: steps How the reach of the blast scales with yield
$ E_\text{blast} \propto Y
The energy that goes into the blast is a roughly fixed share, about half, of the total yield Y. So a weapon with twice the yield puts twice the energy into its shock wave. This is the starting point; the question is how far that extra energy pushes a given level of damage.
---
$ \text{overpressure at } R \sim \frac{Y}{R^3}
A shock wave of a given strength involves a given amount of energy in each unit of volume of air it passes through. When it has spread to a distance R, its energy is shared through a region whose volume grows as R³. So, roughly, the overpressure at distance R depends on the yield divided by R³. This ignores some details, such as the reflection of the wave from the ground, but it captures how the effect scales, and it is the rule used in standard references on weapons effects.
---
$ R \propto Y^{1/3}
Set the overpressure equal to a fixed value, such as the level that destroys houses, and ask at what distance it occurs. Keeping Y/R³ constant requires R³ to grow in proportion to Y, so R grows only as the cube root of the yield. Doubling the yield does not double the radius of destruction; it extends it by about 26%.
---
$ \left(\frac{21}{15}\right)^{1/3} \approx 1.12
Apply this to the two weapons. Nagasaki's yield was about 1.4 times Hiroshima's, but its cube root is only about 1.12, so the radius at which houses were destroyed was only about 12% larger. Combined with the hills that blocked much of the blast, this is why the larger weapon did less damage to its city. The effect of a nuclear weapon depends as much on where it explodes as on how big it is.
:::

## Radiation

Radiation is the effect that sets nuclear weapons apart from all others. **Prompt radiation**, the gamma rays and neutrons released in the first minute, travels at or near the speed of light and passes through walls and bodies alike. Within about 1 km of the hypocentre, it delivered doses that were lethal to most people who survived the blast and the heat. Radiation damages the body by breaking molecules in its cells, and it falls hardest on cells that divide often, such as those in bone marrow, which makes blood cells, and in the lining of the gut. Many people who seemed at first to have escaped unhurt fell ill in the days and weeks that followed with **acute radiation sickness**: nausea, hair loss, bleeding under the skin, and infections they could no longer fight, as their bone marrow failed. Doctors at the time, many of them injured themselves and working with almost no supplies, did not know what they were seeing.

**Fallout** is radioactive material, chiefly fission products, that falls back to the ground after the explosion. How much falls nearby depends on whether the fireball touches the ground. If it does, it vaporises and lifts tonnes of soil and debris, which mix with the fission products and fall back as heavy, radioactive dust. Both of the 1945 weapons burst high enough that the fireballs did not reach the ground, so little soil was drawn up and local fallout was relatively light. Some radioactive material came down in rain, the "black rain", dark with soot and debris, that fell on parts of Hiroshima and its outskirts within hours of the bombing, and on areas east of Nagasaki.

The long-term effects were studied more thoroughly than those of almost any other exposure in history. The Atomic Bomb Casualty Commission, set up by the United States in the late 1940s, began following survivors, and since 1975 its successor, the Radiation Effects Research Foundation, has been run jointly by Japan and the United States. Its central study follows a large group of survivors identified in 1950. These studies found clearly increased rates of leukemia, beginning a few years after the bombings, and later of solid cancers, with risk rising with the dose received. They remain a principal basis of the radiation safety standards used around the world.

::: note
Most deaths in both cities came from blast and fire in the first moments and days. Radiation was the effect that set nuclear weapons apart: it killed some people who were otherwise uninjured, weeks after the explosion, and raised the risk of cancer among survivors for decades.
:::

## How many died

Exact figures cannot be known, and the reasons are themselves part of the history. Population records burned with the cities. The number of people present was uncertain to begin with, because Hiroshima held many soldiers, and both cities held Korean and other conscripted labourers and people evacuated from elsewhere, many of whom were never counted. People went on dying of their injuries and of radiation sickness for months. And different estimates count up to different dates and use different methods.

For Hiroshima, commonly cited figures are 90,000 to 166,000 deaths by the end of 1945. The United States Strategic Bombing Survey, writing in 1946, estimated 70,000 to 80,000, while the city of Hiroshima's later estimates are higher. For Nagasaki, commonly cited figures are 60,000 to 80,000 deaths by the end of 1945. In each city roughly half of those deaths happened on the day of the bombing itself.

## Surrender

On 8 August, between the two bombings, the Soviet Union declared war on Japan, and early the next morning its armies invaded Manchuria, which Japan had occupied. Japan's leadership was deadlocked between those who would accept the Potsdam terms and those who wanted to fight on in hope of better conditions. Emperor Hirohito intervened, in an unprecedented step, in favour of accepting the terms. After an exchange with the Allies over the Emperor's future position, Japan informed them of its acceptance on 14 August. On 15 August a recording of the Emperor's voice, which most Japanese had never heard before, told the nation on the radio. The formal surrender was signed aboard the battleship USS *Missouri* in Tokyo Bay on 2 September 1945.

Historians continue to debate the decision to use the bombs, and the debate turns on questions that the evidence does not settle. One view holds that the bombs ended the war quickly and avoided an invasion of Japan that would have cost far more lives, Japanese and Allied. Others argue that Japan was already close to surrender; that the Soviet entry into the war was as decisive as the bombs in persuading its leaders; that a demonstration, or an earlier assurance about the Emperor, might have been enough; or that deliberately destroying cities full of civilians was wrong whatever its effect on the war. The evidence and its interpretation remain contested. What is not contested is what happened on the ground, and the survivors of both cities have worked ever since to make sure it is remembered.
