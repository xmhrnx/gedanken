A nucleus is held together by the strong force, and pulling it apart into separate protons and neutrons costs energy. That cost is the nucleus's **binding energy**. The more tightly bound a nucleus is, the more energy was released when it formed, and the less its total mass.

## Mass defect

Weigh a helium-4 nucleus and you find it is lighter than two free protons plus two free neutrons, by about 0.75%. This shortfall is the **mass defect**. Einstein's relation says mass and energy are interchangeable:

::: equation Mass–energy equivalence
E = mc^2
:::

So the mass defect, multiplied by c², equals the binding energy. Nuclear physicists measure masses in atomic mass units (u, one twelfth of a carbon-12 atom), and one u corresponds to 931.5 MeV. A mass difference of a thousandth of a u is therefore nearly 1 MeV, roughly a million times the energy of a chemical bond.

## The binding energy curve

What matters for releasing energy is the binding energy *per nucleon* (per proton or neutron), because that tells you how tightly each particle is held. Plotting it against the number of nucleons gives one of the most important graphs in physics.

::: model binding-curve
Binding energy per nucleon against mass number. Iron and nickel sit at the peak; uranium is on the gently falling right-hand side.
:::

The curve rises steeply through the light nuclei, peaks near iron-56 at about 8.8 MeV per nucleon, and then falls slowly to about 7.6 MeV for uranium. Two forces explain the shape. The strong force is short-range: each nucleon is bound only to its immediate neighbours, so small nuclei, with many nucleons at the surface, are loosely bound. The electrical repulsion between protons is long-range: every proton pushes on every other, so in large nuclei it grows faster than the strong-force attraction and erodes the binding.

Any move toward the peak releases energy. Light nuclei can climb it by joining (fusion); heavy nuclei can climb it by splitting (fission). A uranium nucleus that breaks into two fragments near mass 90 to 140 ends up with every nucleon about 0.9 MeV more tightly bound.

::: note
Fission does not convert uranium into energy. The total number of protons and neutrons is unchanged. Energy is released because the nucleons are rearranged into more tightly bound nuclei, and the products weigh slightly less.
:::

## About 200 MeV per fission

::: steps Energy released by one U-235 fission
$ B({}^{236}\mathrm{U}) \approx 236 \times 7.6\,\text{MeV} \approx 1790\,\text{MeV}
Absorbing a neutron turns U-235 into U-236 for an instant. Its total binding energy is the number of nucleons times the binding per nucleon.
---
$ B(\text{fragments}) \approx 236 \times 8.5\,\text{MeV} \approx 2010\,\text{MeV}
The fragments have about 8.5 MeV per nucleon. Counting all 236 nucleons here slightly overstates it, since the two or three freed neutrons are unbound, but it is good enough for an estimate.
---
$ \Delta E \approx 2010\,\text{MeV} - 1790\,\text{MeV} \approx 220\,\text{MeV}
The gain in binding is the energy released. Precise measurements give about 200 MeV.
---
$ \Delta m = \frac{\Delta E}{c^2} \approx 200\,\text{MeV} \div 931.5\,\text{MeV/u} \approx 0.21\,\text{u}
The products are lighter by about a fifth of an atomic mass unit, less than 0.1% of the original mass. This is the figure Meitner found in 1938.
:::

Most of the 200 MeV, about 170 MeV, appears as kinetic energy of the two fragments, which slam into surrounding atoms within a fraction of a millimetre and heat the material. The rest is carried by neutrons, gamma rays, and the later radioactive decay of the fragments.

## Comparison with chemistry

Chemical reactions rearrange electrons, which are bound to atoms by a few electron volts. Burning a carbon atom releases about 4 eV; the detonation of TNT releases a few eV per molecule.

::: equation Nuclear versus chemical energy per reaction
200\,\text{MeV} \div {\sim}4\,\text{eV} \approx 5 \times 10^7
:::

Fission releases tens of millions of times more energy per event. Measured per kilogram of material the factor is similar, as the next calculation shows.

::: steps Fully fissioning one kilogram of U-235
$ N = \left(1000\,\text{g} \div 235\,\text{g/mol}\right) \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}\,\text{/mol} \approx 2.56 \times 10^{24}
The number of nuclei: moles of U-235 in a kilogram times Avogadro's number.
---
$ E = 2.56 \times 10^{24} \times 200\,\text{MeV} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-13}\,\text{J/MeV} \approx 8.2 \times 10^{13}\,\text{J}
Multiply by the energy per fission and convert MeV to joules.
---
$ 8.2 \times 10^{13}\,\text{J} \div 4.18 \times 10^{12}\,\text{J/kt} \approx 20\,\text{kt}
A kiloton of TNT is defined as 4.184 × 10¹² J. About 10 MeV per fission escapes as neutrinos, so the usable figure is closer to 17–20 kilotons.
:::

A kilogram of TNT releases about 4.2 MJ, so a kilogram of fully fissioned uranium is equivalent to roughly twenty million kilograms of explosive. This is the number that alarmed physicists in 1939: a weapon could, in principle, be small enough to carry while matching a city-sized raid of conventional bombs. In practice the 1945 weapons fissioned only a small fraction of their material, for reasons covered in the coming lessons.

::: history 1905 · Bern, Switzerland
Einstein derived E = mc² as a consequence of special relativity, suggesting that radioactive materials might test it. For three decades it seemed of theoretical interest only. Nuclear mass measurements in the 1930s, notably by Francis Aston with his mass spectrograph, made it a practical tool, and fission made it famous.
:::
