In the early 1930s the nucleus was a new frontier. Physicists knew it was tiny and dense and that it held protons, but they had few ways to get inside it. Within seven years they had found the neutron, learned to fire it into every element in the periodic table, and stumbled on a reaction that released tens of millions of times more energy per atom than any chemical process. That reaction, nuclear fission, was found by chemists who trusted a result that seemed impossible and explained by physicists sitting on a log in the snow. This lesson tells how, and works through the first estimate of the energy involved.

## A projectile that is not pushed away

To learn what is inside something, you hit it with something else and watch what happens. Before 1932 the main projectiles for nuclear experiments were **alpha particles**, helium nuclei shot out by radioactive elements such as radium. They were fast and plentiful, and Rutherford had used them to discover the nucleus. But they had a serious limitation. An alpha particle carries a positive charge, and so does every nucleus, so the two repel. The heavier the target, the more protons it has and the more strongly it pushes the alpha away. For heavy elements such as uranium, with 92 protons, alphas from natural sources simply could not get close enough to touch the nucleus.

::: history 1932 · Cambridge, England
James Chadwick, Rutherford's long-time assistant, settled a puzzle that had baffled laboratories in Germany and France. A penetrating radiation came out of beryllium when it was struck by alpha particles, and others had taken it for high-energy gamma rays. Chadwick showed from the way it knocked nuclei about that it had to be a neutral particle with about the mass of a proton, and he called it the **neutron**. The discovery explained at last why nuclei weigh more than their protons alone, and it earned him the 1935 Nobel Prize in Physics.
:::

The neutron's lack of charge, which had made it so hard to find, made it exactly the projectile physicists needed. Nothing pushes a neutron away from a nucleus. It does not need to be fast to get in; even a slow neutron can drift up to a nucleus and be absorbed. Enrico Fermi in Rome saw at once that this opened up the whole periodic table.

::: history 1934 · Rome
Fermi assembled a small group of young physicists and a chemist and set out to bombard every element he could obtain with neutrons, watching for new radioactivity that would show a nucleus had been changed. In October 1934 they noticed something odd: their results depended on what the apparatus stood on. A wooden table gave stronger activity than a marble one. They tried passing the neutrons through paraffin wax first, and the activity jumped by a large factor. Fermi worked out why the same afternoon. Paraffin is full of hydrogen, whose nuclei weigh the same as neutrons, and in collisions with them the neutrons are slowed down. A slow neutron spends longer near each nucleus it passes, so it is far more likely to be captured. When they bombarded uranium, the heaviest element then known, they found several new radioactive substances. The natural reading was that the uranium nucleus had absorbed a neutron and become a new element heavier than uranium, and Fermi's team cautiously suggested so. The German chemist Ida Noddack published a different suggestion: perhaps the nucleus had broken into several large pieces. Nobody followed it up. No known reaction did anything of the kind, and she offered no physics to support it.
:::

For the next four years, laboratories in Rome, Paris and Berlin chased these supposed "transuranic" elements, those beyond uranium. Each new experiment made the picture more confusing, with more and more substances, each with its own half-life, that refused to fit together.

## Barium where there should be none

The strongest team on the problem was in Berlin, at the Kaiser Wilhelm Institute for Chemistry. It was led by the chemist Otto Hahn and the physicist Lise Meitner, who had worked together for three decades, joined by the younger chemist Fritz Strassmann. They had been on the uranium puzzle since 1934. Meitner was of Jewish descent, and her Austrian citizenship had protected her from Nazi law. That protection ended when Germany annexed Austria in March 1938, and in July she fled, eventually reaching Stockholm. Hahn and Strassmann carried on in Berlin and wrote to her about every result.

In December 1938 they were tracing one of the products that they thought was radium, an element just below uranium in the periodic table and chemically similar to barium. To separate it they added ordinary barium as a carrier, a standard technique in which a small amount of an element is made to travel along with a chemically similar one. They then tried to separate the radioactive "radium" from the barium again. They could not. Repeated, careful separations always left the radioactivity with the barium. The conclusion was unavoidable for chemists who trusted their methods: the product *was* barium, element 56.

That was startling. Uranium is element 92. Every known nuclear reaction changed an element by one or two places in the periodic table, by adding or removing a proton or an alpha particle. This one appeared to jump 36 places at once. Hahn wrote to Meitner on 19 December, asking whether she could find a physical explanation for what the chemistry showed. He and Strassmann sent their paper to the journal days later, reporting the result with evident unease, and it appeared on 6 January 1939.

## The liquid drop splits

::: history Christmas 1938 · Kungälv, Sweden
Meitner spent the holiday in a small town on the Swedish west coast with her nephew Otto Frisch, also a physicist, who was working in Niels Bohr's institute in Copenhagen. Frisch had brought a problem of his own, but she wanted to talk only about Hahn's letter. They went out walking in the snow, Frisch on skis and Meitner on foot, insisting she could keep up. At some point they sat down on a tree trunk, found scraps of paper, and began to calculate.
:::

The tool they reached for was the **liquid-drop model** of the nucleus, proposed by George Gamow and developed by Niels Bohr. It treats a nucleus as a drop of electrically charged fluid. Two effects compete in it. The strong nuclear force, which binds each nucleon to its neighbours, acts like the surface tension of a liquid, holding the drop in a compact, round shape. The electrical repulsion between protons pushes outward and tries to break it up. In small nuclei surface tension wins easily. But repulsion grows rapidly with the number of protons, and Meitner and Frisch realised that at 92 protons the two effects are nearly balanced. A uranium nucleus is a drop on the edge of instability.

Now add a neutron. Absorbing it gives the nucleus extra energy, enough to set the drop wobbling. If it stretches far enough, it becomes elongated, then pinches in the middle into a dumbbell. At that point the geometry turns against the strong force: its short reach can no longer hold the two lobes together across the narrowing neck, while electrical repulsion acts between the lobes at full strength. The repulsion wins, and the drop snaps into two smaller nuclei.

::: equation One way a uranium nucleus can split
n + {}^{235}\mathrm{U} \to {}^{141}\mathrm{Ba} + {}^{92}\mathrm{Kr} + 3n
:::

The bookkeeping works. Charge balances: uranium's 92 protons become barium's 56 plus krypton's 36. The total number of nucleons balances too: the 1 neutron plus 235 nucleons on the left equals 141 plus 92 plus 3 free neutrons on the right. This is only one of many possible splits, since the fragments vary from one fission to the next, but barium is a typical product, and it is the one Hahn and Strassmann found.

Meitner and Frisch could also see where the energy would come from. At the moment of the split, the two fragments are both positively charged and almost touching. Their mutual repulsion, no longer opposed by the strong force, drives them apart. That gave a quick way to estimate the energy released.

::: steps Estimating the energy of the split
$ E \approx \frac{k e^2 Z_1 Z_2}{r}
The electrical potential energy of two charges, Z₁e and Z₂e, a distance r apart, is k times the product of the charges divided by r, where k is the constant in Coulomb's law. This energy is stored in the configuration just after the split, and as the fragments fly apart it is all converted into their kinetic energy. So it is a direct estimate of the energy released.
---
$ k e^2 \approx 1.44\,\text{MeV fm}
To use the formula at nuclear scale, it helps to express the combination ke² in units suited to nuclei: MeV, millions of electronvolts, for energy, and femtometres (1 fm is 10⁻¹⁵ m) for distance. In those units ke² is about 1.44 MeV·fm. This is a fixed constant, so the rest of the estimate is a matter of counting protons and judging a distance.
---
$ E \approx 1.44\,\text{MeV fm} \times \frac{56 \times 36}{13\,\text{fm}}
Barium has 56 protons and krypton 36, so those are Z₁ and Z₂. Just after the split the fragments are roughly touching, so the distance between their centres is about the sum of their radii. Using the rule that a nucleus's radius is about 1.2 fm times the cube root of its mass number, that comes to roughly 12 to 13 fm.
---
$ E \approx 220\,\text{MeV}
Multiplying out, 56 × 36 is 2,016, and 1.44 × 2,016 ÷ 13 is about 220 MeV. Given the rough distance, the honest statement is that the energy is of order 200 MeV per fission. For comparison, a chemical reaction such as burning releases a few electronvolts per atom, so this is tens of millions of times more.
:::

Meitner then checked the answer a second way, using the binding energy curve. She remembered the measured masses of nuclei well enough to work out that the two fragments together should weigh less than the original uranium nucleus plus neutron, by about one fifth of the mass of a proton. By E = mc², that missing mass corresponds to about 200 MeV. Two completely different calculations, one from electrical repulsion and one from nuclear masses, gave the same answer. That agreement was what convinced them the idea was right. The next lesson follows the mass bookkeeping in detail.

::: note
Fission was found by chemists who trusted their measurements enough to report something that seemed impossible, and explained by physicists who took a simple model seriously. The energy per fission, about 200 MeV, was clear from the first back-of-the-envelope calculation, and it is tens of millions of times larger than the energy of any chemical reaction.
:::

## Naming it and spreading the news

Back in Copenhagen, Frisch needed a name for the process. He asked an American biologist working there, William Arnold, what biologists call the way a single cell divides in two. "Binary fission," Arnold told him, and Frisch called the new process **nuclear fission**. He also set out to see it directly. If a uranium nucleus really split into two highly charged fragments sharing well over 100 MeV between them, they would tear through a gas and produce bursts of ionization far larger than an alpha particle could. On 13 January 1939 he placed uranium in an ionization chamber, a detector that measures how much a gas is ionized, bombarded it with neutrons, and saw exactly those large pulses. Meitner and Frisch's paper explaining fission appeared in *Nature* on 11 February 1939, followed a week later by Frisch's report of the fragments.

::: history January 1939 · New York and Washington
Bohr heard the news from Frisch just before sailing for the United States, and he spent the voyage working on the theory. He arrived in New York on 16 January, and the news spread among physicists there faster than he had intended. On 26 January he discussed it at a conference on theoretical physics in Washington, and several physicists left the meeting to repeat the experiment. Within days laboratories across the United States had confirmed it. Within weeks Bohr had worked out a subtler point: the rare isotope uranium-235, which makes up less than 1% of natural uranium, was the one that fissions when struck by slow neutrons, while the common uranium-238 does not.
:::

Hahn received the 1944 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery, alone. Meitner's essential part in it, and in the years of work that led to it, went unrecognised by the Nobel committee, an omission now widely regarded as an injustice.

Almost immediately, physicists in several countries asked the obvious next question. The fission fragments come from a heavy nucleus rich in neutrons. Did each fission also release spare neutrons? If it did, and if those neutrons could split other nuclei, one fission could trigger more, and those more again.
