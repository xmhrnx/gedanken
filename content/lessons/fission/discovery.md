In the 1930s the nucleus was a new frontier. Physicists knew it held protons, and that it was tiny and dense, but they had few tools for probing it. Within seven years they found the neutron, learned to fire it into every element, and stumbled on a reaction that released a hundred million times more energy per atom than any chemical process.

## A new probe

Before 1932 the main projectiles for nuclear experiments were alpha particles, helium nuclei from radioactive sources. Being positively charged, alphas are repelled by the positive nucleus, and they cannot get close to heavy elements at all.

::: history 1932 · Cambridge, England
James Chadwick showed that a puzzling penetrating radiation, produced when beryllium was struck by alpha particles, consisted of neutral particles with about the mass of a proton. He called it the **neutron**. The discovery explained why nuclei weigh more than their protons alone, and it earned him the 1935 Nobel Prize.
:::

A neutron has no charge, so nothing pushes it away from a nucleus. Even a slow neutron can drift in and be absorbed. Enrico Fermi saw at once that this made it an ideal probe.

::: history 1934 · Rome
Fermi's group bombarded element after element with neutrons and watched for new radioactivity. In October they found that neutrons slowed down by passing through paraffin wax were far *more* effective, because a slow neutron spends longer near each nucleus. Uranium, the heaviest known element, produced several new radioactive substances, which Fermi's team cautiously interpreted as new elements heavier than uranium. The chemist Ida Noddack suggested the nucleus might instead have broken into large pieces, but no one took the idea up.
:::

For four years laboratories in Rome, Paris and Berlin chased these "transuranic" elements. The results grew more confusing with each experiment.

## Barium where there should be none

In Berlin, the chemist Otto Hahn, the physicist Lise Meitner and the chemist Fritz Strassmann had worked on the uranium puzzle since 1934. In July 1938 Meitner, who was of Jewish descent and had lost the protection of her Austrian citizenship when Germany annexed Austria, fled to Stockholm. Hahn and Strassmann continued and wrote to her about every result.

In December 1938 they carried out careful chemical separations on neutron-irradiated uranium and found that one product behaved exactly like **barium**, element 56. Uranium is element 92. Every known nuclear reaction changed an element by one or two places in the periodic table; this one appeared to jump 36. Hahn wrote to Meitner on 19 December asking whether she could find a physical explanation. Their paper went to press days later and appeared on 6 January 1939.

## The liquid drop splits

::: history Christmas 1938 · Kungälv, Sweden
Meitner spent the holiday in a small Swedish town with her nephew Otto Frisch, also a physicist, who was working in Copenhagen. On a walk in the snow, Frisch on skis and Meitner on foot, they sat on a tree trunk and worked out what Hahn's barium meant.
:::

They used the **liquid-drop model**, proposed by George Gamow and developed by Niels Bohr, which treats the nucleus as a droplet of charged fluid. The strong nuclear force acts like surface tension, holding the drop together; the electrical repulsion between protons pushes it apart. For a nucleus with 92 protons the two are nearly balanced. Absorbing a neutron adds enough energy to set the drop wobbling; it stretches into a dumbbell, the electrical repulsion between the two ends wins, and it snaps into two smaller nuclei.

::: equation One way a uranium nucleus can split
n + {}^{235}\mathrm{U} \to {}^{141}\mathrm{Ba} + {}^{92}\mathrm{Kr} + 3n
:::

Charge balances (92 = 56 + 36) and so does the nucleon count (1 + 235 = 141 + 92 + 3). The two fragments, both positively charged, start almost touching and fly apart under their mutual repulsion. Meitner and Frisch estimated that energy directly.

::: steps Estimating the energy of the split
$ E \approx \frac{k e^2 Z_1 Z_2}{r}
The electrical potential energy of two charges Z₁e and Z₂e at separation r. In nuclear units, k·e² ≈ 1.44 MeV·fm (1 fm, a femtometre, is 10⁻¹⁵ m).
---
$ E \approx 1.44\,\text{MeV fm} \times \frac{56 \times 36}{13\,\text{fm}}
Barium has 56 protons and krypton 36. Just after the split their centres are roughly 13 fm apart, about the sum of their radii.
---
$ E \approx 220\,\text{MeV}\text{, of order } 200\,\text{MeV}
All of this becomes kinetic energy of the fragments as they fly apart. One MeV is a million electron volts; a chemical reaction releases a few eV.
:::

Meitner then checked the books. Using known nuclear masses, she found that the two fragments together weigh less than the original nucleus by about one fifth of a proton mass. By E = mc² that missing mass corresponds to about 200 MeV, matching the electrical estimate. The next lesson follows that bookkeeping in detail.

::: note
Fission was found by chemists who trusted their results enough to report something "impossible", and explained by physicists who took a simple model seriously. The energy per event, about 200 MeV, was clear from the first calculation.
:::

## Naming it and spreading the news

Frisch asked an American biologist in Copenhagen, William Arnold, what biologists call the division of a cell. "Binary fission," he said, and Frisch named the process **nuclear fission**. On 13 January 1939 Frisch detected the large pulses of ionization from the flying fragments, confirming the energy directly. Meitner and Frisch's paper appeared in *Nature* on 11 February 1939.

::: history January 1939 · New York and Washington
Bohr heard the news from Frisch just before sailing to the United States and arrived in New York on 16 January. Word spread quickly among physicists there, and on 26 January Bohr discussed it at a theoretical physics conference in Washington. Within days several American laboratories had reproduced the result. Within weeks Bohr argued that it was the rare isotope uranium-235, not the common uranium-238, that fissioned with slow neutrons.
:::

The obvious next question, asked almost immediately in several countries, was whether each fission also released extra neutrons. If it did, one fission could trigger others.
