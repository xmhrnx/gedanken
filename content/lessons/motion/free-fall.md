Drop a hammer and a feather and the hammer lands first. For two thousand years this was taken as evidence that heavier things fall faster, as Aristotle had taught. It is wrong. The feather is held back by air. Without air, everything near the Earth's surface falls with the same constant acceleration, whatever its mass or shape.

*Free fall* means motion under gravity alone, with air resistance negligible. For dense, compact objects over short drops, that is a good approximation.

## g

The acceleration of free fall is written g:

::: equation Acceleration due to gravity near Earth's surface
g \approx 9.8\,\text{m/s}^2, \quad \text{directed downward}
:::

Every second of falling adds about 9.8 m/s to the downward velocity. The exact value varies slightly with location, from about 9.78 m/s² at the equator to 9.83 m/s² at the poles, because of the Earth's rotation and its slightly flattened shape.

Since g is constant, the four equations from the last lesson apply directly. Take upward as positive, so a = −g.

## The odd-number rule

The marks in the model show a pattern Galileo found. Start from rest at x = 0, and measure the distance fallen d downward, so d = ½gt².

::: steps Distances in successive equal intervals
$ d(1) = \tfrac{1}{2}g, \quad d(2) = \tfrac{1}{2}g \cdot 4, \quad d(3) = \tfrac{1}{2}g \cdot 9
Distances fallen after 1, 2 and 3 time units go as 1, 4, 9: the squares.
---
$ d(2) - d(1) = \tfrac{1}{2}g \cdot 3, \quad d(3) - d(2) = \tfrac{1}{2}g \cdot 5
The distance covered during each separate interval is the difference between successive squares.
---
$ n^2 - (n - 1)^2 = 2n - 1
Differences of consecutive squares are always the odd numbers 1, 3, 5, 7, ...
:::

So in equal time intervals a falling body covers distances in the ratio 1 : 3 : 5 : 7. Galileo could test this without measuring g, or even a precise unit of time, which is what made the experiment possible in 1600.

::: history c. 1604 · Padua
Free fall is too fast to time with the clocks Galileo had. His solution was to slow it down: roll a bronze ball down a long, smooth inclined plane. He argued that rolling down a slope is falling diluted: the motion has the same character, just with a smaller acceleration. He timed the rolls by weighing water that flowed from a tank during each run, and found distances growing as the square of the time, at every slope he tried. Published in *Two New Sciences* (1638), this was one of the first experiments designed to test a mathematical law. The story of dropping balls from the Leaning Tower of Pisa comes from a biography by his student Viviani and may never have happened.
:::

## Why mass does not matter

Galileo also gave a thought-experiment argument. Suppose heavy things fall faster. Tie a heavy stone to a light one. The light one should drag the heavy one back, so the pair falls slower than the heavy stone alone. But the pair is heavier than the heavy stone, so it should fall faster. The assumption contradicts itself, and the only consistent option is that all bodies fall at the same rate.

Newton later explained why: the gravitational force on an object is proportional to its mass, and so is the object's resistance to being accelerated. The two cancel. In 1971 the Apollo 15 astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a falcon feather on the airless Moon, and they landed together.

## Worked example

A stone is dropped from a 20 m cliff. How long does it take to hit the ground, and how fast is it then going?

Distance fallen: 20 = ½(9.8)t², so t² = 40/9.8 ≈ 4.08 and t ≈ 2.0 s.
Speed: from v² = 2gd, v = √(2 × 9.8 × 20) = √392 ≈ 19.8 m/s, about 71 km/h.

A ball thrown upward is the same motion run in both directions. It decelerates at g on the way up, stops for an instant at the top, and accelerates at g on the way down, returning to your hand at the speed it left.

::: note
In the absence of air resistance, every object near the Earth falls with the same acceleration, g ≈ 9.8 m/s², whatever its mass.
:::
