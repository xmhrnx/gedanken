By 1909 physicists knew atoms contained electrons, light and negative, and so must contain positive charge carrying most of the mass. Thomson's plum pudding spread that positive charge evenly through the whole atom. A student's experiment in Manchester showed it was instead packed into a point at the centre, and that the atom is almost entirely empty.

## Firing alpha particles at foil

Ernest Rutherford had shown that some radioactive elements emit **alpha particles**, which he later identified as helium atoms stripped of their electrons. They are heavy, about 7300 times an electron's mass, and fast, around 2 × 10⁷ m/s. That made them ideal probes: shoot them through a thin sheet of metal and see how the atoms deflect them.

In Thomson's model nothing in the atom could deflect them much. Electrons are far too light to push a projectile 7300 times heavier. The positive charge is spread over the whole atom, about 10⁻¹⁰ m across, so its electric field is weak everywhere. An alpha particle crossing thousands of atoms should be nudged a little by each one and emerge within a degree or two of its original path.

::: history 1909–1911 · Manchester
Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden, an undergraduate of twenty, counted alpha particles by watching for faint flashes of light on a zinc sulphide screen through a microscope, in a darkened room. Rutherford suggested Marsden check whether any alphas bounced back off a metal foil. Some did: roughly 1 in 8000 was turned through more than 90°. Rutherford later said it was "almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you." After a year and a half of thought he published his explanation in 1911.
:::

## A point of positive charge

To turn an alpha particle around, a single atom must exert a huge force on it. That needs a very strong field, which requires the positive charge to sit in a very small space. The field just outside a ball of charge Q and radius R is kQ/R², so shrinking the ball 10,000 times makes the field at its surface 100 million times stronger.

Rutherford proposed that all the positive charge and nearly all the mass are concentrated in a tiny **nucleus**, with the electrons far outside. Most alpha particles pass through the empty space and barely notice. The rare one that heads almost straight at a nucleus is flung back by the electric repulsion.

How close can it get? Follow an alpha particle of kinetic energy K aimed straight at a gold nucleus.

::: steps Distance of closest approach
$ K = \frac{k(2e)(Ze)}{d}
At the closest point the alpha particle has stopped momentarily. All its kinetic energy has become electric potential energy, kq₁q₂/d, for charges 2e (alpha) and Ze (nucleus) a distance d apart. The heavy gold nucleus barely recoils, so we ignore its motion.
---
$ d = \frac{2kZe^2}{K}
Solve for d.
---
$ d = \frac{2 \times 79 \times 1.44\,\text{MeV fm}}{7.7\,\text{MeV}} \approx 30\,\text{fm} = 3 \times 10^{-14}\,\text{m}
For gold Z = 79. The combination ke² is 1.44 MeV fm, where 1 fm (femtometre) is 10⁻¹⁵ m. The alpha energy is about 7.7 MeV.
:::

The alpha particles still obeyed Coulomb's law at that distance, so the nucleus must be smaller still: at most about a three-thousandth of the atom's width. Later experiments with faster particles put the gold nucleus at about 7 fm in radius, some 20,000 times smaller than the atom. If the nucleus were a marble 1 cm across, the atom would be about 200 m across, and nearly empty.

## The scattering law

Rutherford did more than tell a story. Treating the alpha particle as following a hyperbola around a point charge, he calculated how many should land at each angle θ:

::: equation Rutherford's scattering formula
N(\theta) \propto \frac{Z^2}{K^2\,\sin^4(\theta/2)}
:::

The prediction is detailed: it says how the count falls with angle, how it grows with the nuclear charge, and how it drops with the alpha energy. In 1913 Geiger and Marsden tested all three. They measured angles from 5° to 150°, over which the predicted count changes by a factor of about 250,000, and found agreement throughout. The atom had a nucleus.

The same experiments gave a rough measure of the nuclear charge, about half the atomic weight in units of e. Henry Moseley's X-ray measurements in 1913 then fixed it exactly: the nuclear charge Z is the element's place in the periodic table.

## A new problem

Rutherford's atom raised a question his physics could not answer. Electrons held at a distance by electric attraction must orbit the nucleus, and an accelerating charge radiates. Classical electromagnetism predicts that an orbiting electron would lose energy and spiral into the nucleus in about 10⁻¹¹ s. Atoms are plainly stable. Niels Bohr, who worked with Rutherford in 1912, took this as a sign that classical physics fails inside atoms, and his quantised orbits of 1913 were the first answer.

The nuclear atom. Not to scale: at this size the real nucleus would be invisible. Electrons do not follow orbits either, as later lessons show; this is the picture as of 1911.
:::

::: note
A few alpha particles bounce straight back from gold foil, so the atom's positive charge must be concentrated in a nucleus at most a few times 10⁻¹⁴ m across. The rest of the atom, about 10⁻¹⁰ m, is nearly empty space occupied by electrons.
:::
