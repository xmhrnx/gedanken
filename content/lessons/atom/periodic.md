Chemists had arranged the elements in a table by 1869, sorted by weight and grouped by behaviour, without any idea why the pattern existed. Lithium, sodium and potassium, at atomic numbers 3, 11 and 19, react almost identically; so do the unreactive gases at 2, 10, 18 and 36. Quantum mechanics explains the table with the orbitals of the previous lesson and one extra rule.

::: history 1869 · St Petersburg
Dmitri Mendeleev ordered the known elements by atomic weight and laid them out so that elements with similar chemistry fell in the same column. Where the pattern demanded it, he left gaps and predicted the missing elements' properties. "Eka-aluminium" turned up as gallium in 1875, eka-boron as scandium in 1879 and eka-silicon as germanium in 1886, each close to his predictions. In a few places he put heavier elements before lighter ones to keep the columns right. In 1913 Henry Moseley showed why: the true order is by nuclear charge, the atomic number Z, not by weight.
:::

## The exclusion principle

A neutral atom of element Z has Z electrons. Left to themselves, you might expect them all to drop into the lowest orbital, 1s. Then every atom would look much like hydrogen or helium, and chemistry would change smoothly from element to element. It does not, and the reason is a rule found by Wolfgang Pauli.

::: note
Pauli exclusion principle: no two electrons in an atom can have the same four quantum numbers n, l, m_l and m_s. Each orbital holds at most two electrons, one spin up and one spin down. This single rule builds the structure of the periodic table.
:::

::: history 1925 · Hamburg
Pauli was trying to make sense of how atomic spectra split in magnetic fields. Building on a 1924 paper by Edmund Stoner on how electrons fill shells, he concluded that electrons needed a fourth, "two-valued" quantum number and that no two could share all four. He stated it as a rule that fit the data, without a deeper reason. The spin that supplied the fourth number was proposed later that year. The principle is now known to hold for all particles with half-integer spin, and it is what holds up white dwarf stars against their own gravity.
:::

## Filling the shells

Add electrons one at a time, each into the lowest-energy orbital still available. The number each subshell can hold follows from counting quantum numbers.

::: steps Capacity of subshells and periods
$ m_l = -l, \ldots, +l \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2l + 1 \text{ orbitals}
A subshell with angular momentum number l has 2l + 1 orbitals, one for each orientation.
---
$ 2(2l + 1) = 2,\; 6,\; 10,\; 14 \quad \text{for s, p, d, f}
By the exclusion principle each orbital takes two electrons of opposite spin.
---
$ 1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d < 4p < 5s < 4d < 5p < 6s < 4f < 5d < 6p
In atoms with many electrons, inner electrons screen the nucleus. Orbitals with low l reach in close to the nucleus and are held more tightly, so 4s fills before 3d, and 6s before 4f.
---
$ 2,\; 2 + 6 = 8,\; 2 + 6 = 8,\; 2 + 10 + 6 = 18,\; 18,\; 2 + 14 + 10 + 6 = 32
Each row of the table starts with a new s subshell and ends when the p subshell is full. Rows 1 to 6 hold 1s; 2s 2p; 3s 3p; 4s 3d 4p; 5s 4d 5p; 6s 4f 5d 6p.
:::

These are the lengths of the rows: 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32. The order of filling in step 3 is a good general guide, not a law; a few elements, such as chromium and copper, deviate by one electron.

## Chemistry from shells

An atom's chemistry depends mainly on its outermost electrons, the **valence electrons**, because they are the ones least tightly held and the ones other atoms meet.

- **Noble gases** (helium, neon, argon, …) end a row with every subshell full. Removing an electron is hard: 21.6 eV for neon, 24.6 eV for helium. They barely react.
- **Alkali metals** (lithium, sodium, potassium, …) start a row with one electron in a new s orbital, outside a closed shell and screened from most of the nuclear charge. Removing it takes only 5.1 eV for sodium. They give that electron away readily and react violently.
- **Halogens** (fluorine, chlorine, …) are one electron short of a closed shell and take an electron eagerly. Sodium and chlorine together make Na⁺ Cl⁻, table salt.
- **Transition metals** fill d orbitals, which sit inside the outer s electrons. Since the outer electrons barely change from one to the next, ten elements in a row look chemically alike.

Columns therefore group elements with the same arrangement of valence electrons: sodium (…3s¹) sits under lithium (…2s¹) and above potassium (…4s¹). Mendeleev's columns turn out to be counts of electrons outside closed shells.

## Electron configurations

The filling is written as a list of occupied subshells with the number of electrons as a superscript. Neon (Z = 10) is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶: two closed shells. Sodium (Z = 11) is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹: neon's core plus one. Iron (Z = 26) is [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶, an argon core plus eight more.
