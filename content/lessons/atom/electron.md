For most of the nineteenth century the atom was the smallest thing there was: the indivisible unit of each chemical element. In 1897 J. J. Thomson showed that atoms contain something far smaller, the same in every element. He found it by weighing a beam of glowing rays against magnetic and electric fields.

## Cathode rays

Seal two metal plates in a glass tube, pump out most of the air, and put a high voltage across them. A glow appears on the glass opposite the negative plate, the **cathode**. Something travels from the cathode in straight lines: an object in its path casts a sharp shadow. These were called **cathode rays**, and through the 1880s and 1890s nobody agreed on what they were.

Many German physicists thought they were waves in the ether, like light. In 1883 Heinrich Hertz passed them between charged plates and saw no deflection, which seemed to settle it. Most British physicists thought they were charged particles, because a magnet bends them. In 1895 Jean Perrin in Paris caught the rays in a metal cup and found that it collected negative charge.

::: history 1897 · Cambridge, England
J. J. Thomson, head of the Cavendish Laboratory, repeated Hertz's experiment with a better vacuum. Hertz's tube had held enough gas that it became conducting and shielded the rays from the plates. With less gas, the rays bent toward the positive plate: they were negatively charged. Thomson then balanced electric against magnetic deflection to measure their charge-to-mass ratio. He called the particles "corpuscles"; the name **electron**, coined by George Johnstone Stoney in 1891 for a unit of charge, soon took over. Thomson received the 1906 Nobel Prize.
:::

## Measuring e/m

A particle of charge e moving at speed v through an electric field E feels a force eE. In a magnetic field B at right angles to its motion it feels a force evB, at right angles to both. Arrange the two fields so the forces point opposite ways. The textbook version of Thomson's method then takes two steps.

::: steps Charge-to-mass ratio from crossed fields
$ eE = evB \quad\Rightarrow\quad v = \frac{E}{B}
Adjust the fields until the beam goes straight. The two forces then cancel, and the charge drops out, leaving the speed.
---
$ evB = \frac{mv^2}{r}
Switch off the electric field. The magnetic force, always at right angles to the motion, bends the beam into a circular arc of radius r. It supplies the centripetal force mv²/r.
---
$ \frac{e}{m} = \frac{v}{Br}
Cancel one v and rearrange.
---
$ \frac{e}{m} = \frac{E}{B^2 r}
Substitute v = E/B from the first step. Every quantity on the right can be measured in the lab.
:::

Thomson's beams moved at around a tenth of the speed of light, and his values of e/m were rough. The modern value is:

::: equation The electron's charge-to-mass ratio
\frac{e}{m_e} = 1.76 \times 10^{11}\,\text{C/kg}
:::

## What the number meant

Two features of the result mattered. First, e/m came out the same whatever metal the cathode was made of and whatever gas was in the tube. The particle was a common ingredient of all matter.

Second, it was enormous. The hydrogen ion, the lightest charged thing known from electrolysis, has a charge-to-mass ratio about 1800 times smaller. If the charges were similar, the corpuscle had to be about two thousand times lighter than a hydrogen atom. Thomson's guess that the charges were equal was confirmed when Robert Millikan measured the charge directly in his oil-drop experiments of 1909 to 1913: e ≈ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, giving m_e ≈ 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, 1/1836 of the proton's mass.

The year before, Pieter Zeeman had seen spectral lines of sodium broaden in a magnetic field, and Hendrik Lorentz had explained it by charged particles vibrating inside atoms. Their e/m was of the same size. The particles in the cathode ray tube and the ones inside atoms that emit light were the same.

## A divisible atom

If atoms contain light negative electrons and are neutral overall, they must also contain positive charge, carrying almost all the mass. In 1904 Thomson proposed the "plum pudding" model: electrons embedded in a uniform sphere of positive charge, arranged in rings that might explain the periodic table. It was the first attempt to build an atom out of parts. It lasted seven years, until Rutherford's alpha particles showed the positive charge was not spread out at all.

::: note
Cathode rays are streams of electrons. Balancing electric and magnetic forces gives their charge-to-mass ratio, about 2000 times that of hydrogen ions and the same from every material: atoms have parts, and the electron is common to all of them.
:::
