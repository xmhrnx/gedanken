At the start of the 1890s, most chemists and physicists who believed in atoms at all thought of them as the end of the line. An atom of gold was the smallest piece of gold there could be, and it had no smaller parts. By the end of the decade that picture was gone. The experiment that broke it was not aimed at atoms at all. It was an attempt to settle an argument about a strange glow in a glass tube, and its central idea is a clever piece of reasoning: if you cannot measure two unknown quantities directly, arrange for them to cancel.

## A glow nobody could explain

Take a glass tube with a metal plate sealed into each end, pump out as much of the air as you can, and connect the plates to a high voltage. The negative plate is called the **cathode**. When the air pressure is low enough, the glass at the far end of the tube, opposite the cathode, begins to glow a pale green.

Something is clearly leaving the cathode and striking the glass. Put a small metal cross in the way and a sharp shadow of the cross appears in the glow, so whatever it is travels in straight lines, like light. These were named **cathode rays**. For twenty years, nobody could agree on what they were.

One camp, mostly in Germany, thought cathode rays were a disturbance in the ether, the medium that light was then thought to travel through. That would make them a kind of wave, a relative of light. The other camp, mostly in Britain, thought they were a stream of tiny charged particles. Each side had evidence. A magnet held near the tube bends the rays, which is what moving charges do and light does not. But in 1883 Heinrich Hertz passed the rays between two metal plates, one charged positive and one negative, and the rays went straight through without bending. Charged particles should have been pulled toward one plate. To many people, that settled it: the rays were waves.

Then, in 1895, Jean Perrin in Paris let the rays fall into a closed metal cup connected to an instrument that measures charge, and the cup filled up with negative charge. That is hard to explain if the rays are waves. The question was open again, and it needed a sharper experiment.

::: history 1897 · Cambridge, England
J. J. Thomson ran the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge. He suspected Hertz's null result had a mundane cause: Hertz's tube still held enough gas that the gas itself conducted electricity, and the charges in the gas spread out and cancelled the field between the plates. With a better vacuum pump, Thomson repeated the experiment, and this time the rays bent clearly toward the positive plate. They carried negative charge. He went on to measure how strongly they responded to electric and magnetic forces, which is the argument of this lesson. He called the particles "corpuscles". The name **electron** had been coined a few years earlier by George Johnstone Stoney for the smallest unit of electric charge, and it soon stuck to the particle itself. Thomson received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1906.
:::

## Why the measurement is hard

Suppose the rays are particles, each with some charge e and some mass m, all moving at some speed v. You would like to know e and m. You cannot weigh one of these particles, and you cannot measure the charge of a single one: all you can see is a glowing spot where the beam hits the glass, and you can watch how far that spot moves when you apply a field.

The trouble is that the amount a beam bends depends on several unknowns at once. A stronger push (more charge) bends it more. More inertia (more mass) bends it less. And a faster particle spends less time in the field, so it bends less too. One measurement of how far the spot moves gives you one equation, but you have three unknowns: e, m and v.

Thomson's way through was to use two different kinds of force, one electric and one magnetic, and set them against each other.

## Two forces on a moving charge

A charged particle in an **electric field** E feels a force of size eE, in the direction of the field (for a positive charge) or against it (for a negative one). This force does not care how fast the particle is moving.

A charged particle moving through a **magnetic field** B feels a force too, but of a stranger kind. Its size is evB when the motion is at right angles to the field, and its direction is at right angles to both the motion and the field. Two things about this force matter here. It grows with speed: a particle at rest feels nothing, and one moving twice as fast feels twice the force. And because it always pushes sideways to the motion, it can change the particle's direction but never its speed.

Now point the two fields so that their forces on the beam act in opposite directions: the electric force pushes the beam up, say, and the magnetic force pushes it down. Then you can adjust the field strengths until the two forces exactly balance.

## The measurement, step by step

::: steps Charge-to-mass ratio from crossed fields
$ eE = evB
Turn on both fields and adjust them until the glowing spot sits exactly where it was with no fields at all. The beam is going straight, so the net sideways force on each particle is zero: the electric push eE is exactly cancelled by the magnetic push evB.
---
$ v = \frac{E}{B}
Divide both sides by eB. The charge e appears on both sides and cancels, which is the whole point of the arrangement. You are left with the speed of the particles, written entirely in terms of the two field strengths, and you set those yourself, so you know them. One of the three unknowns is now known.
---
$ evB = \frac{mv^2}{r}
Now switch the electric field off and keep the magnetic one. The magnetic force still pushes sideways, at right angles to the motion, and keeps doing so as the beam turns. A force that is always at right angles to the motion bends a path into a circle, just as the tension in a string keeps a whirled stone moving in a circle. The force needed to hold anything of mass m moving at speed v on a circle of radius r is mv²/r. Here the magnetic force supplies it. The radius r can be worked out from how far the spot moves.
---
$ \frac{e}{m} = \frac{v}{Br}
Divide both sides by mvB. One factor of v cancels, and the charge and mass end up together on one side. This is as far as the method can go: it cannot separate e from m, only find their ratio. But a ratio is still a measurement of something real, as the next section shows.
---
$ \frac{e}{m} = \frac{E}{B^2 r}
Finally, replace v with E/B from the second step. Every quantity on the right is now something set or measured in the laboratory: the two field strengths and the radius of the curve. So the charge-to-mass ratio of the particles comes out as a number.
:::

Thomson's own numbers were rough, partly because his beam did not move in a clean circle and his fields were not perfectly uniform. Modern measurements give:

::: equation The electron's charge-to-mass ratio
\frac{e}{m_e} = 1.76 \times 10^{11}\,\text{C/kg}
:::

## What the number told him

On its own, e/m is just a ratio. What made it important were two comparisons.

The first was across materials. Thomson changed the metal of the cathode and the gas left in the tube, and e/m came out the same every time. If cathode rays were bits broken off particular atoms, you would expect copper to give one ratio and aluminium another. A single value for everything meant the particle was the same in all of them: something every kind of atom contains.

The second comparison was with the hydrogen ion, the lightest charged object then known. From electrolysis, the process of passing current through solutions, chemists already knew the charge-to-mass ratio of a hydrogen ion. Thomson's particles had a ratio about 1,800 times larger. Either they carried an enormous charge, or they were extremely light. Thomson bet that their charge was the same size as the hydrogen ion's, which would make their mass about 1,800 times smaller than a hydrogen atom's.

The bet was confirmed when Robert Millikan measured the charge on its own in his oil-drop experiments between 1909 and 1913, finding e ≈ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ coulombs. Combined with e/m, that gives the electron's mass: about 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, roughly 1/1836 of the mass of a proton.

There was one more piece of evidence, from a different direction. In 1896 Pieter Zeeman noticed that the spectral lines of sodium spread apart when the light came from a flame inside a strong magnetic field. Hendrik Lorentz explained this by supposing that light is emitted by small charged particles vibrating inside atoms, and from the size of the effect he estimated their charge-to-mass ratio. It matched Thomson's. The particles flying down a cathode ray tube and the particles inside atoms that produce light were the same thing.

## The atom has parts

If every atom contains electrons, and atoms are electrically neutral overall, then each atom must also contain positive charge to balance them. And since electrons are so light, nearly all of an atom's mass must belong to that positive part.

In 1904 Thomson proposed a picture of how this might look: a sphere of evenly spread positive charge, with electrons embedded in it, perhaps arranged in rings. It became known as the "plum pudding" model. It was the first serious attempt to build an atom out of smaller pieces, and that, more than its details, is what made it important. It lasted about seven years, until Ernest Rutherford fired alpha particles at gold foil and found that the positive charge is not spread out at all. That is the next lesson.

::: note
Cathode rays are streams of electrons. Setting an electric force against a magnetic one cancels the unknown charge and reveals the beam's speed; bending the beam in the magnetic field alone then gives the charge-to-mass ratio. It is about 1,800 times that of a hydrogen ion and the same for every material, so atoms have parts, and the electron is a part of all of them.
:::
