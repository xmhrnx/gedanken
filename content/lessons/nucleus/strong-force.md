Two protons 1 fm apart (1 fm, a femtometre, is 10⁻¹⁵ m) repel each other with a force of about 230 newtons, roughly the weight of a 23 kg mass, acting on a particle that weighs 10⁻²⁷ kg. Yet uranium holds 92 protons in a sphere 15 fm across. Gravity is far too weak to help, by a factor of about 10³⁶. A third force must be at work, stronger than electricity at short distances.

## What the force must be like

Experiments in the 1930s and after pinned down its main properties.

- **Strong.** At nuclear distances it overwhelms the electrical repulsion between protons, roughly a hundredfold.
- **Short-ranged.** It falls to almost nothing beyond about 2 fm. Rutherford's alpha particles scattered from gold exactly as electricity alone predicts, because they never came close enough to feel anything else. The force also has no effect on atomic structure at all.
- **Charge-blind.** Proton–proton, neutron–neutron and proton–neutron forces are nearly the same. To the strong force, the proton and neutron look like two versions of one particle.
- **Repulsive at very short range.** Below about 0.5 fm it turns sharply repulsive, which keeps nucleons from collapsing into each other and explains why nuclear matter has a fixed density.

The short range has a direct consequence: each nucleon attracts only its nearest neighbours. Adding nucleons to a big nucleus does not make each one more tightly held. The electrical repulsion, by contrast, reaches across the whole nucleus. This contest between a short-range attraction and a long-range repulsion shapes almost everything in nuclear physics, including why very heavy nuclei are unstable.

The force is also only just strong enough. The **deuteron**, one proton and one neutron, is bound by a mere 2.2 MeV, and no bound state of two protons or two neutrons exists at all.

## Yukawa's meson

::: history 1934 · Osaka, Japan
Hideki Yukawa, a 27-year-old lecturer, asked what would make a force short-ranged. Electricity, he knew, could be described as the exchange of photons, which have no mass and give a force of unlimited range. He proposed that nucleons exchange a new particle with mass, and that the mass sets the range. His paper appeared in early 1935 and was largely ignored outside Japan for two years.
:::

The argument is an estimate based on the uncertainty principle, and it predicts the mass of the new particle from the range of the force.

::: steps Estimating the mass of the exchanged particle
$ \Delta E\,\Delta t \sim \hbar
A nucleon can emit a particle of rest energy mc² without breaking energy conservation in any measurable way, provided it lasts no longer than about ħ/ΔE.
---
$ \Delta t \sim \frac{\hbar}{mc^2}
The energy borrowed is the particle's rest energy, ΔE = mc².
---
$ r \sim c\,\Delta t \sim \frac{\hbar}{mc}
Even moving at nearly the speed of light, the particle can travel only about cΔt before it must be reabsorbed. That distance is the range of the force.
---
$ mc^2 \sim \frac{\hbar c}{r} = \frac{197\,\text{MeV fm}}{1.4\,\text{fm}} \approx 140\,\text{MeV}
Rearrange and use ħc = 197 MeV·fm. A range of about 1.4 fm needs a particle about 270 times the electron's mass, between the electron and the proton. Yukawa called it a **meson**, from the Greek for "middle".
:::

The picture of particles "borrowing" energy is a heuristic; the real calculation uses quantum field theory, but it gives the same relation between range and mass. Yukawa's force falls off faster than any inverse-square law:

::: equation Yukawa potential
V(r) = -g^2 \frac{e^{-r/a}}{r}, \quad a = \frac{\hbar}{mc}
:::

At short distances it looks like 1/r, like electricity. Beyond the range a, the exponential factor kills it.

::: history 1936–1947 · Pasadena and Bristol
In 1936 Carl Anderson and Seth Neddermeyer found a cosmic-ray particle of about the right mass, and for a decade it was taken to be Yukawa's meson. But it barely interacted with nuclei, which a carrier of the strong force must do. In 1947 Cecil Powell, César Lattes and Giuseppe Occhialini, exposing photographic plates on mountaintops, found the real one: the **pion**, with a mass of about 140 MeV/c². The earlier particle, now called the muon, is a heavy cousin of the electron. Yukawa received the Nobel Prize in 1949, Powell in 1950.
:::

::: note
A force carried by a particle of mass m has a range of about ħ/(mc). The strong force reaches about a femtometre, so its carrier between nucleons weighs about 140 MeV/c²: the pion.
:::

## The modern view

Since the 1970s, protons and neutrons have been understood as made of quarks, held by a deeper force carried by massless gluons. The force between nucleons is a residue of that deeper force, rather as the forces between neutral molecules are a residue of electricity. Pion exchange still describes the longer-range part of the force between nucleons well; the short-range repulsion and other details need heavier mesons and, ultimately, quarks.
