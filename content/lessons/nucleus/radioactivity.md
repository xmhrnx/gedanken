Radioactivity was the first sign that atoms are not permanent. It was found by accident, explained over a decade by a handful of physicists and chemists, and it gave them the probes that later revealed the nucleus itself.

## Becquerel's plates

::: history February–March 1896 · Paris
Weeks after Wilhelm Röntgen announced X-rays, Henri Becquerel wondered whether phosphorescent minerals, which glow after exposure to sunlight, also emitted them. He laid uranium salts on photographic plates wrapped in black paper, set them in the sun, and found the plates fogged. Then a spell of cloudy weather intervened, and he left a prepared plate in a drawer. Developing it anyway on 1 March, he found it fogged just as strongly. Sunlight had nothing to do with it: the uranium emitted penetrating radiation on its own, continuously, with no outside source of energy.
:::

Becquerel showed that the rays also made air conduct electricity, and that the effect depended only on the amount of uranium present, not on its chemical form. Interest then faded; X-rays seemed far more exciting.

## The Curies

Marie Skłodowska Curie, a Polish-born doctoral student in Paris, took up the uranium rays in late 1897. Instead of photographic plates, she measured the faint electric current the rays produced in air, using a sensitive electrometer built by her husband Pierre and his brother Jacques. This turned a qualitative effect into a number.

She found that thorium was also active, and that the activity was a property of the atoms themselves, unaffected by heating, dissolving or combining them. She named the phenomenon **radioactivity**. The crucial clue came from pitchblende, a uranium ore: it was several times more active than its uranium content could explain.

::: history 1898–1902 · Paris
Marie and Pierre Curie concluded that pitchblende must contain unknown elements far more radioactive than uranium. Grinding and chemically separating tonnes of ore residue in a leaky shed, they announced **polonium**, named after Poland, in July 1898 and **radium** in December. Isolating a tenth of a gram of radium chloride took until 1902. Becquerel and the Curies shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics; Marie Curie received the 1911 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Neither understood the danger: her notebooks are still too radioactive to handle without protection.
:::

## Alpha, beta, gamma

In 1899 Ernest Rutherford, then at McGill University in Montreal, found that uranium's radiation had two components: one stopped by a sheet of paper, which he called **alpha**, and one that passed through thin metal, **beta**. In 1900 Paul Villard found a third, far more penetrating, later named **gamma**. Magnetic fields bent alpha and beta rays in opposite directions and left gamma rays undeflected, so alphas are positive, betas negative and gammas neutral.

| Radiation | What it is | Stopped by |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha (α) | helium-4 nucleus, 2 protons + 2 neutrons | paper, or a few cm of air |
| Beta (β) | electron | a few mm of aluminium |
| Gamma (γ) | high-energy photon | several cm of lead (reduced, never fully stopped) |

The identities took a decade to establish. Becquerel measured the charge-to-mass ratio of beta rays in 1900 and found it matched the electron. In 1908 Rutherford and Thomas Royds collected alpha particles in a sealed glass tube and saw the spectrum of helium appear. Gamma rays were shown in 1914 to diffract from crystals, like X-rays, but with shorter wavelength.

Each kind of decay changes the nucleus in a definite way.

::: equation Alpha decay of uranium-238
{}^{238}_{92}\mathrm{U} \to {}^{234}_{90}\mathrm{Th} + {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}
:::

The nucleus loses two protons and two neutrons: the mass number drops by 4 and the element moves two places down the periodic table.

::: equation Beta decay of carbon-14
{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C} \to {}^{14}_{7}\mathrm{N} + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e
:::

Inside the nucleus, a neutron turns into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino. The mass number is unchanged and the element moves one place up. The antineutrino was proposed by Wolfgang Pauli in 1930, because beta electrons came out with a spread of energies, as if some energy were going missing; it was detected in 1956. Gamma decay changes neither Z nor A: a nucleus left in an excited state by an earlier decay drops to a lower energy, as an atom emits light, but with photons about a million times more energetic.

::: note
Radioactivity is a nucleus changing itself. Alpha decay removes a helium nucleus, beta decay turns a neutron into a proton, and gamma decay sheds excess energy. In the first two, one element becomes another.
:::

## Transmutation

That last point was the shock. In 1902 Rutherford and the chemist Frederick Soddy showed that thorium steadily produced a chemically different radioactive substance. Radioactivity, they concluded, is the spontaneous transformation of one element into another, the alchemists' transmutation happening on its own. The energy involved was enormous: a gram of radium releases about 100 calories of heat per hour, indefinitely as far as anyone could then tell.

Why a nucleus decays at one moment rather than another has no answer in classical physics. Each nucleus has a fixed probability of decaying per second, unaffected by its age, temperature or chemistry. The next lesson shows what that implies for a large sample.
