For most of the nineteenth century, an atom was thought to be permanent. Chemistry could combine atoms and separate them, but an atom of uranium would remain uranium forever. Radioactivity was the first sign that this was wrong: some atoms change on their own, turning into atoms of other elements and giving off energy as they do. It was found by accident in 1896, explained over the following decade by a small group of physicists and chemists, and it handed them the tools, fast particles flung out of atoms, that soon revealed the nucleus itself. This lesson follows how the phenomenon was discovered, what the radiations turned out to be, and what each kind of decay does to a nucleus.

## A fogged plate in a drawer

::: history February–March 1896 · Paris
In January 1896 the whole scientific world was talking about Wilhelm Röntgen's X-rays, a penetrating radiation that could photograph the bones inside a living hand. Röntgen's rays came from the glowing spot where a beam struck the glass of a vacuum tube, and Henri Becquerel, an expert on phosphorescence, wondered whether the two were connected. Phosphorescent minerals glow for a while after being exposed to sunlight. Perhaps, he reasoned, they also give off X-rays while they glow. To test this he wrapped photographic plates in thick black paper, which no sunlight could pass, laid crystals of a uranium salt on top, and set them out in the sun. When he developed the plates, he found dark silhouettes of the crystals. It seemed to confirm his idea: sunlight made the uranium glow, and the glow included something that passed through paper. Then a spell of cloudy weather stopped the experiments, and he put a prepared plate, with its uranium, away in a drawer. On 1 March he developed it anyway, perhaps expecting a faint image at most. It was fogged just as strongly as the ones left in the sun. Sunlight had nothing to do with it. The uranium was giving off penetrating radiation on its own, continuously, without any outside source of energy.
:::

That last point was the deeply strange one. Phosphorescence is easy to understand: a material stores energy from light and gives it back later. Becquerel's uranium had stored nothing, yet kept radiating day after day. He went on to show that the radiation made the air around it conduct electricity, by knocking electrons off air molecules, and that the strength of the effect depended only on how much uranium was present, not on which uranium compound he used. Then interest faded. X-rays had obvious and dramatic uses, and the weak rays from uranium seemed a curiosity by comparison.

## Measuring instead of looking

The person who turned the curiosity into a science was Marie Skłodowska Curie, a Polish-born physicist who began her doctoral research in Paris in late 1897 and chose the uranium rays as her subject, partly because so little had been done on them. Her key decision was about method. Photographic plates give a picture, but it is hard to say exactly how dark a fogged plate is. Becquerel had found that the rays make air conduct, so Curie measured that instead: she placed a sample between two metal plates and measured the faint electric current that flowed through the air between them. To measure such tiny currents she used a sensitive electrometer devised by her husband Pierre and his brother Jacques. This turned "the plate is fogged" into a number that could be compared from one sample to the next.

With that tool she tested every element and compound she could obtain. She found that thorium was also active. More importantly, she found that the activity of a sample depended only on how much uranium or thorium it contained. Heating it, dissolving it, or combining it with other elements made no difference. That meant the radiation could not come from the way atoms were bonded together, since chemistry changes bonds; it had to come from inside the atoms themselves. She gave the phenomenon its name, **radioactivity**.

Then she found the clue that led further. Pitchblende, a dark uranium ore, was several times more active than its uranium content could account for. Since activity tracked the amount of radioactive element, the excess had to come from something else in the ore, an element not yet known, present in amounts too small for chemists to have noticed, and far more radioactive than uranium.

::: history 1898–1902 · Paris
Pierre Curie set aside his own research to join the search. The method was laborious: dissolve the ore, separate it chemically into fractions, measure the activity of each, keep the most active and separate it again. Each step concentrated the unknown substance. In July 1898 they announced a new element, which they named **polonium** after Marie's occupied homeland, and in December a second, **radium**, after the Latin for "ray". Chemists wanted to see the new elements in quantity before they would believe in them, and the Curies spent the next years processing tonnes of ore residue in a leaky, poorly heated shed. By 1902 they had isolated about a tenth of a gram of radium chloride, enough to measure radium's atomic weight. Becquerel and the Curies shared the 1903 Nobel Prize in Physics, and Marie Curie received the 1911 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery of the two elements. None of them understood the danger at first. Becquerel was burned by a tube of radium he carried in his waistcoat pocket, and Marie's notebooks are still too radioactive to handle without protection.
:::

## Three kinds of ray

While the Curies hunted for new elements, others asked what the radiation actually was. In 1899 Ernest Rutherford, then at McGill University in Montreal, covered a uranium sample with successively thicker layers of aluminium foil and measured how much radiation got through. The result showed two distinct components. One was stopped almost completely by a very thin layer; he called it **alpha**. The other passed through much thicker foil; he called it **beta**. In 1900 Paul Villard in Paris found a third component, far more penetrating than either, which was later named **gamma**.

Magnetic fields sorted them further. A magnetic field bends the path of a moving charged particle, in a direction that depends on the sign of its charge, and does nothing to a neutral one. Beta rays bent strongly one way, alpha rays bent weakly the other way, and gamma rays went straight. So alphas carry positive charge, betas negative, and gammas none, and the weak bending of the alphas meant they were much heavier than the betas.

| Radiation | What it is | Stopped by |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha (α) | helium-4 nucleus, 2 protons + 2 neutrons | paper, or a few cm of air |
| Beta (β) | electron | a few mm of aluminium |
| Gamma (γ) | high-energy photon | several cm of lead (reduced, never fully stopped) |

The penetrating power follows from what each one is. An alpha particle is heavy, slow by comparison and doubly charged, so it tears electrons from every atom it passes and spends its energy within a few centimetres of air. A beta electron is light and singly charged, so it interacts less often and travels farther. A gamma ray has no charge at all and interacts only occasionally, so a thick shield reduces its intensity step by step but never cuts it to exactly zero.

Establishing these identities took about a decade. In 1900 Becquerel measured the charge-to-mass ratio of beta rays and found it matched J. J. Thomson's electrons. The alphas were harder. In 1908 Rutherford and Thomas Royds let alpha particles pass through a very thin glass wall into an evacuated tube, waited for them to collect, and then passed an electric spark through the gas that had built up. Its light showed the spectrum of helium: alpha particles, once they had slowed down and picked up electrons, were helium atoms. Gamma rays were shown in 1914 to reflect from crystals in the regular pattern that X-rays produce, which identified them as electromagnetic waves like X-rays and light, only of much shorter wavelength.

## What each decay does to a nucleus

Once the nucleus was understood as protons and neutrons, each kind of decay could be described as a definite change in the nucleus. The notation writes the mass number A, the total count of nucleons, at the upper left of the symbol, and the atomic number Z, the count of protons, at the lower left. In any decay both totals are conserved: the numbers on each side must add up.

::: equation Alpha decay of uranium-238
{}^{238}_{92}\mathrm{U} \to {}^{234}_{90}\mathrm{Th} + {}^{4}_{2}\mathrm{He}
:::

In alpha decay the nucleus throws out a tightly bound cluster of two protons and two neutrons. The mass number drops by 4, from 238 to 234, and the atomic number by 2, from 92 to 90. Since Z decides the element, uranium has become thorium, two places lower in the periodic table. Alpha decay is common in heavy nuclei because shedding protons relieves the electrical repulsion that strains them, and because the helium-4 cluster is so tightly bound that ejecting it releases energy.

::: equation Beta decay of carbon-14
{}^{14}_{6}\mathrm{C} \to {}^{14}_{7}\mathrm{N} + e^- + \bar{\nu}_e
:::

Beta decay is subtler. The nucleus does not contain electrons, as the previous lessons showed, so the electron must be created at the moment of decay. What happens is that one neutron inside the nucleus turns into a proton, emitting an electron and an antineutrino, written ν̄ₑ. The mass number stays the same, since a neutron has been exchanged for a proton, but Z rises by 1, and carbon becomes nitrogen, one place higher. Nuclei with too many neutrons for stability decay this way, edging toward a better balance.

The antineutrino has its own story. Early measurements showed that beta electrons come out with a spread of energies, from almost nothing up to a maximum, even though every decay of a given nucleus releases the same total energy. Energy seemed to be disappearing. In 1930 Wolfgang Pauli proposed, as what he called a desperate remedy, that an unseen neutral particle carries off the missing share. It interacts so weakly with matter that it was not detected until 1956.

Gamma decay changes neither Z nor A. A nucleus is often left in an excited state, with excess energy, after an alpha or beta decay. It drops to a lower energy state by emitting a photon, much as an atom emits light when an electron falls to a lower level, but with photons about a million times more energetic, because the energies involved in nuclei are about a million times larger.

::: note
Radioactivity is a nucleus changing itself. Alpha decay removes a helium-4 nucleus, beta decay turns a neutron into a proton, and gamma decay sheds excess energy as a photon. In alpha and beta decay the number of protons changes, so one element becomes another.
:::

## Transmutation

That last point was the true shock, and it was established before the nucleus was even known. In 1902 Rutherford and the chemist Frederick Soddy, working together at McGill, studied thorium and found that it steadily produced a separate radioactive substance with different chemical properties. They could remove it chemically, watch its activity fade, and watch fresh activity grow back in the thorium. The only consistent explanation was that thorium atoms were turning, one by one, into atoms of a different element. Soddy is said to have exclaimed that this was transmutation, and Rutherford warned him not to call it that, or they would be dismissed as alchemists. But that is what it was: the change of one element into another that alchemists had sought for centuries, happening spontaneously.

The energy involved was startling too. In 1903 Pierre Curie and Albert Laborde found that radium keeps itself warmer than its surroundings, releasing about 100 calories of heat per gram every hour, and as far as anyone could tell, it would keep doing so indefinitely. No chemical reaction could supply energy at that rate for that long. Here was the first hint of the enormous energy locked inside atoms, though the source, the binding energy of the nucleus, was still decades from being understood.

One more puzzle remained, and it went deeper than anyone realised at first. Why does a particular nucleus decay at one moment rather than another? Classical physics has no answer. Each radioactive nucleus has a fixed probability of decaying in any given second, unaffected by its age, its temperature or its chemical surroundings, and nothing determines which nucleus goes first. The next lesson shows how that individual randomness turns into a perfectly regular law for a large sample.
