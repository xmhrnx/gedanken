In 1911 Ernest Rutherford showed that an atom keeps its positive charge and almost all of its mass in a tiny central **nucleus**, about ten thousand times smaller across than the atom itself. That answered one question and opened another: what is the nucleus made of? It took physicists more than twenty years to find out, and the answer needed a particle nobody had yet seen. This lesson follows that search, and then uses its result to work out something remarkable about the nucleus: every nucleus, light or heavy, is packed to the same astonishing density.

Rutherford's planetary picture of 1911, shown as history: the electrons do not really follow orbits like these. What survives is the nucleus, a tiny, heavy, positive core at the centre.
:::

## A charge that did not match the mass

The simplest nucleus belongs to hydrogen, the lightest element. Around 1920 Rutherford gave it a name, the **proton**, from the Greek for "first", because it looked like the basic building block from which other nuclei might be made.

There was good reason to think so. Every nucleus carries a positive charge that is an exact whole number of proton charges. That number, called the **atomic number** Z, is what makes an element what it is. A neutral atom must hold Z electrons to balance the nuclear charge, and the electrons are what take part in chemistry, so Z fixes how the atom behaves. Hydrogen has Z = 1, helium 2, carbon 6, uranium 92.

But the masses did not fit. If a nucleus were just Z protons, it would weigh about Z times as much as a hydrogen nucleus. Instead, helium has a charge of 2 and a mass of about 4; uranium has a charge of 92 and a mass of about 238. Nuclei weigh roughly twice what their charge alone would suggest, and more than twice for heavy ones. Something in the nucleus adds mass without adding charge.

The obvious explanation used only the particles already known. Suppose the nucleus contains enough protons to account for its mass, and some electrons to cancel the surplus charge. Helium would then be four protons and two electrons: mass 4, charge 2. This **proton–electron model** was the standard picture through the 1920s, and it looked tidy. It failed for two reasons that only became sharp as quantum mechanics matured.

The first reason is the uncertainty principle, which says that the more tightly you confine a particle, the larger the spread in its momentum must be. A nucleus is only a few femtometres across (a femtometre, written fm, is 10⁻¹⁵ m). An electron squeezed into a region that small would have to carry so much momentum that its energy would be tens of millions of electronvolts, or MeV (an electronvolt is the energy an electron gains crossing one volt). Nothing known could hold an electron with that much energy in place; it should simply fly out.

The second reason came from **spin**, an intrinsic amount of rotation that every particle carries, measured in units of ħ, the reduced Planck constant. Protons and electrons both have spin ½. Spins combine in a simple way: an even number of spin-½ particles always adds up to a whole-number total, and an odd number always to a half-integer one. Nitrogen-14 has charge 7 and mass 14, so in the proton–electron model it would contain 14 protons and 7 electrons, which is 21 particles, an odd number. Its total spin would have to be a half-integer. Measurements of nitrogen's molecular spectra in 1929 showed that it is a whole number. The model gave the wrong answer for a property that cannot be fudged.

::: history 1920 · London
Rutherford had already guessed at a way out. In a lecture to the Royal Society he speculated that a proton and an electron might bind together very tightly into a single neutral particle. Having no charge, it would slip through matter almost unhindered and could enter nuclei easily. His assistant James Chadwick took the idea to heart and spent much of the following decade hunting for such a particle, without success. Something neutral is hard to find, because nearly every detector works by responding to charge.
:::

## How the neutron was found

The particle turned up first in other people's experiments, and they misread it. In 1930 Walther Bothe and Herbert Becker in Germany bombarded beryllium with alpha particles, the helium nuclei given off by radioactive elements, and found that it emitted a radiation that was neutral and unusually penetrating. The only neutral radiation then known was gamma rays, high-energy light, so that is what they assumed it was.

In January 1932 Irène Curie and Frédéric Joliot in Paris put a sheet of paraffin wax in the path of this radiation. Paraffin is rich in hydrogen, and the radiation knocked protons out of it at high speed, with energies of about 5 MeV. Curie and Joliot interpreted this as gamma rays bouncing protons out of the wax, an effect already known for electrons.

::: history February 1932 · Cambridge, England
Chadwick read the Paris report and saw at once that the numbers did not work. A gamma ray carries very little momentum for its energy, so it is poor at shoving a heavy particle such as a proton. To kick a proton up to 5 MeV, a gamma ray would need about 50 MeV, far more than the beryllium reaction could possibly supply. Chadwick repeated the experiment and aimed the radiation at other targets, including nitrogen, measuring how hard each kind of nucleus recoiled. The gamma-ray story predicted nitrogen recoils far smaller than the ones he saw. Then he tried a different assumption: a neutral particle with about the mass of a proton. Collisions between objects of similar mass transfer energy efficiently, like one billiard ball striking another, and with that one assumption the recoils of hydrogen and nitrogen both came out right. Within a month of the Paris paper he sent a letter to *Nature* titled "Possible Existence of a Neutron". He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1935.
:::

The **neutron** turned out not to be the tight proton–electron pair Rutherford had imagined. It is a particle in its own right, slightly heavier than the proton: its rest energy is 939.6 MeV against the proton's 938.3 MeV. That small difference matters. Because the neutron is heavier, it can decay into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino (a nearly massless neutral particle), and outside a nucleus it does so after about 15 minutes on average. Inside a stable nucleus it is protected from decaying, for reasons the lessons on binding energy and radioactivity will make clear.

Within months, Werner Heisenberg and others rebuilt the nucleus from protons and neutrons alone, together called **nucleons**. Both old problems vanished at once. No electron has to be confined in the nucleus, so the uncertainty principle raises no objection. And nitrogen-14 becomes 7 protons and 7 neutrons; since the neutron also has spin ½, that is 14 spin-½ particles, an even number, giving the whole-number spin that had been measured.

## Weighing a particle nobody could see

Chadwick's argument deserves to be followed in full, because it is a model of how to measure something you cannot detect directly. He could not see the neutrons. He could see only the nuclei they struck, which, being charged, left tracks and pulses in his detectors. And he did not know the neutrons' speed either. So, like Thomson with the electron, he arranged for the unknown he could not measure to cancel.

The tool is the physics of an elastic collision, one in which no energy is lost to heat or to breaking things apart. When a moving body of mass m and speed u strikes a body of mass M at rest head-on, conservation of momentum and conservation of kinetic energy together fix the speed of the recoiling target. Hitting different targets with the same stream of neutrons then gives two equations with the same unknown speed u, which can be divided out.

::: steps Chadwick's estimate of the neutron's mass
$ v = \frac{2m}{m+M}\,u
This is the recoil speed of a target of mass M, initially at rest, struck head-on by a particle of mass m moving at speed u, when both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved. A head-on hit gives the largest possible recoil, so it corresponds to the fastest recoils Chadwick recorded. Notice what happens when m equals M: the target leaves at the full speed u, which is why a billiard ball can stop dead after a straight hit.
---
$ \frac{v_p}{v_N} = \frac{m + 14}{m + 1}
Write the recoil formula twice, once for a hydrogen target (M = 1, in units where the proton has mass 1) and once for nitrogen (M = 14), and divide the first by the second. The unknown neutron speed u and the factor 2m appear in both and cancel. What remains links the ratio of two speeds Chadwick could measure to the one mass he wanted.
---
$ \frac{3.3 \times 10^7\,\text{m/s}}{4.7 \times 10^6\,\text{m/s}} \approx 7.0
These are Chadwick's measured maximum recoil speeds: about 3.3 × 10⁷ m/s for protons and 4.7 × 10⁶ m/s for nitrogen nuclei. Protons come off about seven times faster, because a light target is far easier to set moving than a heavy one.
---
$ m = \frac{14 - 7.0}{7.0 - 1} \approx 1.16
Set the ratio equal to 7.0, multiply both sides by (m + 1) to clear the fraction, and collect the terms in m on one side: 7m + 7 = m + 14, so 6m = 7. The neutron's mass comes out at about 1.16 proton masses.
:::

Chadwick himself quoted 1.15, and he was careful to say his recoil measurements could be off by about ten per cent. Within that uncertainty, the mass is the proton's. Modern values put the neutron about 0.14% heavier than the proton, far too small a difference for his method to see, but the essential conclusion was secure: the neutral radiation from beryllium is made of particles as heavy as protons, and gamma rays, which carry far too little momentum, could not have produced either recoil. With a mass close to 1, a neutron hitting a proton head-on hands over nearly all its energy, so the 5 MeV protons in Paris meant neutrons of at least 5 MeV, an energy the beryllium reaction could easily supply.


## Counting nucleons

With two building blocks, every nucleus can be described by two numbers: Z, the number of protons, and N, the number of neutrons. Their sum is the **mass number**, A = Z + N, which is the total count of nucleons and, because protons and neutrons weigh nearly the same, roughly the nucleus's mass in units of the proton mass. Carbon-12 is written ¹²C: it has 6 protons and 6 neutrons.

The neutron also explained a puzzle that chemists had lived with for two decades. In 1913 Frederick Soddy had named **isotopes**: forms of the same element with different masses. Chemically they were indistinguishable, yet they weighed differently, and nobody could say why. Now the answer was simple. Isotopes share the same Z but differ in N. Carbon-14 has the same 6 protons as carbon-12, and so the same 6 electrons and the same chemistry, but carries 8 neutrons instead of 6. The extra neutrons change the mass and nothing else that chemistry can see.

Counting this way also reveals a pattern. Light stable nuclei have about as many neutrons as protons: helium-4 has 2 and 2, oxygen-16 has 8 and 8, calcium-40 has 20 and 20. Heavier stable nuclei carry a growing surplus of neutrons. Iron-56 has 26 protons and 30 neutrons; lead-208 has 82 and 126, about 1.5 neutrons per proton. Not every combination survives. Of the thousands of combinations of Z and N that have been made in laboratories, only about 250 are stable, and they lie along a narrow band when plotted with N against Z. Why the band bends toward extra neutrons, and what happens to nuclei that stray from it, are questions for the lessons on binding energy and radioactivity; the short answer is that neutrons add attraction without adding electrical repulsion.

A word on notation, since it is easy to misread. The symbol for an element already fixes Z, so the full label ²³⁸₉₂U is usually shortened to ²³⁸U or uranium-238. The number written is always A, the total count of nucleons, not N. Uranium-238 and uranium-235 both have 92 protons; they differ by three neutrons, 146 against 143.


## How big is a nucleus?

Once nuclei could be counted, they could be measured. Scattering experiments, which fire fast particles at nuclei and record how they bounce off, show that nuclear sizes follow a strikingly simple rule.

::: equation Nuclear radius
R \approx r_0 A^{1/3}, \quad r_0 \approx 1.2\,\text{fm}
:::

The radius grows as the cube root of the mass number. That cube root is the interesting part. The volume of a sphere is proportional to R³, and cubing A^(1/3) gives back A. So the volume of a nucleus is proportional to the number of nucleons it contains. Each nucleon takes up the same amount of room, whatever nucleus it sits in, the way marbles packed into a bag fill a volume in proportion to how many there are. The analogy is loose, since nucleons are quantum objects without hard surfaces, but on the point that matters it is right: nucleons do not crowd closer together in big nuclei. Follow that idea through and it gives the density of nuclear matter.

::: steps The density of nuclear matter
$ \rho = \frac{A\,m_u}{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3}
Density is mass divided by volume. The mass of the nucleus is about A times the mass of one nucleon, which is close to one atomic mass unit, m_u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. Treating the nucleus as a sphere of radius R gives the volume in the denominator. This ignores the small mass lost to binding, which is under 1% and does not matter here.
---
$ \rho = \frac{A\,m_u}{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r_0^3 A}
Now put in the measured size. Cubing R = r₀A^(1/3) gives R³ = r₀³A, so the mass number A now appears in the denominator as well as the numerator. This is the step where the empirical radius rule does its work.
---
$ \rho = \frac{m_u}{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r_0^3}
The A on top and the A on the bottom cancel. What remains contains no reference to any particular nucleus, only the mass of a nucleon and the constant r₀. So every nucleus, from helium to uranium, has the same density, which is the marble picture expressed as an equation.
---
$ \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r_0^3 = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi \left(1.2 \times 10^{-15}\,\text{m}\right)^3 \approx 7.2 \times 10^{-45}\,\text{m}^3
Before dividing, work out the denominator: the volume each nucleon effectively occupies. Cubing 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m gives 1.73 × 10⁻⁴⁵ m³, and multiplying by 4π/3, about 4.19, gives roughly 7.2 × 10⁻⁴⁵ m³. It is a sphere about 2.4 fm across.
---
$ \rho \approx \frac{1.66 \times 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}}{7.2 \times 10^{-45}\,\text{m}^3} \approx 2 \times 10^{17}\,\text{kg/m}^3
Dividing the nucleon's mass by that volume gives the density. The exponents tell the story: a mass of order 10⁻²⁷ kg in a volume of order 10⁻⁴⁴ m³ means a density of order 10¹⁷ kg per cubic metre.
:::

That number deserves a moment. Water has a density of 1,000 kg/m³, and the densest metals reach about 22,000. Nuclear matter is about two hundred trillion times denser than water. A teaspoon of it, about 5 millilitres, would have a mass of about a billion tonnes. Ordinary matter seems solid only because atoms are almost entirely empty space, with electrons spread thinly around a nucleus that holds nearly all the mass in a minute fraction of the volume. Matter at roughly nuclear density does exist in bulk: a neutron star, the collapsed core left by some exploding stars, is a ball of matter at about this density some twenty kilometres across, held together by its own gravity rather than by nuclear forces.

::: note
The nucleus is built from two kinds of nucleon: Z protons, which carry the charge and so fix the element, and N neutrons, which add mass without charge. Because nucleons pack at a fixed density, a nucleus's volume is proportional to its mass number, and every nucleus has about the same density, roughly 2 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³.
:::

## Worked example: the nucleus Rutherford never touched

The radius rule lets us look back at the experiment that discovered the nucleus and ask how close the alpha particles actually came. Gold has A = 197, and the cube root of 197 is about 5.8, so its nucleus has a radius of about 1.2 × 5.8 ≈ 7.0 fm.

Rutherford's alpha particles had kinetic energies of about 7.7 MeV. An alpha particle fired straight at a nucleus slows as electrical repulsion pushes back on it, and it stops at the distance where all its kinetic energy has been turned into electrical potential energy. That potential energy, between charges Z₁e and Z₂e a distance d apart, is kZ₁Z₂e²/d, and a useful shortcut is that ke² ≈ 1.44 MeV·fm. With Z₁ = 2 for the alpha particle and Z₂ = 79 for gold, the closest approach is d ≈ 1.44 × 2 × 79 / 7.7 ≈ 30 fm.

So the alpha particles that bounced straight back never came within about four nuclear radii of the gold nucleus's edge region; they turned round well outside it. That is a happy accident of history. Rutherford's scattering formula assumes that only the electrical force acts, and it worked perfectly for gold because his alpha particles never got close enough to feel anything else. When later experimenters used lighter targets, with less charge to hold the alpha particles off, or faster alpha particles, the formula began to fail at large angles, and the distance at which it failed gave the first estimates of nuclear size. The departure from Rutherford's law was the first sign of the force that holds nuclei together.

A second comparison puts the nucleus in its setting. A gold atom is about 0.14 nm in radius, which is 140,000 fm. The nucleus is therefore about 20,000 times smaller across than the atom, and occupies less than a trillionth of its volume. If the gold atom were a concert hall, the nucleus would be a grain of rice in the middle, and that grain would hold 99.98% of the mass.

## Going deeper: why an electron cannot live in a nucleus

The first objection to the proton–electron model was stated in words earlier. With the uncertainty principle and one useful constant, it can be checked in numbers, and the numbers show how decisive it is.

::: steps Confining an electron to a nucleus
$ p \approx \frac{\hbar}{\Delta x}
The uncertainty principle says that a particle confined to a region of size Δx has a spread in momentum of at least about ħ/Δx, and its typical momentum cannot be smaller than that spread. This is a rough estimate, good to a factor of two or so, which is all the argument needs.
---
$ pc \approx \frac{\hbar c}{\Delta x} = \frac{197\,\text{MeV fm}}{5\,\text{fm}} \approx 40\,\text{MeV}
Multiplying by the speed of light turns momentum into an energy, and the product ħc has the convenient value 197 MeV·fm. Taking Δx as 5 fm, the diameter of a nucleus such as nitrogen, gives pc of about 40 MeV.
---
$ E \approx pc \approx 40\,\text{MeV} \gg m_e c^2 = 0.511\,\text{MeV}
An electron's rest energy is only half an MeV, so an electron with 40 MeV of momentum energy is moving at almost the speed of light and its total energy is essentially pc. The confinement alone would give it about eighty times its own rest energy.
---
$ \frac{k Z e^2}{r} \approx \frac{1.44\,\text{MeV fm} \times 7}{2.5\,\text{fm}} \approx 4\,\text{MeV}
Could the nucleus's charge hold such an electron? The electrical potential energy of an electron at the surface of a nitrogen nucleus, radius about 2.5 fm and charge 7e, is only about 4 MeV deep. A well 4 MeV deep cannot hold a particle with 40 MeV of energy.
:::

There was experimental support, too. Electrons emitted in beta decay, the one process in which nuclei give off electrons, come out with energies of at most a few MeV, not forty. That fits a picture in which the electron is created at the moment of decay, which is what Enrico Fermi proposed in 1933, rather than one in which it was sitting inside the nucleus all along. Protons and neutrons, about 1,800 times heavier, are a different matter: the same confinement gives them momentum energies of only a few MeV each, comparable to the binding energies that the lesson on binding energy will measure, so a nucleus of nucleons is a self-consistent object in a way that a nucleus containing electrons is not.

The radius rule hides a second subtlety. Nuclei do not have sharp edges. In the 1950s Robert Hofstadter at Stanford fired high-energy electrons at nuclei and mapped how their charge is spread. The density is nearly constant through the interior and then falls off over a skin about 2 to 3 fm thick, whatever the size of the nucleus. The radius R in the formula is best read as the distance at which the density has dropped to half its central value. For a small nucleus the skin is a large fraction of the whole, which is why the rule works less well at the light end.

## Common misconceptions

The most natural mistake is to think that the neutron is a proton and an electron stuck together, because that is what it decays into. Rutherford thought so too. But decay products are not ingredients. The neutron's spin is ½, while a proton–electron pair would have spin 0 or 1, and the electron, as we have just seen, could not be confined in so small a space. In a neutron's decay the electron and antineutrino are created at the moment the neutron turns into a proton, in the same way that a photon is created when an atom emits light rather than being stored inside it beforehand.

A second mistake is to read the mass number as the mass. A is a count of particles and is always a whole number; the actual mass of a nucleus is slightly less than A atomic mass units for most nuclei, because energy is released when nucleons bind and that energy carries mass away. The difference is under 1%, small enough to ignore in the density estimate, but it is the whole subject of the lesson on binding energy and the source of nuclear energy.

A third is to imagine the nucleus as a miniature solar system, with nucleons in neat orbits, or as a rigid bag of hard balls. Nucleons are quantum objects, described by wavefunctions spread over the whole nucleus, and they are packed so tightly that their wavefunctions overlap. The marble picture is right about one thing, the constant density, and wrong about almost everything else. Finally, the word isotope does not mean radioactive. Carbon-12 and carbon-13 are both isotopes of carbon and both are perfectly stable; only some isotopes, such as carbon-14, decay.

## Limits of the simple picture

The picture of a nucleus as a uniform ball of protons and neutrons, with radius 1.2A^(1/3) fm, is excellent for medium and heavy nuclei and frays at the edges. The lightest nuclei are not uniform balls at all. The deuteron, one proton and one neutron, is loosely bound and unusually spread out. Some neutron-rich light nuclei, such as lithium-11, have one or two neutrons that spend much of their time far outside a compact core, forming a diffuse halo that makes the nucleus comparable in size to nuclei with several times as many nucleons. Many heavy nuclei are not spherical either, but stretched into the shape of a rugby ball, which shows up in the pattern of their energy levels.

The nucleons themselves are not the end of the line. Experiments at Stanford in the late 1960s, firing electrons at protons with far more energy than Hofstadter's, found that the proton has hard, point-like pieces inside it. These are quarks, and a proton is about 0.84 fm in radius, not much smaller than the spacing between nucleons in a nucleus. For the physics of nuclei, protons and neutrons remain the right building blocks, in the same way that atoms remain the right building blocks for chemistry even though they have parts. The lesson on quarks takes the story down a level.

## Where it shows up

The two-number description of a nucleus runs through the rest of this course. Isotopes are the reason radiocarbon dating works, since carbon-14 behaves chemically like ordinary carbon and is taken up by living things, as the lesson on half-life explains. They are also the reason the lessons on fission dwell on uranium-235 rather than uranium-238: chemically identical, the two differ by three neutrons, and that difference decides whether a slow neutron can split them. Separating isotopes, which cannot be done by chemistry, became one of the great industrial efforts of the twentieth century for exactly this reason.

The neutron itself became the most useful probe in nuclear physics. Having no charge, it is not repelled by nuclei and can enter them at any energy, which is how fission was discovered, as The Discovery of Fission recounts. Beams of slow neutrons are used today to study the structure of materials, because they scatter from nuclei rather than from electrons and so see light atoms such as hydrogen that X-rays barely notice. Nuclear spin, which this lesson met as the clue that sank the proton–electron model, is what magnetic resonance imaging detects: the hospital scanner images the spins of hydrogen nuclei in the body's water. And the density of nuclear matter is the density of neutron stars, whose existence is part of the story told in Can Stars Really Collapse?

## What holds it together?

The neutron solved the problem of what nuclei are made of, and in doing so it made another problem impossible to ignore. Protons all carry positive charge, and like charges repel, more strongly the closer they are. In a nucleus they are packed only a femtometre or two apart, where that repulsion is enormous. Neutrons have no charge, so they cannot attract protons electrically, and gravity between such tiny masses is far too weak to matter. By everything known in 1932, a nucleus with more than one proton should fly apart instantly. It does not, so some other force, strong enough to overpower electrical repulsion at close range, must be holding it together. That force is the subject of the next lesson.
