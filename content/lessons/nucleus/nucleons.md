Rutherford's gold-foil experiment of 1911 showed that an atom's positive charge and almost all of its mass sit in a nucleus about ten thousand times smaller than the atom. What the nucleus was made of took another twenty years to settle.

## Protons, and a problem

The hydrogen nucleus, the lightest, was named the **proton** by Rutherford around 1920. Every other nucleus has a charge that is a whole number Z of proton charges, the **atomic number**, which fixes how many electrons the atom holds and so its chemistry. But nuclei weigh more than Z protons. Helium has charge 2 and mass about 4; uranium has charge 92 and mass about 238.

The obvious fix was to put extra protons in the nucleus and cancel their charge with electrons: helium as 4 protons plus 2 electrons. This proton–electron model failed on two counts. The uncertainty principle says an electron squeezed into a region the size of a nucleus must have a momentum so large that its energy would be tens of MeV, far more than anything holding it there. And nitrogen-14 had the wrong spin: 14 protons plus 7 electrons is an odd number of spin-½ particles, which must give a half-integer total spin, but measurements in 1929 showed nitrogen-14's spin is a whole number.

::: history 1920 · London
In a lecture to the Royal Society, Rutherford speculated that a proton and an electron might bind tightly into a neutral particle, which could pass through matter easily and enter nuclei. His assistant James Chadwick spent much of the next decade looking for it without success.
:::

## The neutron

In 1930 Walther Bothe and Herbert Becker in Germany found that beryllium struck by alpha particles gave off a very penetrating, neutral radiation, which they took to be gamma rays. In January 1932 Irène Curie and Frédéric Joliot in Paris reported that this radiation knocked protons out of paraffin wax at speeds corresponding to about 5 MeV.

::: history February 1932 · Cambridge, England
Chadwick saw that the gamma-ray interpretation made no sense. To give a proton 5 MeV in a collision, a gamma ray would need about 50 MeV, far more than the reaction could supply, and it would give nitrogen nuclei much smaller recoils than he measured. A neutral particle with about the proton's mass explained both recoils as simple billiard-ball collisions. Within a month of the Paris report he sent a letter to *Nature*, "Possible Existence of a Neutron". He received the 1935 Nobel Prize in Physics.
:::

The **neutron** is not a bound proton and electron, as Rutherford had imagined. It is a particle in its own right, slightly heavier than the proton (939.6 MeV/c² against 938.3 MeV/c²). Outside a nucleus it lives about 15 minutes on average before decaying into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino. Within months of Chadwick's letter, Werner Heisenberg and others described the nucleus as protons and neutrons alone, together called **nucleons**. Both problems vanished: no electrons need be confined, and nitrogen-14 has 7 protons and 7 neutrons, an even number of spin-½ particles.

## Counting nucleons

A nucleus is labelled by its proton number Z, neutron number N, and **mass number** A = Z + N. Carbon-12 is written ¹²C, with 6 protons and 6 neutrons. Nuclei with the same Z but different N are **isotopes**: carbon-14 has 8 neutrons, but the same 6 protons, so the same electrons and the same chemistry. Isotopes had been known since Frederick Soddy named them in 1913; the neutron finally explained them.

Scattering experiments show that nuclear radii grow with mass number in a simple way:

::: equation Nuclear radius
R \approx r_0 A^{1/3}, \quad r_0 \approx 1.2\,\text{fm}
:::

Here 1 fm (a femtometre) is 10⁻¹⁵ m. The volume, proportional to R³, is proportional to A: each nucleon takes up the same space, like marbles packed in a bag. That has a striking consequence.

::: steps The density of nuclear matter
$ \rho = \frac{A\,m_u}{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3}
Density is mass over volume. Each nucleon weighs about one atomic mass unit, m_u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.
---
$ \rho = \frac{A\,m_u}{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r_0^3 A} = \frac{m_u}{\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r_0^3}
Substitute R³ = r₀³A. The mass number cancels, so every nucleus has the same density.
---
$ \rho \approx \frac{1.66 \times 10^{-27}\,\text{kg}}{7.2 \times 10^{-45}\,\text{m}^3} \approx 2 \times 10^{17}\,\text{kg/m}^3
With r₀ = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m, the volume per nucleon is about 7 × 10⁻⁴⁵ m³.
:::

A teaspoon of nuclear matter would weigh about a billion tonnes. Neutron stars are made of matter at roughly this density.

::: note
The nucleus is built from two kinds of nucleon: Z protons, which fix the element, and N neutrons, which add mass without charge. Nucleons pack at a fixed density, so a nucleus's volume is proportional to its mass number.
:::

The neutron left an obvious question. Protons repel each other electrically, and neutrons have no charge to attract them. Something else must hold the nucleus together.
