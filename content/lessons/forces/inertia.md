The motion chapter described how things move: positions, velocities and accelerations, with no questions asked about what makes them change. This chapter asks that question. The first answer is the one that took longest to find, because it runs against nearly everything we see. A body moving at a steady speed in a straight line needs nothing to keep it going. What needs a cause is not motion but a *change* in motion. Once that idea is in place, the rest of mechanics can be built on it. This lesson traces how people came to accept it, states it carefully, and then tests it: on a coasting spacecraft, on a cup in a braking car, on a ball dropped in a train, and on the rotating Earth itself. By the end you should be able to say why a passenger lurches forward when a bus brakes, why no experiment inside a smoothly moving ship can tell that it moves, and why the law only holds for some observers.

## Why do moving things stop?

Everyday experience seems to say that motion needs a push. Push a cart and it rolls; stop pushing and it soon halts. Kick a ball across grass and it slows to a stop. Nothing we handle keeps moving on its own for long, so it is natural to conclude that rest is the normal state of things and that motion is something you have to keep supplying.

Aristotle, writing in Athens around 350 BC, turned this experience into a theory. He divided motion into two kinds. *Natural motion* was the motion a body makes of its own accord toward its proper place: heavy things like stones move down, toward the centre of the Earth, and light things like fire move up. Every other motion was *violent*, meaning forced on a body from outside, and it lasted only as long as something kept pushing. Take away the mover and the motion ends. For the cart and the ball, that account fits what we see.

The theory had one obvious weakness, and Aristotle knew it. A stone thrown from the hand keeps flying after the hand lets go. If violent motion needs a continual mover, what pushes the stone in mid-air? Aristotle's suggestion was that the air itself does the work: air displaced at the front rushes round to the back and shoves the stone along. Hardly anyone found this convincing, since the same air seems to slow things down rather than speed them up.

The medieval answer was better. In the sixth century John Philoponus, a scholar in Alexandria, and in the fourteenth Jean Buridan, in Paris, proposed that the thrower gives the stone something of its own, a driving quality Buridan called *impetus*. The impetus carries the stone along after it leaves the hand, and it gradually wears away, which is why the stone eventually falls. Buridan even suggested that the heavenly spheres might have been given an impetus at the creation which, meeting no resistance, had never worn away. This was a real step, because it located the cause of continued motion in the moving body rather than in the surrounding air, and because it hinted that motion might last forever if nothing opposed it. But on Earth it kept the central mistake. Motion was still something that had to be fed, and left to itself it would die out.

## What Galileo's ramps showed

The way out came from Galileo, in the early seventeenth century, and it came from imagining an experiment more perfect than any he could actually perform.

Let a ball roll down one ramp and up a second ramp facing it. If the surfaces are smooth, the ball climbs back up almost to the height it started from. The small shortfall is due to friction, and the smoother the ramps, the smaller it gets. Galileo took the limit seriously: with perfectly smooth ramps, the ball would return to exactly its starting height.

Now make the second ramp less steep. The ball still climbs back to its starting height, but to do so it must roll further along the gentler slope. Make the slope gentler still and it rolls further still. Finally, lay the second ramp flat. The ball can never regain its starting height, because the flat ramp never rises, so it never has a reason to stop. It should roll on forever.

The argument changes what needs explaining. When a real ball on a real floor slows down, that is not motion running out of fuel, and it is not the natural state of rest reasserting itself. It is the effect of forces that oppose the motion, mainly friction between the ball and the floor and the resistance of the air. Take those away and the motion simply continues. The ordinary experience that started Aristotle off is real, but it was being misread: what we see in everyday life is not motion on its own, but motion plus friction.

It is worth seeing why this was so hard to notice. Friction is everywhere in ordinary life, and it never switches off, so there is no everyday experience of motion without it. Galileo's step was to treat friction as one more thing acting on the ball, something that could in principle be made smaller and smaller, rather than as part of what motion is. Once friction is seen as a separate influence, the remaining motion can be studied on its own, and it turns out to be the simplest thing imaginable: nothing happens to it.

Galileo set out the ramp argument in his *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* of 1632 and again in *Two New Sciences* of 1638. He needed it partly to defend the idea that the Earth moves: if a moving Earth carries everything on it along without a continual push, then there is no reason we should feel the motion. In the *Dialogue* he asked the reader to imagine shutting themselves below decks in a large ship with some flies, butterflies and a bowl of fish. Whether the ship lies at anchor or sails smoothly at any speed, the flies drift to all sides alike and the fish swim as easily toward the stern as the bow; nothing in the cabin shows the motion. But Galileo drew a subtly wrong conclusion about what persists. The "horizontal" motion that lasts forever, in his view, follows the curved surface of the Earth, so a body left alone would circle the Earth's centre. It was René Descartes, in his *Principles of Philosophy* of 1644, who first stated clearly that a body left to itself moves in a *straight* line at constant speed, and that any turning away from a straight line needs a cause. Newton took the principle from both men and made it the first of his three laws.
:::

## How did Newton state the law?

In the *Principia* of 1687, Newton put it this way: every body continues in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a straight line, unless it is compelled to change that state by forces acting on it.

Two terms in that sentence need pinning down. A **force** is a push or a pull exerted on a body by something else: a hand, a rope, the floor, the Earth's gravity. Forces have both a size and a direction, so they are vectors, and when several act at once what matters is their vector sum, called the **net force**. That is why the first law speaks of a body being "compelled", not merely touched. Several forces can act on a body and still cancel. A book lying on a table is pulled down by gravity and pushed up by the table, and since the two are equal and opposite the net force is zero and the book stays put.

In symbols, the law says that zero net force and constant velocity go together:

::: equation The first law
\sum \vec{F} = 0 \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad \vec{v} = \text{constant}
:::

It is worth pausing on what "constant velocity" includes. Rest is simply the special case where the velocity is zero, so the first law treats a parked car and a car cruising steadily down a straight motorway as the same kind of thing: neither has a net force on it. That can feel wrong, since the cruising car's engine is clearly working hard. But the engine's push through the tyres is matched by air resistance and rolling friction pushing back, and the sum is zero. The engine is not keeping the car moving; it is cancelling the forces that would otherwise stop it.

Velocity has a direction as well as a size, so constant velocity means constant speed *and* constant direction. A car going round a corner at a steady 50 km/h has a constant speed but a changing direction, so its velocity is changing, and by the first law something must be pushing it sideways. That sideways push turns out to come from friction between the tyres and the road, and the Circular Motion lesson works out exactly how large it must be.

The tendency of a body to keep its velocity is called **inertia**, from the Latin for idleness or inactivity. Bodies differ in how much of it they have. A loaded supermarket trolley is harder to get moving than an empty one, and once moving it is harder to stop or to steer round a corner. The measure of how much inertia a body has is its **mass**. The first law says only that a force is needed to change velocity; the next lesson says how much force, and that is where mass becomes a number.

## Three cases worked through

The law is easiest to trust once you have watched it make predictions. Here are three, each showing a different side of it.

The first is a body with almost nothing acting on it. Voyager 1 was launched in 1977, and after its last planetary encounter, with Saturn in 1980, its engines have done almost nothing to its speed. It is now moving away from the Sun at about 17 km/s. The Sun's gravity still tugs it back, but at its distance of more than 160 times the Earth–Sun distance the pull is so weak that its speed falls by only about 7 m/s a year, out of 17,000. So to a very good approximation it simply coasts. A year is about 3.16 × 10⁷ seconds, so in one year it covers about 17 km/s × 3.16 × 10⁷ s ≈ 5.4 × 10⁸ km, roughly three and a half times the distance from the Earth to the Sun, with no fuel spent at all. That is the first law in its purest form: a large velocity, maintained for decades, by nothing.

The second is the cup on the dashboard of a braking car, seen from the ground. Suppose the car is going at 15 m/s (54 km/h) and brakes steadily at 6 m/s², and the dashboard is too smooth to grip the cup. After one second the car's speed has dropped to 9 m/s, and it has travelled the average of 15 and 9 times one second, which is 12 m. The cup, with nothing slowing it, is still at 15 m/s and has travelled 15 m. So in one second the cup has moved 3 m forward *relative to the car*, which means it has hit the windscreen long before. Nobody pushed it. The car was slowed by its brakes, and the cup, attached to nothing, carried on. A passenger without a seatbelt is in exactly the cup's position, which is why seatbelts exist: the belt is the force that slows the passenger along with the car.

The third is a ball dropped inside a train running smoothly at 30 m/s on straight track. Let it fall from a height of 1.0 m. Falling from rest through 1.0 m takes √(2 × 1.0 / 9.8) ≈ 0.45 s. During that time the train moves forward 30 × 0.45 ≈ 13.5 m. Does the ball land 13.5 m behind the point it was dropped from? No: it lands at your feet. Before you let go, the ball was moving forward at 30 m/s along with you and the train, and once released nothing acts on it horizontally, so it keeps that 30 m/s and moves forward exactly as far as the train does. Seen from the platform, it follows a curved path, a projectile launched horizontally at 30 m/s; seen from the train, it falls straight down. Both are correct, and the first law is what makes them agree.

## Does the law hold for everyone?

There is a catch, and it shows up as soon as you try the law from inside a vehicle that is changing speed. Go back to the cup on the dashboard. To someone sitting in the braking car, the cup has changed its velocity with no force acting: it was at rest, and now it is sliding forward. From inside the car, the first law appears to fail.

To someone standing on the pavement, as we saw, the same event looks entirely ordinary. The cup carried on at its old speed while the car slowed beneath it. The first law holds perfectly well for the person on the pavement. It fails only for the person who is decelerating along with the car.

So the law needs a statement of who is watching. A **frame of reference** is the point of view from which positions and velocities are measured, such as the ground or the inside of the car. Frames in which the first law holds are called **inertial frames**. As the Relative Motion lesson showed, two frames moving at constant velocity relative to each other measure the same accelerations for everything they watch, because adding a constant velocity changes no velocity's rate of change. It follows that if one frame is inertial, every frame moving steadily relative to it is inertial too. A train gliding smoothly along a straight track at constant speed is as good a place to test the first law as the platform, which is what the dropped ball showed. Frames that accelerate or rotate are not inertial, and in them bodies appear to start moving with nothing pushing them.

This has a striking consequence, the one Galileo saw from his ship's cabin. If every steadily moving frame is equally good, then no experiment on the laws of motion can tell you which of them is "really" at rest. A ball dropped in a smooth train behaves exactly as one dropped on the platform. The question "how fast am I really moving?" has no answer that mechanics can give; only motion relative to something else can be measured. This is the **principle of relativity**, and it is older than Einstein by nearly three centuries. What Einstein added, in Einstein's Two Postulates, was the insistence that it hold for light as well.

## Going deeper: is the Earth an inertial frame?

The ground beneath us is very nearly an inertial frame, which is why the law works so well in everyday life. It is not exactly one, because the Earth rotates once a day and travels round the Sun, and both motions are changes of direction, so both are accelerations. How large are they?

A point on the equator is carried round a circle of radius about 6,378 km once a day. Turning at that rate needs an acceleration toward the Earth's axis of about 0.034 m/s², roughly a third of one per cent of the 9.8 m/s² with which things fall. The Earth's orbit round the Sun needs a smaller acceleration still, about 0.006 m/s², and since the Sun's gravity pulls everything on Earth almost equally, we fall around the Sun together and hardly notice it at all. So the error made by treating the ground as inertial is a fraction of a per cent, invisible in most experiments and large enough to matter only in long, careful ones.

The best known of those is the pendulum hung by Léon Foucault in the Panthéon in Paris in 1851: a 28 kg ball on a wire 67 m long. Set swinging, its plane of swing slowly turns, by about 11 degrees an hour in Paris. The pendulum is not being pushed sideways. In an inertial frame its plane of swing would stay fixed, and it is the Earth that turns underneath it. At the North Pole the floor would turn a full circle beneath the pendulum in a day; at lower latitudes only part of the Earth's spin is about the vertical, and in Paris a full turn takes about 32 hours. At the equator the plane does not turn at all.

That raises a deeper question. If the Earth is not quite inertial, and the Sun is not either, since it orbits the centre of the galaxy, what is? In practice, physicists find inertial frames by experiment: a frame is inertial if bodies with no forces on them move in straight lines at constant speed when observed from it. Seen this way, the first law is not merely the special case of the second law with zero force, as it is sometimes described. It is the claim that such frames exist at all, and it tells you which frames the second law is to be used in. Remarkably, a frame that does not rotate relative to the distant galaxies is, as far as anyone can measure, also a frame in which a gyroscope or a Foucault pendulum shows no rotation. Ernst Mach in the 1880s wondered whether that agreement was a clue that inertia itself comes from the rest of the universe. The question has never been fully settled.

## What people often get wrong

The most common mistake is the one Aristotle made: thinking that a moving body must have a force on it in its direction of motion. Asked to draw the forces on a hockey puck sliding steadily across ice, many people draw an arrow pointing forward. There is no such force. Once the stick has left the puck, the only forces on it are gravity, the ice pushing up, and a small friction pointing backward. A forward arrow would mean the puck speeds up.

A close relative is the idea that a thrown ball "carries the force of the throw" and that this force wears off. This is Buridan's impetus, and it survives in everyday speech. A force is something one body does to another, and once the hand has let go it does nothing to the ball. What the ball carries away is its velocity, and that velocity changes only because gravity and air act on it now.

A third mistake is to treat inertia as a force, as in "inertia pushed me forward when the bus braked". Inertia is not a push; it is the absence of one. Nothing pushed you forward. The bus slowed and you did not, until the seat or a handrail pushed you back.

Finally, people often think that in space, where things are "weightless", they also have no inertia. An astronaut on the International Space Station who tries to stop a drifting cargo container of several hundred kilograms finds it just as hard to stop as it would be on a frictionless floor on Earth. Weight is the pull of gravity; inertia is resistance to changes in velocity, and that goes wherever the mass goes.

## Where does the law stop working?

Within Newton's mechanics the first law has no exceptions, provided it is applied in an inertial frame. Its limits appear at a deeper level, in what counts as an inertial frame.

Special relativity keeps the first law exactly: a body with no force on it still moves in a straight line at constant speed. What changes is how velocities seen by different inertial observers are related, which is no longer by simple addition when the speeds approach that of light.

General relativity changes more. Einstein noticed that a person falling freely, in a lift whose cable has snapped, feels no gravity at all: dropped objects float beside them, exactly as in deep space. Over a small enough region and a short enough time, such a falling frame behaves like an inertial one. In this view gravity is not a force that pushes bodies off straight lines, but the curvature of the spacetime they move through, and a freely falling body is the one following the straightest path available. The Equivalence Principle lesson develops this. The price is that there is no single inertial frame spread over the whole universe, only small local ones, and the first law becomes a statement about those.

## Where it shows up

Every seatbelt, airbag and headrest is a device for dealing with inertia: when a car stops suddenly, your body keeps its velocity until something applies a force to it, and these supply that force over as long a distance and as wide an area as possible. You can tighten a loose hammer head by banging the handle on the floor: the handle stops, the heavy head carries on down and wedges itself tighter. Shaking ketchup from a bottle, whipping a tablecloth from under the plates, and the way water flies off a spinning salad drier, which carries it round until the holes let it continue in a straight line, all work the same way.

In spaceflight the first law is the whole economy of travel. A probe fires its engines only to change its velocity; between burns it coasts for months or years for free, which is why a mission to Saturn can run on a few tonnes of fuel.

In the course, the first law is the ground floor. The next lesson, F = ma, says how much force produces how much change in velocity, and turns mass into a number. Action and Reaction says where forces come from. Circular Motion takes up the car on the roundabout and asks exactly how large the sideways force must be. Later, Momentum and Collisions shows that the first law, extended to many bodies pushing on each other, becomes conservation of momentum, and The Equivalence Principle reopens the question of which frames are inertial.

::: note
Motion at constant velocity needs no cause. A net force is needed only to change velocity: to speed a body up, slow it down or turn it. This holds in inertial frames, those that are not themselves accelerating or rotating, and no experiment on motion can tell one inertial frame from another.
:::
