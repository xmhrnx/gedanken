The motion chapter described how things move: positions, velocities and accelerations, with no questions asked about what makes them change. This chapter asks that question. The first answer is the one that took longest to find, because it runs against nearly everything we see. A body moving at a steady speed in a straight line needs nothing to keep it going. What needs a cause is not motion but a *change* in motion. Once that idea is in place, the rest of mechanics can be built on it, and this lesson traces how people came to accept it.

## Why do moving things stop?

Everyday experience seems to say that motion needs a push. Push a cart and it rolls; stop pushing and it soon halts. Kick a ball across grass and it slows to a stop. Nothing we handle keeps moving on its own for long, so it is natural to conclude that rest is the normal state of things and that motion is something you have to keep supplying.

Aristotle, writing in Athens around 350 BC, turned this experience into a theory. He divided motion into two kinds. *Natural motion* was the motion a body makes of its own accord toward its proper place: heavy things like stones move down, toward the centre of the Earth, and light things like fire move up. Every other motion was *violent*, meaning forced on a body from outside, and it lasted only as long as something kept pushing. Take away the mover and the motion ends. For the cart and the ball, that account fits what we see.

The theory had one obvious weakness, and Aristotle knew it. A stone thrown from the hand keeps flying after the hand lets go. If violent motion needs a continual mover, what pushes the stone in mid-air? Aristotle's suggestion was that the air itself does the work: air displaced at the front rushes round to the back and shoves the stone along. Hardly anyone found this convincing, since the same air seems to slow things down rather than speed them up.

The medieval answer was better. In the sixth century John Philoponus, a scholar in Alexandria, and in the fourteenth Jean Buridan, in Paris, proposed that the thrower gives the stone something of its own, a driving quality Buridan called *impetus*. The impetus carries the stone along after it leaves the hand, and it gradually wears away, which is why the stone eventually falls. This was a real step, because it located the cause of continued motion in the moving body rather than in the surrounding air. But it kept the central mistake. Motion was still something that had to be fed, and left to itself it would die out.

## What Galileo's ramps showed

The way out came from Galileo, in the early seventeenth century, and it came from imagining an experiment more perfect than any he could actually perform.

Let a ball roll down one ramp and up a second ramp facing it. If the surfaces are smooth, the ball climbs back up almost to the height it started from. The small shortfall is due to friction, and the smoother the ramps, the smaller it gets. Galileo took the limit seriously: with perfectly smooth ramps, the ball would return to exactly its starting height.

Now make the second ramp less steep. The ball still climbs back to its starting height, but to do so it must roll further along the gentler slope. Make the slope gentler still and it rolls further still. Finally, lay the second ramp flat. The ball can never regain its starting height, because the flat ramp never rises, so it never has a reason to stop. It should roll on forever.

The argument changes what needs explaining. When a real ball on a real floor slows down, that is not motion running out of fuel, and it is not the natural state of rest reasserting itself. It is the effect of forces that oppose the motion, mainly friction between the ball and the floor and the resistance of the air. Take those away and the motion simply continues. The ordinary experience that started Aristotle off is real, but it was being misread: what we see in everyday life is not motion on its own, but motion plus friction.

::: history 1632–1644 · Florence and Amsterdam
Galileo set out the ramp argument in his *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* of 1632 and again in *Two New Sciences* of 1638. He needed it partly to defend the idea that the Earth moves: if a moving Earth carries everything on it along without a continual push, then there is no reason we should feel the motion. But Galileo drew a subtly wrong conclusion. The "horizontal" motion that persists forever, in his view, follows the curved surface of the Earth, so a body left alone would circle the Earth's centre. It was René Descartes, in his *Principles of Philosophy* of 1644, who first stated clearly that a body left to itself moves in a *straight* line at constant speed, and that any turning away from a straight line needs a cause. Newton took the principle from both men and made it the first of his three laws.
:::

## How did Newton state the law?

In the *Principia* of 1687, Newton put it this way: every body continues in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a straight line, unless it is compelled to change that state by forces acting on it.

Two terms in that sentence need pinning down. A **force** is a push or a pull exerted on a body by something else: a hand, a rope, the floor, the Earth's gravity. Forces have both a size and a direction, so they are vectors, and when several act at once what matters is their vector sum, called the **net force**. That is why the first law speaks of a body being "compelled", not merely touched. Several forces can act on a body and still cancel. A book lying on a table is pulled down by gravity and pushed up by the table, and since the two are equal and opposite the net force is zero and the book stays put.

In symbols, the law says that zero net force and constant velocity go together:

::: equation The first law
\sum \vec{F} = 0 \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad \vec{v} = \text{constant}
:::

It is worth pausing on what "constant velocity" includes. Rest is simply the special case where the velocity is zero, so the first law treats a parked car and a car cruising steadily down a straight motorway as the same kind of thing: neither has a net force on it. But velocity has a direction as well as a size, so constant velocity means constant speed *and* constant direction. A car going round a corner at a steady 50 km/h has a constant speed but a changing direction, so its velocity is changing, and by the first law something must be pushing it sideways. That sideways push turns out to come from friction between the tyres and the road, and the circular motion lesson works out exactly how large it must be.

The tendency of a body to keep its velocity is called **inertia**, from the Latin for idleness or inactivity. Bodies differ in how much of it they have. A loaded supermarket trolley is harder to get moving than an empty one, and once moving it is harder to stop or to steer round a corner. The measure of how much inertia a body has is its **mass**. The first law says only that a force is needed to change velocity; the next lesson says how much force, and that is where mass becomes a number.

## Does the law hold for everyone?

There is a catch, and it shows up as soon as you try the law from inside a moving vehicle. Put a coffee cup on the dashboard of a car and have the driver brake hard. The cup slides forward, although nothing pushed it. To someone sitting in the car, the cup has changed its velocity with no force acting, and the first law appears to fail.

To someone standing on the pavement, the same event looks entirely ordinary. The cup was moving forward with the car. When the car braked, the brakes slowed the car, but nothing slowed the cup, because the smooth dashboard could not grip it. So the cup simply carried on at its old speed while the car slowed beneath it. The first law holds perfectly well for the person on the pavement. It fails only for the person who is decelerating along with the car.

So the law needs a statement of who is watching. A **frame of reference** is the point of view from which positions and velocities are measured, such as the ground or the inside of the car. Frames in which the first law holds are called **inertial frames**. As the relative motion lesson showed, two frames moving at constant velocity relative to each other measure the same accelerations for everything they watch, because adding a constant velocity changes no velocity's rate of change. It follows that if one frame is inertial, every frame moving steadily relative to it is inertial too. A train gliding smoothly along a straight track at constant speed is as good a place to test the first law as the platform. Frames that accelerate or rotate are not inertial, and in them bodies appear to start moving with nothing pushing them.

The ground beneath us is very nearly an inertial frame, which is why the law works so well in everyday life. It is not exactly one, because the Earth rotates once a day and travels round the Sun. These motions produce small effects that a careful experiment can detect. The best known is the Foucault pendulum, a long pendulum whose plane of swing slowly turns over the course of hours. The pendulum is not being pushed sideways; the Earth is turning underneath it.

::: note
Motion at constant velocity needs no cause. A net force is needed only to change velocity: to speed a body up, slow it down or turn it. This holds in inertial frames, those that are not themselves accelerating or rotating.
:::
