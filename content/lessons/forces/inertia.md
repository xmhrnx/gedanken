The motion chapter described how things move without asking why. Now comes the why. The first step is the hardest one historically: realising that steady motion needs no cause at all. Only *changes* in motion do.

## Aristotle's answer

Everyday experience says that moving things stop. Push a cart and it rolls; stop pushing and it halts. Aristotle, around 350 BC, made this a principle. Heavy bodies have a *natural motion* toward the centre of the Earth, and any other motion is *violent*: it lasts only while something keeps pushing. Remove the mover and the motion ends.

The theory had an obvious problem. A thrown stone keeps going after it leaves the hand. Aristotle suggested the air rushing in behind it kept pushing, which never convinced anyone much. In the 6th century John Philoponus, and in the 14th Jean Buridan, proposed instead that the thrower gives the stone an *impetus*, an internal driving quality that gradually wears away. Impetus was closer to the truth but kept the core mistake: motion was still something that needed to be fed and that died out by itself.

## Galileo's ramps

Galileo attacked the question with an idealised experiment. Let a ball roll down one ramp and up a second facing it. With smooth surfaces, the ball climbs back almost to its starting height, whatever the slope of the second ramp. Make the second ramp shallower and the ball must roll further to reach that height. Now lay it flat. The ball can never regain its height, so it should roll forever.

What stops real carts and balls is not the absence of a push but the presence of forces opposing motion: friction and air resistance. Remove those and motion simply continues. The cart halting is not the natural state reasserting itself; it is a force at work.

::: history 1632–1644 · Florence and Amsterdam
Galileo described the ramp argument in his *Dialogue* (1632) and *Two New Sciences* (1638), but he got one thing wrong: he thought the motion that persists unaided is horizontal in the sense of following the Earth's curved surface, a circle around the centre. René Descartes, in his *Principles of Philosophy* (1644), was the first to state clearly that a body left alone moves in a *straight* line at constant speed. Newton took the law from them both and made it the first of his three.
:::

## Newton's first law

Newton's version, in the *Principia* of 1687: every body continues in its state of rest, or of uniform motion in a straight line, unless compelled to change that state by forces acting on it.

A *force* here means a push or a pull exerted on a body by something else. Since forces are vectors, what matters is their vector sum, the **net force**. Several forces can act and still cancel: a book on a table is pulled down by gravity and pushed up by the table, and it stays put.

::: equation The first law
\sum \vec{F} = 0 \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad \vec{v} = \text{constant}
:::

Rest is just the special case v = 0. Constant velocity means constant speed *and* constant direction, so a car taking a corner at a steady 50 km/h is not in a state of zero net force. Something must be pushing it sideways; the circular motion lesson makes that exact.

The tendency of a body to keep its velocity is called **inertia**. The amount of inertia a body has is its **mass**: a loaded supermarket trolley is harder to start and harder to stop than an empty one. The next lesson turns this into a number.

## Which observers?

The first law cannot hold for everyone. In a car that brakes hard, a coffee cup on the dashboard slides forward with no force pushing it. To an observer in the car the law fails; to one on the pavement, the cup simply carries on at the old speed while the car slows beneath it.

Frames in which the first law holds are called **inertial frames**. As the relative motion lesson showed, frames moving at constant velocity relative to each other agree on accelerations, so if one frame is inertial, so is every frame moving steadily relative to it. Accelerating or rotating frames are not. The ground is very nearly inertial for most purposes; the Earth's rotation produces small effects, such as the slow swing of a Foucault pendulum, that a precise experiment can detect.

::: note
Motion at constant velocity needs no cause. A force is needed only to change velocity: to speed up, slow down or turn.
:::
