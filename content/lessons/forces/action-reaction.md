The first two laws are about a single body. The third is about two bodies at once: whenever one pushes or pulls on another, the second pushes or pulls back, equally hard.

## The law

If body A exerts a force on body B, then B exerts a force on A that is equal in size and opposite in direction.

::: equation Newton's third law
\vec{F}_{\text{on }A\text{ by }B} = -\vec{F}_{\text{on }B\text{ by }A}
:::

The two forces are often called action and reaction, though neither comes first; they arise together. Three features keep the law from being misused:

- The two forces act on **different bodies**. They never cancel each other, because cancelling only makes sense for forces on the same body.
- They are the **same kind** of force. If A pulls B by gravity, B pulls A by gravity. If A pushes B by contact, B pushes A by contact.
- They are equal **at every instant**, however the bodies move.

## The horse and the cart

An old puzzle: a horse pulls a cart, and by the third law the cart pulls back on the horse equally hard. So how does anything move?

The answer is to ask which forces act on each body. On the cart: the horse's pull forward, and friction from the road backward. The cart accelerates if the pull beats the friction. On the horse: the cart's pull backward, and the ground's push forward on its hooves. The horse accelerates if the ground pushes it forward harder than the cart pulls it back. The equal and opposite pair between horse and cart sits on two different free-body diagrams, so it cannot stop either one.

The same reasoning explains walking. Your foot pushes backward on the ground; by the third law the ground pushes forward on you, and that forward push, a friction force, is what accelerates you. On perfectly smooth ice you cannot push the ground backward, and you cannot start walking.

## Weight is not paired with the floor

A book rests on a table. Two forces act on it: its weight, pulling down, and the table's upward push, called the **normal force** (normal meaning perpendicular to the surface). They are equal and opposite, but they are *not* a third-law pair. They act on the same body and are different kinds of force; they balance because the book is not accelerating, which is the second law at work.

The third-law partner of the book's weight is the book's gravitational pull on the Earth. The partner of the table's push on the book is the book's push on the table.

The Earth really is pulled up by everything on it. It just does not notice:

::: steps How much does the Earth move when you jump?
$ F = mg = 70 \times 9.8 \approx 690\,\text{N}
While a 70 kg jumper is in the air, the Earth pulls them down with their weight.
---
$ F_{\text{on Earth}} = 690\,\text{N} \text{ upward}
By the third law, the jumper pulls the Earth toward them just as hard.
---
$ a_{\text{Earth}} = \frac{F}{M} = \frac{690}{5.97 \times 10^{24}} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{-22}\,\text{m/s}^2
The second law applied to the Earth. Same force, but a mass about 10²³ times larger gives a correspondingly smaller acceleration.
:::

## Internal forces cancel

The law has a powerful consequence. Treat several bodies together as one *system*. Each force between two bodies inside the system comes with its equal and opposite partner, also inside. Added up, every such pair cancels, and only forces from outside the system remain. So a system accelerates only because of external forces: you cannot lift yourself by your own bootstraps. This is the root of the conservation of momentum, the subject of a later lesson.

A rocket uses this. It pushes exhaust gas backward at high speed; the gas pushes the rocket forward. Nothing outside is needed to push against, so a rocket works as well in a vacuum as in air, better in fact.

::: history 1687 and 1920 · Cambridge and New York
Newton illustrated the law in the *Principia* with plain examples: press a stone with your finger, and the finger is pressed by the stone; a horse pulling a stone on a rope is pulled back equally toward the stone. He checked it with colliding pendulum bobs. More than two centuries later, in January 1920, a *New York Times* editorial mocked Robert Goddard for claiming a rocket could work in space, arguing that in a vacuum there would be nothing for it to push against. On 17 July 1969, with Apollo 11 on its way to the Moon, the paper printed a correction.
:::

The law is not exact in every circumstance. Between moving electric charges the forces can be unequal, because the electromagnetic field itself carries momentum; but the deeper principle, conservation of momentum, survives.

::: note
Forces come in pairs, equal and opposite, acting on two different bodies. They never cancel each other; they cancel only when you add up a whole system.
:::
