The three laws of motion were first set out together in a single book: *Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica*, "Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy", published by Isaac Newton in 1687. It did far more than state them. It showed that the force pulling an apple down is the same force holding the Moon and planets in their paths, and it derived the motions of the solar system from a handful of laws. Before it, the heavens and the Earth were thought to follow different rules. After it, they followed the same ones.

## A question in a coffee house

By the 1680s several people suspected that the Sun pulls the planets with a force that weakens as the inverse square of distance. Kepler had described the planets' orbits as ellipses early in the century, and the circular motion rule a = v²/r, combined with Kepler's third law, pointed toward the inverse square for circular orbits. Nobody could prove that an inverse-square force produces an *ellipse*.

::: history 1684 · London and Cambridge
In January 1684 Christopher Wren, Robert Hooke and the young astronomer Edmond Halley discussed the problem in London. Hooke claimed he had a proof; Wren offered a book worth forty shillings to whoever could produce one in two months. Nobody did. That August Halley visited Newton in Cambridge and asked what curve a planet would follow under an inverse-square attraction. Newton answered at once: an ellipse. He had calculated it years before. He could not find the papers, promised to redo the work, and in November sent Halley a nine-page treatise, *De motu corporum in gyrum* ("On the motion of bodies in orbit"). Halley saw what it was and urged him on. Over the next two and a half years the nine pages grew into the *Principia*.
:::

## Three books

The *Principia* opens with definitions of mass, momentum and force, and the three laws. Then come three books.

**Book I** treats motion under forces in empty space. Newton proves that any force directed toward a fixed centre makes a body sweep out equal areas in equal times, which is Kepler's second law; that an inverse-square force gives conic sections (ellipses, parabolas, hyperbolas); and that a uniform sphere attracts outside bodies as if all its mass were at its centre.

**Book II** treats motion through resisting fluids. Its main target was René Descartes' theory that the planets are carried round by whirlpools of invisible matter; Newton showed such vortices could not reproduce Kepler's laws.

**Book III**, "The System of the World", applies all this to nature. Gravity acts between every pair of bodies, in proportion to each mass and inversely as the square of the distance between them:

::: equation Newton's law of universal gravitation
F = \frac{GMm}{r^2}
:::

From this Newton explained Kepler's laws, the tides as the pull of Moon and Sun on the oceans, the slow wobble of the Earth's axis, the flattening of the Earth at the poles, and the orbits of comets, which he showed move on very elongated conic sections. The constant G was not measured until Henry Cavendish's experiment in 1798; Newton worked only with ratios.

## The Moon test

The central claim is that terrestrial gravity reaches the Moon. Newton checked it with numbers anyone can now reproduce. The Moon is about 60 Earth radii away.

::: steps Does gravity at the Moon fall off as the inverse square?
$ a_{\text{predicted}} = \frac{g}{60^2} = \frac{9.8}{3600} \approx 2.72 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m/s}^2
If gravity weakens as 1/r², then at 60 times the distance from the Earth's centre it is 60² = 3600 times weaker than g at the surface.
---
$ a_{\text{observed}} = \frac{4\pi^2 r}{T^2}
The Moon's actual acceleration follows from its nearly circular orbit, using the centripetal formula from the circular motion lesson.
---
$ a_{\text{observed}} = \frac{4\pi^2 \times 3.84 \times 10^8}{(2.36 \times 10^6)^2} \approx 2.72 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m/s}^2
Use the Moon's distance, 3.84 × 10⁸ m, and its orbital period of 27.3 days, 2.36 × 10⁶ s. The two numbers agree.
:::

The Moon is falling toward the Earth, all the time, with exactly the acceleration an apple would have if it were carried that far up. Its sideways speed keeps it from ever arriving. Newton later recalled doing a rougher version of this comparison around 1666 and finding the results "answer pretty nearly".

In a popular account published after his death, Newton illustrated the idea with a cannon on a mountaintop firing ever faster until the ball no longer comes down, the picture used in the circular motion lesson.

## How it was written and received

The book is in Latin and almost entirely geometric. Newton had invented his calculus, which he called fluxions, but he argued with diagrams and limiting ratios of vanishing quantities, the language his readers knew. It is very hard going; few read it all.

The Royal Society agreed to publish it but had spent its money on a lavish *History of Fishes*, so Halley paid for the printing himself and saw it through the press. When Hooke demanded credit for the inverse-square law, Newton threatened to withhold Book III, and cut references to Hooke instead. The book appeared in July 1687.

Continental critics, followers of Descartes and Leibniz, objected that gravity acting across empty space with no mechanism was a return to occult qualities. Newton did not claim to know what gravity was. In the second edition of 1713 he wrote "hypotheses non fingo": I frame no hypotheses. The predictions spoke for themselves. Using Newton's theory, Halley showed that the comets of 1531, 1607 and 1682 were one object and predicted its return in 1758. It was sighted on Christmas night that year, sixteen years after Halley's death.

::: note
The *Principia* showed that one set of laws, the three laws of motion plus universal gravitation, governs falling apples, the Moon, the planets, comets and tides. Heaven and Earth are not separate realms.
:::

Newton's mechanics held without challenge for more than two centuries. Einstein's relativity eventually replaced it at high speeds and in strong gravity, but for everything from bridges to spacecraft trajectories, the *Principia*'s laws are still the ones used. The gravity chapter develops them in full.
