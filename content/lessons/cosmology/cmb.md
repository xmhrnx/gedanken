If the early universe was hot and dense, it was filled with light. That light should still be here, stretched by the expansion into microwaves and arriving from every direction. It was predicted in 1948, forgotten, and found by two engineers trying to get rid of a noise in their antenna.

## When the universe became transparent

For its first few hundred thousand years the universe was a plasma of nuclei, free electrons and photons. Photons scattered off the free electrons constantly, as light does in fog, so the plasma was opaque. As it cooled to about 3000 K, the electrons joined nuclei to form neutral atoms, an event called **recombination**, at about 380,000 years. Neutral atoms barely scatter light, so the photons have travelled freely ever since. What we see is a snapshot of the universe at that moment, a **surface of last scattering** that surrounds us at a great distance.

Because the plasma was in thermal equilibrium, the light had a **blackbody spectrum**, the spectrum of a perfect absorber and emitter at one temperature. Expansion keeps it a blackbody but lowers its temperature in proportion to the stretch.

::: steps From 3000 K to today
$ 1 + z = \frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{now}}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{then}}} \approx 1100
Since recombination every wavelength has been stretched by a factor of about 1100, the growth of the universe since then.
---
$ T \propto \frac{1}{\lambda}
A blackbody's typical photon wavelength is inversely proportional to its temperature, so stretching all wavelengths by a factor lowers the temperature by the same factor.
---
$ T_{\mathrm{now}} = \frac{T_{\mathrm{then}}}{1 + z} \approx \frac{3000\,\text{K}}{1100} \approx 2.7\,\text{K}
Less than three degrees above absolute zero.
---
$ \lambda_{\mathrm{peak}} = \frac{2.898\,\text{mm K}}{T} = \frac{2.898\,\text{mm K}}{2.725\,\text{K}} \approx 1.06\,\text{mm}
Wien's law gives the wavelength where a blackbody is brightest. At 2.725 K that is about a millimetre, in the microwave band.
:::

## Found by accident

::: history 1948 · Maryland
Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman, extending Gamow's work on the early universe, predicted leftover radiation at about 5 K. The prediction drew little attention, and no one at the time thought such faint radiation could be measured.
:::

::: history 1964–65 · Holmdel, New Jersey
At Bell Labs, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson were using a 6 m horn antenna, built for satellite communication, for radio astronomy at a wavelength of 7.35 cm. They found a steady noise equal to about 3.5 K that came equally from every direction, day and night, in every season. They checked their electronics and evicted a pair of pigeons and cleaned out what they called "white dielectric material". The noise stayed. A colleague put them in touch with Robert Dicke at Princeton, about 50 km away, whose group was building an instrument to look for exactly this. "Boys, we've been scooped," Dicke told his team. Penzias and Wilson shared the 1978 Nobel Prize.
:::

The discovery largely ended the steady-state theory. A universe that has always looked the same has no hot past to leave a glow.

## COBE, WMAP and Planck

The radiation is not perfectly the same in all directions. It is about 0.1% hotter than average in one direction and cooler in the opposite one, because the Earth moves at about 370 km/s relative to it, a Doppler shift. Remove that and what remains is uniform to about one part in 100,000.

::: history 1989–2013 · in orbit
NASA's COBE satellite, launched in 1989, measured the spectrum in minutes of observing: a perfect blackbody at 2.725 K, the most precise blackbody ever measured in nature. Its other instrument found the tiny temperature ripples, about 1 part in 10⁵, that later grew into galaxies. John Mather and George Smoot shared the 2006 Nobel Prize. WMAP (2001–2010) and the European Planck satellite (2009–2013) mapped the ripples in ever finer detail.
:::

The ripples are sound waves in the early plasma, frozen at recombination. Their pattern of sizes depends on the universe's contents and geometry, so fitting it measures them. Planck's results: space is flat to within about half a percent; ordinary matter makes up about 5% of the universe's energy, dark matter about 27% and dark energy about 68%; the age is 13.8 billion years; and H₀ ≈ 67.4 km/s/Mpc, the value at the centre of the Hubble tension.

::: note
The microwave background is light released when the universe became transparent at about 3000 K. Stretched by a factor of about 1100, it is now a blackbody at 2.725 K, and its tiny ripples record the universe's contents.
:::
