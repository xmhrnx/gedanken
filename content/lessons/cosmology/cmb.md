If the early universe was hot and dense, it was also full of light, because anything hot glows. That light cannot simply have vanished. It should still be here, filling all of space, but stretched by billions of years of expansion until its wavelengths are no longer visible but measured in millimetres, in the microwave part of the spectrum, and arriving equally from every direction in the sky. This **cosmic microwave background** was predicted in 1948, largely forgotten, and then found by accident in 1964 by two radio astronomers trying to get rid of an annoying hiss in their antenna. This lesson explains where the light comes from, why it has the temperature it does, how it was found, and how its faint ripples have become the most precise measurement we have of what the universe contains.

## When did the universe become transparent?

For its first few hundred thousand years the universe was filled with a **plasma**, a hot gas in which electrons are not attached to atoms but move freely alongside bare nuclei. Free electrons are very good at scattering light: a photon, a particle of light, cannot travel far before bouncing off one in a new direction. So the early universe was opaque, in the way that fog is opaque. Light was everywhere, but none of it could travel in a straight line for long, and a hypothetical observer could not have seen through it.

As the universe expanded, it cooled. When the temperature fell to about 3,000 kelvin, the electrons slowed enough to be captured by nuclei and stay captured, forming neutral atoms, mostly hydrogen and helium. This happened about 380,000 years after the start and is called **recombination**, although the electrons and nuclei had never been combined before. It may seem surprising that it took so long; the energy needed to pull an electron off hydrogen corresponds to a much higher temperature. The reason is the same as for the deuterium bottleneck: photons outnumbered atoms by more than a billion to one, so even when the average photon was too weak, the few energetic ones in the tail were numerous enough to knock electrons off as fast as they were captured, until the temperature dropped to about 3,000 K.

Neutral atoms barely scatter light at all. So once recombination was over, the universe became transparent, almost suddenly, and the light that filled it at that moment has travelled freely ever since. When we detect it, we are seeing the universe as it was 380,000 years after the Big Bang. The light from every direction comes from the place where it last scattered off an electron, and that forms a vast sphere around us called the **surface of last scattering**. Looking at it is like looking at the inside of a cloud from within clear air: we cannot see past it, because before it the universe was opaque.

## What should the light look like now?

Before recombination, matter and light were constantly exchanging energy, so they were in **thermal equilibrium**, sharing one temperature. Light in thermal equilibrium has a very particular spread of wavelengths called a **blackbody spectrum**, the spectrum of an ideal object that absorbs all light falling on it and emits according to its temperature alone. The shape of the spectrum is fixed, and only its peak shifts with temperature: hotter bodies peak at shorter wavelengths. So the light released at recombination was a blackbody at about 3,000 K, glowing a dull orange, peaking in the near infrared.

The expansion has stretched every wavelength since then, and stretching preserves the blackbody shape but lowers its temperature.

::: steps From 3000 K to today
$ 1 + z = \frac{\lambda_{\mathrm{now}}}{\lambda_{\mathrm{then}}} \approx 1100
The redshift z measures how much the light has been stretched, and 1 + z is the ratio of its wavelength now to its wavelength then, which equals the factor by which the universe has grown. Since recombination, the universe, and every wavelength in it, has grown by a factor of about 1,100.
---
$ T \propto \frac{1}{\lambda}
For a blackbody, the typical wavelength of its light is inversely proportional to its temperature: double the temperature and the wavelengths halve. Stretching every wavelength by the same factor therefore produces exactly the spectrum of a cooler blackbody, with the temperature lowered by that same factor.
---
$ T_{\mathrm{now}} = \frac{T_{\mathrm{then}}}{1 + z} \approx \frac{3000\,\text{K}}{1100} \approx 2.7\,\text{K}
Divide the temperature at recombination by the stretch factor. The background light today should be a blackbody at less than three degrees above absolute zero. The prediction is not just a faint glow but a specific temperature and a specific spectral shape.
---
$ \lambda_{\mathrm{peak}} = \frac{2.898\,\text{mm K}}{T} = \frac{2.898\,\text{mm K}}{2.725\,\text{K}} \approx 1.06\,\text{mm}
Wien's law gives the wavelength at which a blackbody is brightest: a constant, 2.898 millimetre-kelvins, divided by the temperature. Using the measured temperature of 2.725 K, the peak falls at about a millimetre. That is in the microwave band, which is why it took radio and microwave instruments, not optical telescopes, to find it.
:::

## Found by accident

::: history 1948 · Washington, D.C.
Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman, extending Gamow's work on how nuclei formed in the early universe, realised that the hot early radiation should still exist, cooled by the expansion. They estimated its present temperature at about 5 K, close to the true value. The prediction attracted little attention. Radio astronomy was in its infancy, nobody thought such faint radiation spread evenly over the sky could be measured, and the steady-state theory, which predicted no such radiation, was gaining support. For more than a decade the idea was largely forgotten, even by some of the people who had worked on it.
:::

::: history 1964–65 · Holmdel, New Jersey
At Bell Telephone Laboratories, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson had taken over a 6-metre horn-shaped antenna built for early satellite communications, and they wanted to use it for radio astronomy at a wavelength of 7.35 centimetres. To do that they needed to account for every source of noise in the system. They were left with a steady excess hiss, equivalent to about 3.5 K, that came equally from every direction, day and night, through every season. It did not come from New York City, from the Milky Way or from their electronics. They found a pair of pigeons nesting in the horn, removed them, and cleaned out what they politely called "white dielectric material". The hiss remained. A colleague mentioned that Robert Dicke's group at Princeton, about 50 kilometres away, was building an instrument to look for leftover radiation from the Big Bang. Penzias telephoned Dicke, and after the call Dicke turned to his team and said, "Boys, we've been scooped." The two groups published side by side in 1965. Penzias and Wilson shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1978.
:::

The discovery was decisive in the argument between the Big Bang and the steady-state theory. A universe that has always looked the same has no hot, dense past and therefore no reason to be bathed in uniform thermal radiation. Steady-state supporters tried to explain the background as starlight scattered and warmed by dust, but no such explanation could produce a uniform glow with a blackbody spectrum, and by the end of the 1960s most cosmologists accepted that the universe had a hot beginning.

## How smooth is it?

The background is not perfectly the same in every direction. It is about 0.1% hotter than average in one direction and cooler by the same amount in the opposite direction. This is not a feature of the early universe but of our own motion. The Sun, and the Earth with it, moves at about 370 kilometres per second relative to the background radiation, and the Doppler effect makes the radiation slightly blueshifted, and so hotter, ahead of us and redshifted, and cooler, behind.

Subtract that effect, and what remains is astonishingly uniform: the same temperature in every direction to about one part in 100,000. But not perfectly uniform, and the tiny differences turned out to be the most important thing about it. Galaxies, clusters and everything else in the universe must have grown from small irregularities in the early matter, places slightly denser than average that pulled in more material over time. Those irregularities should have left their mark on the background.

::: history 1989–2013 · in orbit
NASA's Cosmic Background Explorer satellite, COBE, launched in 1989. One instrument, led by John Mather, measured the spectrum of the background, and within minutes of observing had shown it to be a blackbody at 2.725 K, matching the theoretical curve so perfectly that the audience at its first presentation gave a standing ovation; it is the most precise blackbody ever measured in nature. Another instrument, led by George Smoot, found the tiny temperature ripples, about one part in 100,000, which are the seeds from which galaxies later grew. Mather and Smoot shared the 2006 Nobel Prize in Physics. Two later satellites, NASA's WMAP, which observed from 2001 to 2010, and the European Space Agency's Planck, from 2009 to 2013, mapped the ripples in ever finer detail.
:::

## What do the ripples tell us?

The ripples are not random. Before recombination, the plasma was a fluid in which gravity pulled matter into denser regions while the pressure of the light trapped in it pushed back. The result was sound waves, regions of compression and rarefaction rippling through the early universe. At recombination the light escaped and the waves froze, leaving a pattern of hot and cold spots imprinted on the background, with a characteristic set of sizes. The largest of these spots span about one degree on the sky, twice the width of the full Moon.

The pattern depends sensitively on what the universe contains. The amount of ordinary matter affects how strongly the sound waves were compressed; the amount of dark matter affects how fast gravity pulled the dense regions in; and the geometry of space affects how large the spots appear across the great distance to us. So fitting a theoretical model to the observed pattern measures all of these at once. Planck's results are that space is flat, without overall curvature, to within about half a percent; that ordinary matter makes up about 5% of the universe's energy, dark matter about 27% and dark energy about 68%; that the universe is 13.8 billion years old; and that, according to the standard model of cosmology, the present expansion rate H₀ is about 67.4 km/s/Mpc. That last number, lower than the value found from nearby supernovae, is one side of the Hubble tension described in the lesson on expansion.

::: note
The microwave background is the light released when the universe became transparent, at about 3,000 kelvin and 380,000 years after the Big Bang. Stretched by a factor of about 1,100, it is now a near-perfect blackbody at 2.725 K, and its tiny ripples, frozen sound waves from the early plasma, record what the universe is made of.
:::
