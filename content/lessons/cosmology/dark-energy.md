Gravity pulls everything in the universe toward everything else, so astronomers long assumed the expansion must be slowing down. The question in the 1990s was only by how much: enough to stop it and pull everything back together, or not. Two teams set out to measure the slowing and found the opposite.

## Standard candles

To trace the expansion over billions of years you need distances to very remote galaxies. The tool was the **Type Ia supernova**, the explosion of a white dwarf star, which for a few weeks can outshine its entire galaxy. They are nearly, but not exactly, equally bright at peak. In 1993 Mark Phillips showed that the brighter ones fade more slowly, so measuring how fast one fades tells you its true brightness to within about 10%. A source of known brightness is a **standard candle**: how faint it looks gives its distance, since brightness falls as 1/d².

The redshift of the host galaxy gives how much the universe has stretched since the light left. Distance and redshift together give the expansion history. If the expansion had been slowing, the universe was expanding faster in the past, so it would have taken less time to reach its present stretch; the light from a given redshift would have travelled a shorter time and distance, and the supernova would look brighter.

::: history 1998 · Berkeley and Mount Stromlo
The Supernova Cosmology Project, led by Saul Perlmutter, and the High-z Supernova Search Team, led by Brian Schmidt with Adam Riess as lead author, found dozens of supernovae at redshifts around 0.5. Both found them about 20–25% fainter than expected, fainter even than in an empty universe with no gravity at all. The expansion has been speeding up. Each team had hoped to beat the other and half expected an error; both got the same answer. They shared the 2011 Nobel Prize.
:::

## Einstein's constant returns

::: history 1917 · Berlin
Applying general relativity to the whole universe, Einstein found it could not stay static. To hold it still he added a term to his equations, the **cosmological constant** Λ, a uniform repulsion built into space. When the expansion was found he dropped it. George Gamow later recalled him calling it his biggest blunder; whether he used those words is doubtful.
:::

The accelerating expansion is most simply explained by Λ, or equivalently by an energy belonging to empty space itself, **vacuum energy**. Its key property is that its density does not thin out as space expands. Why that causes acceleration follows from the equation for how the expansion changes, in which pressure acts as a source of gravity alongside density.

::: steps Why vacuum energy accelerates the expansion
$ \frac{1}{a}\frac{\mathrm{d}^2 a}{\mathrm{d}t^2} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho + \frac{3p}{c^2}\right)
Friedmann's acceleration equation from general relativity. a is the scale factor, the relative size of the universe; ρ is mass density and p pressure. A positive right side means speeding up.
---
$ p \approx 0 \Rightarrow \frac{1}{a}\frac{\mathrm{d}^2 a}{\mathrm{d}t^2} < 0
Galaxies and dark matter exert almost no pressure, so ordinary gravity decelerates the expansion.
---
$ \mathrm{d}E = -p\,\mathrm{d}V, \quad E = \rho c^2 V
Energy conservation for an expanding region of volume V: the energy inside changes by the work its pressure does on the surroundings.
---
$ \rho = \text{constant} \Rightarrow \rho c^2\,\mathrm{d}V = -p\,\mathrm{d}V \Rightarrow p = -\rho c^2
For vacuum energy the density stays fixed, so the energy grows with volume. That requires a negative pressure, a tension.
---
$ \rho + \frac{3p}{c^2} = \rho - 3\rho = -2\rho
The negative pressure counts three times and outweighs the density.
---
$ \frac{1}{a}\frac{\mathrm{d}^2 a}{\mathrm{d}t^2} = +\frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho_{\mathrm{vac}} > 0
The right side is now positive: vacuum energy makes the expansion accelerate. Writing ρ_vac = Λc²/(8πG) gives Einstein's term, Λc²/3.
:::

Early on, matter was dense and won, so the expansion slowed. As space grew, matter thinned while vacuum energy stayed fixed, and about 6 billion years ago the vacuum term took over. Today it makes up about 68% of the universe's energy. Since 1998 the result has been confirmed independently: the microwave background shows that space is flat, which requires a total density that matter supplies only about a third of, and the imprint of early sound waves on the distribution of galaxies gives a matching expansion history.

## What is it?

The name **dark energy** covers whatever is responsible. The cosmological constant fits nearly all the data, but it has a severe problem: quantum theory predicts a vacuum energy larger than the observed value by something like 120 orders of magnitude, and no one knows why the real value is so small but not zero. Since 2024 the DESI galaxy survey has found hints, not yet decisive, that dark energy may be weakening over time, which a true constant cannot do. If it is constant, the expansion will continue to accelerate for ever, and galaxies beyond our local group will eventually recede out of sight.

::: note
Distant supernovae are fainter than a slowing universe allows, so the expansion is accelerating. In general relativity, anything with strongly negative pressure, such as a constant vacuum energy, drives acceleration.
:::
