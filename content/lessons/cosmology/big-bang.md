If galaxies are moving apart now, they were closer together in the past. Run the expansion backwards far enough and everything we see was once packed into a hot, dense state. That is the **Big Bang** theory: not an explosion at a point in space, but the whole of space being much denser and hotter, everywhere, about 13.8 billion years ago.

## How old is the universe?

The Hubble constant has units of speed per distance, which is one over time. Its inverse estimates how long the expansion has been going on: if every galaxy had always moved at its present speed, then a galaxy at distance d moving at v = H₀d would have started at our position a time d/v = 1/H₀ ago, whatever its distance.

::: steps The Hubble time, step by step
$ t_H = \frac{d}{v} = \frac{1}{H_0}
At constant speed, time is distance over speed. Every galaxy gives the same answer because v is proportional to d.
---
$ H_0 = 70\,\frac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}, \quad 1\,\text{Mpc} = 3.086 \times 10^{19}\,\text{km}
Converting the megaparsec to kilometres lets the distance units cancel.
---
$ H_0 = \frac{70}{3.086 \times 10^{19}}\,\text{s}^{-1} \approx 2.27 \times 10^{-18}\,\text{s}^{-1}
Kilometres cancel, leaving one over seconds.
---
$ t_H = \frac{1}{2.27 \times 10^{-18}}\,\text{s} \approx 4.41 \times 10^{17}\,\text{s}
Invert to get a time.
---
$ t_H \approx \frac{4.41 \times 10^{17}}{3.156 \times 10^{7}}\,\text{years} \approx 1.4 \times 10^{10}\,\text{years}
There are about 3.156 × 10⁷ seconds in a year. The Hubble time is about 14 billion years.
:::

The expansion has not been steady. Gravity slowed it for most of cosmic history, and dark energy has sped it up for the last several billion years. A careful calculation using the measured contents of the universe gives 13.8 billion years. That the two effects almost cancel, making 1/H₀ close to the true age, is a coincidence of the present epoch.

Hubble's own value of about 500 km/s/Mpc gave a Hubble time of about 2 billion years, less than the age of the Earth from radioactive dating. That embarrassment helped keep rival theories alive until the 1950s, when corrected distances lowered H₀.

::: history 1931–1949 · Louvain and London
Lemaître proposed in 1931 that the universe began as a "primeval atom" that broke apart. The rival **steady-state theory** of Fred Hoyle, Hermann Bondi and Thomas Gold (1948) held that new matter appears continuously as space expands, so the universe looks the same for ever and has no beginning. Hoyle coined the name "Big Bang" on BBC radio in 1949, to distinguish it from his own idea; he denied it was meant as mockery.
:::

## The first three minutes

The expansion cools whatever fills space: the temperature of radiation falls in proportion to 1/(size of the universe). Early on it was hot enough to break apart any nucleus. George Gamow and his student Ralph Alpher worked out in 1948 what happens as it cools, and the theory, refined since, is called **Big Bang nucleosynthesis**.

At about one second after the start, at some ten billion kelvin, neutrons and protons stop converting into each other. There is then about one neutron for every six protons, because the neutron is slightly heavier and harder to make. Free neutrons decay, with a half-life of about ten minutes, so the ratio keeps falling.

::: model decay
Free neutrons decay with a half-life of about 10 minutes. The early universe had a few minutes to lock them into nuclei before they were gone.
:::

Nuclei could not form at first: any deuterium (one proton plus one neutron) was broken apart by energetic photons as soon as it formed. Only after about three minutes, below roughly a billion kelvin, did deuterium survive. Then it was quickly built up into helium-4, the most tightly bound light nucleus, and almost every remaining neutron ended up there. By then the ratio had fallen to about one neutron per seven protons.

::: steps How much helium?
$ \frac{n}{p} \approx \frac{1}{7}
Take a sample of 16 nucleons: 2 neutrons and 14 protons.
---
$ 2n + 2p \to {}^{4}\mathrm{He}
Each helium-4 nucleus holds two neutrons and two protons, so the 2 neutrons make one helium nucleus, using 2 of the protons.
---
$ Y = \frac{4}{16} = 0.25
Helium carries 4 of the 16 units of mass; the other 12 protons stay as hydrogen. Y is the fraction of ordinary mass in helium.
:::

The oldest stars and gas clouds contain about 24–25% helium by mass, as predicted; stars could not have made that much in the time available. The theory also predicts small amounts of deuterium, helium-3 and lithium-7. The deuterium abundance agrees with the density of ordinary matter measured independently from the microwave background. Lithium comes out about three times too high, an unresolved puzzle.

::: note
Running the expansion backwards gives an age close to 1/H₀, about 14 billion years, and a hot dense early phase. The helium made in its first few minutes, about a quarter of all ordinary mass, is direct evidence that it happened.
:::
