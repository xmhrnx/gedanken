If galaxies are moving apart now, they must have been closer together in the past. Run the expansion backwards far enough and everything we can see was once packed into a state far hotter and denser than the centre of any star. That is the **Big Bang** theory. Its name invites a misleading picture of an explosion at some point in space, with debris flying outward into emptiness. The theory says something different: that the whole of space was once much denser and hotter, everywhere at once, about 13.8 billion years ago, and has been expanding and cooling ever since. This lesson asks two questions about that past. How long ago did the expansion begin? And what evidence, beyond the expansion itself, shows that the hot dense phase really happened? The answer to the second turns out to be written in the amount of helium in the universe.

## How old is the universe?

The Hubble constant H₀, the expansion rate, gives a quick estimate of the universe's age, and the reason is in its units. H₀ is quoted in kilometres per second per megaparsec: a speed divided by a distance. A distance divided by a speed is a time, so a speed divided by a distance is one over a time. The inverse of H₀ is a time, and it turns out to be the right time to ask about.

Imagine that every galaxy has always moved away from us at its present speed. A galaxy now at distance d, moving at speed v, would have been right next to us a time d/v ago. By the Hubble–Lemaître law, v = H₀d, so that time is d/(H₀d) = 1/H₀. The distance cancels. A galaxy twice as far away is moving twice as fast, so it took exactly the same time to get there. Every galaxy points back to the same moment, which is what you would expect if they all started together.

::: steps The Hubble time, step by step
$ t_H = \frac{d}{v} = \frac{1}{H_0}
At constant speed, the time taken to cover a distance is that distance divided by the speed. Substituting the Hubble–Lemaître law, v = H₀d, cancels the distance and leaves 1/H₀. This is called the **Hubble time**, and it is the same for every galaxy precisely because speed is proportional to distance.
---
$ H_0 = 70\,\frac{\text{km/s}}{\text{Mpc}}, \quad 1\,\text{Mpc} = 3.086 \times 10^{19}\,\text{km}
To get an actual time, the units of H₀ must be simplified. They contain two lengths, kilometres in the speed and megaparsecs in the distance. Writing the megaparsec in kilometres will let the two lengths cancel.
---
$ H_0 = \frac{70}{3.086 \times 10^{19}}\,\text{s}^{-1} \approx 2.27 \times 10^{-18}\,\text{s}^{-1}
Replace 1 Mpc with 3.086 × 10¹⁹ km. The kilometres on top and bottom cancel, leaving H₀ in units of one over seconds. The expansion rate is a tiny fraction per second, which is why it took telescopes to notice it.
---
$ t_H = \frac{1}{2.27 \times 10^{-18}}\,\text{s} \approx 4.41 \times 10^{17}\,\text{s}
Inverting H₀ turns a rate into a time. The Hubble time is about 4.4 × 10¹⁷ seconds.
---
$ t_H \approx \frac{4.41 \times 10^{17}}{3.156 \times 10^{7}}\,\text{years} \approx 1.4 \times 10^{10}\,\text{years}
A year contains about 3.156 × 10⁷ seconds, so dividing by that converts to years. The Hubble time is about 14 billion years.
:::

The estimate assumed the galaxies always moved at their present speeds, and they did not. For most of cosmic history the gravity of all the matter in the universe was slowing the expansion down, so it used to be faster. That alone would make the true age less than 1/H₀. But for the last several billion years a mysterious **dark energy** has been speeding the expansion up, which pushes the other way. A careful calculation, following the expansion backward using the measured amounts of matter and dark energy, gives an age of 13.8 billion years. That the two effects nearly cancel, so that 1/H₀ comes out close to the true age, is a coincidence of the present era; at other times in cosmic history it would not have been true.

The age was not always so comfortable. Hubble's own value of H₀, about 500 km/s/Mpc, gave a Hubble time of only about 2 billion years. By the 1930s radioactive dating showed that rocks on Earth were older than that. A universe younger than the Earth was an embarrassment, and it kept alternatives to the Big Bang alive until the 1950s, when corrected distances lowered H₀ and lengthened the time.

::: history 1931–1949 · Louvain and Cambridge, England
Lemaître was the first to take the beginning seriously. In 1931 he proposed that the universe started as a single "primeval atom", which broke apart and became everything we see. Many physicists disliked the idea of a beginning, which seemed to put the origin of the universe beyond the reach of physics. In 1948 Fred Hoyle, Hermann Bondi and Thomas Gold proposed an elegant alternative, the **steady-state theory**. They accepted the expansion but argued that new matter is created continuously, at a rate too small to detect, filling in the gaps as galaxies move apart, so that the universe looks the same at all times and has no beginning. Hoyle, speaking on BBC radio in 1949, described the rival idea as one in which all the matter was created "in one big bang". The name stuck. Hoyle later denied that he meant it as mockery; he wanted a vivid phrase for radio. For nearly two decades the two theories competed, and deciding between them needed evidence of the universe's past, not just its present.
:::

## The first three minutes

That evidence came from thinking carefully about what a hot, dense early universe would do. As space expands, the light filling it is stretched to longer wavelengths, and longer wavelengths mean lower energy, so the radiation cools: its temperature falls in proportion to one over the size of the universe. Run that backward, and the early universe was hotter the earlier you look. Early enough, it was hot enough to break apart any atomic nucleus. As it cooled, there must have been a moment when nuclei could first form and survive. George Gamow and his student Ralph Alpher worked out in 1948 what would happen at that moment. Their theory, refined a great deal since, is called **Big Bang nucleosynthesis**, the making of nuclei in the Big Bang.

The story begins about one second after the start, when the temperature was around ten billion kelvin. Before this, protons and neutrons were constantly turning into each other through weak-force reactions with electrons and neutrinos, and the balance between them was set by temperature. A neutron is slightly heavier than a proton, so it takes a little more energy to make, and in the balance there were always somewhat fewer neutrons than protons. At about one second the universe had cooled and thinned enough that these reactions stopped. The balance was frozen at about one neutron for every six protons.

That was not the end of the change. A free neutron is unstable: it decays into a proton, an electron and an antineutrino, with a half-life of about ten minutes. So from then on the neutrons were steadily disappearing, and they would all have been lost unless something locked them safely inside nuclei, where neutrons are stable.

::: model decay
Watch the number of free neutrons halve every ten minutes or so. The early universe had only a few minutes to lock them into nuclei before most of them were gone.
:::

The way into nuclei led through deuterium, the nucleus of heavy hydrogen, one proton bound to one neutron. A proton and a neutron meeting would readily form a deuteron, but at first every deuteron was smashed apart again almost immediately by energetic photons. This went on long after the typical photon energy had fallen well below the 2.2 MeV that holds a deuteron together. The reason is numbers: photons outnumbered protons and neutrons by more than a billion to one, so even the rare photons in the high-energy tail of the distribution were enough to break up every deuteron as it formed. Only at about three minutes, when the temperature fell below roughly a billion kelvin, did deuterium survive. This delay is called the **deuterium bottleneck**.

Once deuterium could survive, the rest happened in minutes. Deuterons combined with protons, neutrons and each other, building helium-4, two protons and two neutrons, which is by far the most tightly bound of the light nuclei. Almost every neutron that still existed ended up in helium-4. By then, after three minutes of decay, the ratio had fallen to about one neutron for every seven protons, and that ratio fixes how much helium was made.

::: steps How much helium?
$ \frac{n}{p} \approx \frac{1}{7}
Take the ratio of neutrons to protons when helium formation began. To make the arithmetic concrete, consider a sample of 16 nucleons, which at this ratio means 2 neutrons and 14 protons.
---
$ 2n + 2p \to {}^{4}\mathrm{He}
Each helium-4 nucleus holds two neutrons and two protons. Since almost every neutron ends up in helium, our 2 neutrons pair with 2 of the protons to make exactly one helium nucleus.
---
$ Y = \frac{4}{16} = 0.25
Count the mass. The helium nucleus holds 4 of the 16 nucleons, and the other 12 protons remain as hydrogen nuclei. So the fraction of ordinary mass in helium, written Y, is 4/16, or 25%. That figure depends on almost nothing except the neutron-to-proton ratio, which is fixed by well-understood nuclear physics.
:::

This was a sharp prediction, and it matched what astronomers saw. The oldest stars and the most pristine clouds of gas, which have been changed least by later generations of stars, contain about 24–25% helium by mass. Stars do make helium, but at the rate they shine they could have made only a few percent in the age of the universe, nowhere near a quarter. The helium had to come from somewhere else, and the Big Bang accounts for it.

The theory predicts small amounts of other light nuclei too: deuterium, helium-3 and lithium-7, which were left over when the reactions stopped. Deuterium is especially useful, because how much survives depends sensitively on how dense ordinary matter was: the denser it was, the more completely deuterium was burned into helium. The measured deuterium abundance gives a density of ordinary matter that agrees with a completely independent measurement from the cosmic microwave background. Lithium is the exception. The theory predicts about three times more lithium-7 than is seen in old stars, and whether the fault lies in the theory, the nuclear data or our understanding of the stars is still unresolved.

::: note
Running the expansion backwards gives an age close to 1/H₀, about 14 billion years, and implies a hot, dense early phase. The helium made in the first few minutes, about a quarter of all ordinary mass, matches the prediction of nuclear physics and is direct evidence that this hot phase really happened.
:::
