Time dilation cannot be the whole story. If moving clocks run slow, then something must also happen to lengths, because otherwise two observers would reach different conclusions about events they can both check, such as whether a particle survives to hit a detector. The partner of time dilation is **length contraction**: an object moving relative to you is shorter, as you measure it, along its direction of motion, by the same factor γ that slows its clocks. This lesson shows why the contraction is forced on us, derives it from time dilation, and then takes up the question everyone asks: is the object really shorter?

## How does the muon see its own journey?

The previous lesson explained why muons made 15 km up in the atmosphere reach the ground, even though they decay on average after 2.2 microseconds: seen from the ground, a muon moving at 0.995c has its internal clock slowed by a factor γ ≈ 10, so it lasts long enough to make the trip.

Now take the muon's own point of view. The first postulate says its frame is just as valid as ours for doing physics. In that frame the muon is at rest, so its clock is not slowed at all: it lives its normal 2.2 microseconds on average. The Earth, with its atmosphere, rushes up toward it at 0.995c. In 2.2 microseconds, at that speed, the ground covers only about 660 m. So if the atmosphere were 15 km thick in the muon's frame, the muon would almost certainly decay long before the ground arrived.

Yet the muon does reach the ground: a detector at sea level clicks. Whether a detector clicks is not a matter of viewpoint. It is a single event, at a single place, and every observer must agree that it happened. So in the muon's frame, something else must give. The only candidate is the distance. In the muon's frame, the atmosphere rushing past at 0.995c must be not 15 km thick but about a tenth of that, around 1.5 km, which the ground can cover in the muon's lifetime.

The two descriptions now fit together. The ground observer says the muon's clock is slow and the atmosphere is its normal thickness. The muon says its clock is normal and the atmosphere is thin. They describe the same events with different accounts of time and distance, and they agree on every outcome that can actually be checked.

## How much shorter, exactly?

The muon argument suggests that lengths along the direction of motion shrink by γ. To show it in general, consider a rod lying at rest on the ground. Its length measured in its own rest frame, the frame in which it is not moving, is called its **proper length**, L₀. A small clock travels along the rod at speed v, from one end to the other.

::: steps Length contraction from time dilation
$ \Delta t = \frac{L_0}{v}
Work first in the rod's frame. The clock covers the rod's full length L₀ at speed v, so the trip takes this long, measured by clocks at rest beside the rod. There is nothing relativistic yet; this is distance divided by speed.
---
$ \Delta t_0 = \frac{\Delta t}{\gamma} = \frac{L_0}{\gamma v}
The travelling clock itself measures the duration of the trip as its proper time, which by time dilation is shorter by the factor γ. The clock's reading is something every observer agrees on: passing the first end and passing the second end are two events that both happen right at the clock, and what its dial shows at each is a plain fact anyone can read off. That makes it a solid bridge between the two frames.
---
$ L = v\,\Delta t_0
Now switch to the clock's frame. There the clock is at rest, and the rod slides past it at speed v. The rod's length in this frame is the distance that slides past in the time between its two ends passing the clock, and that time, in this frame, is just the clock's own reading Δt₀. So the length is speed times that time.
---
$ L = v \cdot \frac{L_0}{\gamma v} = \frac{L_0}{\gamma}
Replace Δt₀ with the expression from the second step. The speed v appears on the top and the bottom and cancels, leaving a result that depends on v only through γ. In the frame where the rod moves, it is shorter than its proper length by the factor γ.
---
$ L = L_0\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}
Write 1/γ out in full, since γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²). Because the square root is less than 1 for any speed above zero, the moving rod is always measured as shorter than its proper length, and at everyday speeds only slightly so.
:::

::: equation Length contraction
L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma} = L_0\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}
:::

Only lengths **along** the direction of motion contract. The light-clock lesson showed, with the argument of the two passing metre sticks and their paintbrushes, that lengths at right angles to the motion are the same for every observer. So a fast-moving ball is measured not as a smaller ball but as a flattened one, squashed along its direction of travel into a shape like a lentil, while its width across the motion is unchanged.

Raise the clock's speed. The γ shown is the factor by which the clock's ticks are stretched, and also the factor by which the clock, or any object moving with it, is shortened along its motion.
:::

## How large is the effect?

At 0.6c, where γ = 1.25, a metre stick is measured at 80 cm. At 0.866c, where γ = 2, it is 50 cm. At 0.99c it is about 14 cm. At everyday speeds the contraction is far too small to notice. Take the fastest everyday motion there is, Earth's orbit around the Sun at 30 km/s. Measured by someone at rest relative to the Sun, Earth's diameter of 12,700 km is shortened along its direction of motion by about 6 cm, roughly the width of a hand, out of nearly thirteen thousand kilometres. That is a fraction of five parts in a billion, the size of v²/(2c²).

## Is the object really shorter?

That depends on what "really" means, and the answer is subtle in two ways.

First, the effect is **reciprocal**. An observer standing beside the rod measures a passing spaceship as shortened. The spaceship's crew, regarding themselves as at rest, measure the rod as shortened by the same factor. Neither measurement is the true one, and neither is an illusion; each is correct in the frame where it is made. Nothing is squeezed. No forces act on the rod, and it feels no stress; an engineer on board the spaceship would find every part of her ship exactly the size it always was.

How can both be right? The answer lies in what it means to measure the length of a moving object. A ruler laid alongside a rod at rest can be read at leisure. For a moving rod you must mark where its front end is and where its back end is **at the same moment**, and then measure the distance between the two marks. If you marked the front end now and the back end a little later, the rod would have moved on in between, and you would get the wrong answer. The trouble, as the next lesson shows, is that observers moving relative to each other disagree about which events happen at the same moment. The two observers mark different pairs of points, each pair "simultaneous" by their own clocks, and so they get different lengths. Length contraction and the relativity of simultaneity are the same fact seen from two sides.

Second, measuring is not the same as seeing. When you look at a fast-moving object, or photograph it, the light reaching your eye at one instant left different parts of the object at different times: light from the far side set out earlier than light from the near side, because it had farther to come. In 1959 James Terrell and, independently, Roger Penrose worked out what this does to the image. A sphere moving past at nearly the speed of light does not look flattened at all. It still looks circular, but appears rotated, as if you were seeing a little of its back. The contraction is still there in the measurements; the photograph mixes it with the different travel times of the light. A measurement asks where all parts of an object are at one moment in your frame; a photograph asks what light arrives at one moment at your camera, and these are different questions.

::: note
An object moving at speed v is shorter along its direction of motion, as measured by an observer it moves past, by the factor γ. The contraction is required by time dilation, because two observers who disagree about how long a journey took must still agree on whether it was completed, so they must also disagree about how far it was.
:::

::: history 1889–1905 · Dublin, Leiden and Bern
The idea of a contraction came first as a rescue for the ether. After Michelson and Morley's experiment of 1887 failed to detect Earth's motion through it, the Irish physicist George FitzGerald suggested, in a short letter to the American journal Science in 1889, that objects moving through the ether might be shortened along their motion by just enough to hide the effect. The letter was so brief that it was almost forgotten, and FitzGerald himself seems not to have known it had been printed. Hendrik Lorentz in Leiden reached the same idea independently in 1892 and gave it a physical argument: the forces holding a solid together are electrical, electrical forces between moving charges change with their speed, so the spacing of atoms in a moving solid might shrink by exactly the right amount. For both men the contraction was a real compression, caused by motion through the ether, and every object had one true length, its length when at rest in the ether. Einstein's version of 1905 uses the same formula but means something different by it. It needs no ether and no special forces, it follows from the two postulates alone, and it is symmetric: each of two observers measures the other's lengths as contracted, and neither is privileged.
:::
