Time dilation has a partner. If a moving clock runs slow, then a moving object must be shorter along its direction of motion, by the same factor γ. Otherwise two observers would disagree about things they can both check, such as whether a particle reaches the ground.

## The muon's point of view

From the ground, a muon made 15 km up survives the trip because its internal clock runs slow. But the first postulate says the muon's frame is as good as ours. In its own frame the muon is at rest and lives its normal 2.2 microseconds on average. The ground rushes up at 0.995c, and in 2.2 μs it covers only about 660 m. Yet the muon still reaches the ground: both frames must agree on that, because a detector either clicks or does not.

The only way out is that in the muon's frame the atmosphere is not 15 km thick. Moving at 0.995c relative to the muon, it is contracted by γ ≈ 10, to about 1.5 km. The ground observer says "the muon's clock is slow"; the muon says "your atmosphere is thin". They describe the same events and agree on every outcome.

## Deriving the contraction

Take a rod at rest on the ground with length L₀ measured in its own rest frame (its **proper length**). A clock moves along the rod at speed v, from one end to the other.

::: steps Length contraction from time dilation
$ \Delta t = \frac{L_0}{v}
In the rod's frame, the clock covers the rod's length L₀ at speed v.
---
$ \Delta t_0 = \frac{\Delta t}{\gamma} = \frac{L_0}{\gamma v}
The moving clock's own reading for the trip is the proper time, shorter by γ. This is the time dilation result. Passing each end of the rod are two events that happen at the clock, so its reading is unambiguous; every observer agrees what it showed.
---
$ L = v\,\Delta t_0
In the clock's frame, the clock is at rest and the rod slides past at speed v. The rod's length in this frame is the speed times the time it takes the rod to pass, as read on the clock.
---
$ L = v \cdot \frac{L_0}{\gamma v} = \frac{L_0}{\gamma}
Substitute Δt₀ from the second step. v cancels.
---
$ L = L_0\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}
Write out 1/γ. The moving rod is shorter than its proper length.
:::

::: equation Length contraction
L = \frac{L_0}{\gamma} = L_0\sqrt{1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}}
:::

Only lengths **along** the motion contract. The light-clock lesson showed that lengths perpendicular to the motion are unchanged. A moving ball is measured as flattened into an ellipsoid, not shrunk evenly.

## How much?

At 0.6c a metre stick measures 80 cm; at 0.866c, 50 cm; at 0.99c, 14 cm. At everyday speeds the effect is unmeasurably small. Earth's orbital speed, 30 km/s, would contract Earth's 12,700 km diameter, as measured by someone at rest relative to the Sun, by about 6 cm.

## Is it real?

It is reciprocal: an observer on the rod sees a passing spaceship shortened, and the spaceship crew sees the rod shortened. Neither is the "true" length; each is right in their own frame. Nothing is squeezed, and no stresses build up in the rod.

Measuring the length of a moving object means marking where its two ends are **at the same time** and measuring the distance between the marks. Observers in relative motion disagree about which events happen at the same time (the next lesson), so they mark different pairs of points and get different lengths. Length contraction and the relativity of simultaneity are the same fact seen from two sides.

What you would actually **see** is different again. Light from the far side of a fast object left earlier than light from the near side, and in 1959 James Terrell and Roger Penrose showed that this makes a passing sphere look rotated, not flattened. Photographs and measurements are different things.

::: note
An object moving at speed v is shorter along its motion by the factor γ. This is required by time dilation: two observers must agree on what happens, even when they disagree on how long it took and how far it went.
:::

::: history 1889–1905 · Dublin, Leiden, Bern
To rescue the ether from the Michelson–Morley null result, George FitzGerald suggested in 1889, in a short letter to Science, that bodies moving through the ether are shortened along their motion. Hendrik Lorentz arrived at the same idea independently in 1892 and argued that the electric forces holding matter together might change with motion so as to produce it. For them, the contraction was a real physical squeezing caused by motion through the ether, with one true length at rest in the ether. Einstein's 1905 version needs no ether and no forces: it follows from the two postulates, and it is symmetric between observers.
:::
