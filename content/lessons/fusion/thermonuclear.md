The two fission bombs used in 1945 released the energy of about 15 and 21 thousand tonnes of TNT. Within ten years the United States and the Soviet Union had both tested weapons hundreds of times more powerful. These used fusion, the reaction that powers the Sun, and they were called thermonuclear weapons because the fusion is driven by heat. This lesson covers two things: the physics of why fusing light nuclei releases more energy per kilogram than splitting heavy ones, and the history of how the hydrogen bomb came to be built, which is as much a story about a moral and political argument among physicists as about physics.

## Why does fusion release more energy per kilogram?

The energy released by any nuclear reaction comes from a change in binding. Every nucleus weighs a little less than the protons and neutrons it is made of, because energy was released when they bound together, and by E = mc² that energy carried mass away. The **binding energy per nucleon** (a nucleon is a proton or a neutron) measures how tightly each particle is held, on average. A reaction releases energy whenever its products are more tightly bound than its starting materials.

Plot binding energy per nucleon against the number of nucleons and you get a curve that rises steeply from hydrogen, peaks around iron and nickel, and then falls gently toward uranium. Both fission and fusion move toward the peak. Fission splits a heavy nucleus on the gentle right-hand slope into two medium ones nearer the top. Fusion joins light nuclei on the steep left-hand slope into a heavier one. Because the left side is so much steeper, a fusion reaction gains much more binding for each nucleon involved.

::: model binding-curve
Compare the steep climb from hydrogen to helium with the gentle slope from iron down to uranium. Each nucleon gains far more binding when light nuclei fuse than when a heavy one splits.
:::

The standard example is the fusion of deuterium (hydrogen-2, one proton and one neutron) with tritium (hydrogen-3, one proton and two neutrons). Of all fusion reactions, it proceeds fastest at the lowest temperature, because both nuclei carry only a single charge and so repel each other as weakly as possible.

::: equation Deuterium–tritium fusion
{}^{2}\mathrm{H} + {}^{3}\mathrm{H} \to {}^{4}\mathrm{He} + n + 17.6\,\text{MeV}
:::

The product is helium-4, one of the most tightly bound light nuclei, plus a spare neutron, and the energy released is 17.6 MeV (million electronvolts). Comparing that with fission needs a common measure, and the fair one is energy per nucleon, since a kilogram of any fuel contains roughly the same number of nucleons.

::: steps Energy per kilogram: fusion against fission
$ \frac{17.6\,\text{MeV}}{5\,\text{nucleons}} \approx 3.5\,\text{MeV per nucleon}
Five nucleons go into the reaction: two in the deuterium and three in the tritium. Sharing the 17.6 MeV among them shows that each ends up, on average, about 3.5 MeV more tightly bound than before.
---
$ \frac{200\,\text{MeV}}{236\,\text{nucleons}} \approx 0.85\,\text{MeV per nucleon}
For comparison, a uranium-235 nucleus that absorbs a neutron becomes uranium-236 and then splits, releasing about 200 MeV in total. That is more per reaction than fusion, but it is shared among 236 nucleons, so each gains only about 0.85 MeV. Per nucleon, and so per kilogram, fusion releases about four times more.
---
$ N = \frac{1000\,\text{g}}{5\,\text{g/mol}} \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}\,\text{/mol} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{26}
To see what that means in practice, count the reactions in one kilogram of an equal deuterium–tritium mixture. One deuterium plus one tritium nucleus weighs about 5 atomic mass units, so a mole of pairs weighs about 5 grams. Dividing 1000 grams by 5 grams per mole and multiplying by Avogadro's number gives the number of pairs, and so the number of reactions.
---
$ E \approx 1.2 \times 10^{26} \times 17.6\,\text{MeV} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-13}\,\text{J/MeV} \approx 3.4 \times 10^{14}\,\text{J}
Multiply the number of reactions by the energy of each, and convert MeV to joules. The result is the energy from burning the whole kilogram completely.
---
$ \frac{3.4 \times 10^{14}\,\text{J}}{4.18 \times 10^{12}\,\text{J/kt}} \approx 80\,\text{kt}
A kiloton of TNT is defined as 4.18 × 10¹² joules, so dividing converts the energy into the unit used for explosions. One kilogram of deuterium–tritium fuel, fully burned, matches about 80 thousand tonnes of TNT. Complete fission of one kilogram of uranium-235 gives about 20 kilotons, so the factor of four from the per-nucleon comparison holds.
:::

## Why there is no limit on size

The factor of four is real, but it is not what made the hydrogen bomb so much more powerful than the fission bomb. The deeper difference is about how each fuel starts to react.

Fission fuel is always ready to go. A uranium-235 nucleus will split whenever a slow neutron hits it, at any temperature, and each split releases more neutrons. If enough uranium is gathered in one place, those neutrons trigger further splits faster than they escape, and a chain reaction runs away. The amount at which this happens is the **critical mass**. It is a limit as well as a threshold: a fission weapon must keep its fuel in pieces below critical mass until the moment of detonation, and there is a practical limit on how much fuel can be held safely apart and then brought together fast enough. Fission weapons therefore top out at yields of hundreds of kilotons.

Fusion fuel has no critical mass. Deuterium at room temperature does nothing at all, because its nuclei cannot get close enough to fuse; the electrical repulsion between them, the Coulomb barrier of the previous lesson, keeps them apart. They fuse only when heated to tens of millions of degrees, so that they collide fast enough to tunnel through that barrier. Gather a tonne of fusion fuel in one place and nothing happens. That makes the amount of fuel, and so the yield, unlimited in principle. The difficulty is all at the start: on Earth in the 1940s, the only way to reach such temperatures, even briefly, was a fission explosion.

::: note
Fusing light nuclei releases about four times more energy per kilogram than splitting uranium, because binding energy per nucleon rises far more steeply among light nuclei than it falls among heavy ones. Fusion fuel also has no critical mass, so there is no inherent limit on how much can be used. The obstacle is ignition, which needs temperatures of tens of millions of degrees.
:::

## The Super

The idea of a fusion bomb is older than the fission bomb itself. In September 1941, walking back from lunch at Columbia University in New York, Enrico Fermi wondered aloud to Edward Teller whether an exploding fission bomb might heat deuterium enough to make it fuse. Teller, a Hungarian-born physicist with a restless and combative mind, took up the idea and never let it go. At Los Alamos during the war it was known as the "Super", and Teller pressed for it so persistently that he clashed with the laboratory's leadership, who needed every available person on the fission bomb that might end the war. After 1945 work on the Super continued slowly, with little sign that Teller's approach would succeed.

What changed the pace was not physics but politics.

::: history 1949–1950 · Washington
On 29 August 1949 the Soviet Union tested its first fission bomb, years earlier than American officials had expected. The question of whether to answer with a crash programme for the Super went to the General Advisory Committee of the US Atomic Energy Commission, a panel of scientists chaired by Robert Oppenheimer, who had led the wartime laboratory at Los Alamos. In October 1949 the committee advised against it. The majority argued that a weapon of unlimited size could have no use except the destruction of whole populations, and might become "a weapon of genocide". Fermi and Isidor Rabi went further in a separate note, calling it "necessarily an evil thing considered in any light". On the other side were Teller, Ernest Lawrence and Luis Alvarez of Berkeley, and the commissioner Lewis Strauss, who argued that the Soviet Union would build it regardless and that the United States could not afford to be second. On 31 January 1950 President Harry Truman announced that work would continue. Days later the physicist Klaus Fuchs, who had worked at Los Alamos, was arrested in Britain for passing secrets to the Soviet Union, and the case for urgency seemed confirmed.
:::

The President's decision did not make the weapon possible. During 1950 the mathematician Stanislaw Ulam, working first with Cornelius Everett and then with Fermi, ran long calculations on Teller's design, partly by hand, and found that it would not work: the fusion reactions would die out instead of spreading through the fuel. Teller was bitterly unhappy at the result. Then, in early 1951, Ulam came to Teller with a new idea, Teller developed it further, and together they found an approach that worked. Its principle can be stated only in general terms: a fission explosion is used to create the conditions to ignite fusion fuel. Their joint report is dated 9 March 1951. The credit for the idea was disputed between the two men for the rest of their lives.

The argument of 1949 left lasting divisions. In 1954 a security board of the Atomic Energy Commission revoked Oppenheimer's security clearance after hearings in which his earlier opposition to the hydrogen bomb was held against him. Teller testified that he would prefer to see the country's vital interests "in hands which I understand better, and therefore trust more". Many physicists never forgave him, and for years he was shunned by former colleagues.

## Megatons

Thermonuclear weapons forced a new unit. A **megaton** is the energy of a million tonnes of TNT, about 4.2 × 10¹⁵ joules, or roughly 70 times the energy of the Hiroshima bomb.

::: history 1 November 1952 · Enewetak Atoll, Marshall Islands
The United States tested the first full-scale thermonuclear device, code-named Ivy Mike. It was not a weapon but a building-sized experimental apparatus that no aircraft could have carried. It yielded about 10 megatons, some 700 times the Hiroshima bomb. The small island of Elugelab, on which it stood, disappeared, leaving a crater nearly 2 kilometres across. Teller, unable to attend, watched a seismograph in California for the tremor that told him it had worked.
:::

The Soviet Union was not far behind. In August 1953 it tested a device of about 400 kilotons that used fusion but was of a kind that could not be scaled up to megatons. Its first true megaton-range weapon, known as **RDS-37**, was dropped from an aircraft on 22 November 1955 and yielded about 1.6 megatons. Among its principal designers was Andrei Sakharov, who later became the Soviet Union's best-known dissident and received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1975 for his campaigning for human rights and against nuclear testing.

::: history 1 March 1954 · Bikini Atoll
The American test Castle Bravo was expected to yield about 6 megatons. It gave 15, the largest explosion the United States ever produced, because the designers had miscalculated how the device would behave. The wind had shifted, and radioactive fallout, the dust and debris made radioactive by the explosion, drifted over inhabited atolls. People on Rongelap, about 180 kilometres away, saw white ash fall like snow; they suffered radiation burns and were evacuated only after two days. The 23 crew of the Japanese fishing boat *Lucky Dragon No. 5*, outside the declared danger zone, fell ill, and its radio operator, Aikichi Kuboyama, died that September. In Japan, nine years after Hiroshima, the incident caused national outrage, and Castle Bravo turned fallout into a public concern around the world.
:::

Other nations followed: Britain tested a thermonuclear weapon in 1957, China in 1967 and France in 1968.

::: history 30 October 1961 · Novaya Zemlya, Soviet Arctic
The largest explosion ever made was a Soviet test now known as **Tsar Bomba**. It yielded about 50 megatons, roughly 3,000 times the Hiroshima bomb and more than all the explosives used in the Second World War put together. It had been designed for about 100 megatons and deliberately scaled back, partly to limit fallout. The mushroom cloud rose about 60 kilometres, far into the stratosphere. It was too large to be a practical weapon; it was a demonstration of what unlimited yield meant.
:::

## The test ban

By the early 1960s testing had spread radioactive material across the whole planet. Strontium-90, a radioactive element that the body treats like calcium and stores in bones and teeth, was showing up in the milk teeth of children; a survey in St. Louis collected hundreds of thousands of them and found the levels rising with the years of testing. The Cuban missile crisis of October 1962, when the two superpowers came close to nuclear war, gave the leaders on both sides a reason to find some agreement.

On 5 August 1963 the United States, the Soviet Union and Britain signed the **Partial Test Ban Treaty** in Moscow. It banned nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in outer space and underwater, the places from which fallout could spread. It did not ban tests underground, because the three powers could not agree on how to verify a full ban, and underground testing continued for decades. France and China did not sign, and kept testing in the atmosphere until 1974 and 1980 respectively. A Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty, banning all nuclear explosions, was opened for signature in 1996, but because several of the countries whose ratification it requires have not ratified it, it has never entered into force.
