The fission bombs of 1945 released the energy of about 15 and 21 kilotons of TNT. Within a decade both superpowers had tested weapons hundreds of times more powerful, using fusion, the reaction that powers the Sun. This lesson covers why fusion releases more energy per kilogram than fission, and the history of how the hydrogen bomb came about.

## Why fusion yields more per kilogram

The binding energy curve rises far more steeply on the light side than it falls on the heavy side.

::: model binding-curve
Compare the jump from hydrogen to helium with the gentle drop from iron to uranium. Each nucleon gains far more binding in fusion.
:::

A standard example is the fusion of deuterium (hydrogen-2) and tritium (hydrogen-3), the reaction with the lowest temperature requirement:

::: equation Deuterium–tritium fusion
{}^{2}\mathrm{H} + {}^{3}\mathrm{H} \to {}^{4}\mathrm{He} + n + 17.6\,\text{MeV}
:::

::: steps Energy per kilogram: fusion against fission
$ \frac{17.6\,\text{MeV}}{5\,\text{nucleons}} \approx 3.5\,\text{MeV per nucleon}
Five nucleons go in. Each ends up about 3.5 MeV more tightly bound, on average.
---
$ \frac{200\,\text{MeV}}{236\,\text{nucleons}} \approx 0.85\,\text{MeV per nucleon}
Fission of uranium-235 releases about 200 MeV, shared over 236 nucleons.
---
$ N = \frac{1000\,\text{g}}{5\,\text{g/mol}} \times 6.02 \times 10^{23}\,\text{/mol} \approx 1.2 \times 10^{26}
The number of reactions in a kilogram of deuterium–tritium mixture: one reaction per 5 grams per mole.
---
$ E \approx 1.2 \times 10^{26} \times 17.6\,\text{MeV} \times 1.60 \times 10^{-13}\,\text{J/MeV} \approx 3.4 \times 10^{14}\,\text{J} \approx 80\,\text{kt}
Convert to joules and divide by 4.18 × 10¹² J per kiloton. Complete fission of a kilogram of uranium-235 gives about 20 kt, so fusion yields about four times more per kilogram.
:::

The deeper difference is not the factor of four. Fission fuel has a critical mass: too much of it in one place starts a chain reaction, which limits how large a fission weapon can practically be. Fusion fuel has no critical mass. It does nothing at all until it is heated to tens of millions of degrees, so there is no inherent limit on the amount, or on the yield. The difficulty is the heat: on Earth in the 1940s, only a fission explosion could produce such conditions.

::: note
Fusion of light nuclei releases about four times more energy per kilogram than fission, and fusion fuel has no critical mass. The obstacle is ignition, which needs temperatures that only a fission explosion could then create.
:::

## The Super

::: history 1941–1950 · New York and Los Alamos
In 1941 Enrico Fermi remarked to Edward Teller that a fission bomb might heat deuterium enough to fuse it. Teller pursued the idea, called the "Super", through the war and after, but by 1950 calculations by Stanislaw Ulam and Cornelius Everett, and by Fermi and Ulam, showed that his design would not work. Then, on 29 August 1949, the Soviet Union tested its first fission bomb. The US Atomic Energy Commission's General Advisory Committee, chaired by Robert Oppenheimer, advised against a crash programme; Fermi and Isidor Rabi called the weapon "necessarily an evil thing considered in any light". On 31 January 1950 President Truman ordered work to continue.
:::

In early 1951 Ulam and Teller found an approach that worked: a fission explosion is used to create the conditions to ignite fusion fuel. Their report is dated 9 March 1951.

The decision left lasting divisions. In 1954 a security board revoked Oppenheimer's clearance, after hearings in which his earlier opposition to the hydrogen bomb was held against him and Teller testified that he would prefer to see the country's vital interests "in hands which I understand better". Many physicists never forgave Teller.

## Megatons

A megaton is the energy of a million tonnes of TNT, about 4.2 × 10¹⁵ J, or roughly 70 Hiroshima bombs.

::: history 1 November 1952 · Enewetak Atoll, Marshall Islands
The United States tested the first full-scale thermonuclear device, Ivy Mike, a building-sized experimental apparatus that no aircraft could carry. It yielded about 10 megatons, some 700 times the Hiroshima bomb, and the island of Elugelab disappeared, leaving a crater nearly 2 km across.
:::

The Soviet Union tested a smaller device of about 400 kilotons in August 1953, of a kind that could not be scaled up to megatons. Its first megaton-range thermonuclear weapon, **RDS-37**, was dropped from an aircraft on 22 November 1955 and yielded about 1.6 megatons. Andrei Sakharov, later a Nobel Peace laureate, was among its principal designers.

::: history 1 March 1954 · Bikini Atoll
The US test Castle Bravo was expected to yield about 6 megatons and gave 15, the largest American test ever. The designers had miscalculated. Wind carried radioactive fallout over inhabited atolls: people on Rongelap, about 180 km away, suffered radiation burns and were evacuated only after two days. The 23 crew of the Japanese fishing boat *Lucky Dragon No. 5*, outside the declared danger zone, fell ill; its radio operator, Aikichi Kuboyama, died that September. Bravo turned fallout into a worldwide public concern.
:::

Britain tested a thermonuclear weapon in 1957, China in 1967 and France in 1968.

::: history 30 October 1961 · Novaya Zemlya, Soviet Arctic
The largest explosion ever made, the Soviet test known as **Tsar Bomba**, yielded about 50 megatons, roughly 3,000 Hiroshimas and more than all the explosives used in the Second World War. It had been designed for about 100 megatons and deliberately scaled back, partly to limit fallout. The mushroom cloud rose about 60 km. It was a demonstration rather than a practical weapon.
:::

## The test ban

By the early 1960s, strontium-90 from atmospheric tests was measurable in children's teeth around the world. On 5 August 1963 the United States, the Soviet Union and Britain signed the **Partial Test Ban Treaty** in Moscow, banning nuclear tests in the atmosphere, in outer space and underwater. Underground testing continued. France and China did not sign and tested in the atmosphere until 1974 and 1980. A Comprehensive Nuclear-Test-Ban Treaty was opened for signature in 1996 but has not entered into force.
