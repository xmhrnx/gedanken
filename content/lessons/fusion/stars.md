The Sun radiates about 3.8 × 10²⁶ watts and has done so, give or take, for 4.6 billion years. Explaining where that energy comes from took most of a century and needed relativity, nuclear physics and quantum mechanics together.

## Too old to be burning

In the 19th century the only candidate energy sources were chemistry and gravity. A Sun made of coal would burn out in a few thousand years. Hermann von Helmholtz and Lord Kelvin proposed that the Sun was slowly shrinking, turning gravitational energy into heat. That could last a few tens of millions of years, and Kelvin used it to argue that the Earth could not be much older. Geologists and Darwin needed hundreds of millions of years at least. The discovery of radioactivity showed that rocks were billions of years old, and the Sun had to be older still.

::: history August 1920 · Cardiff, Wales
Arthur Eddington, addressing the British Association, pointed to Francis Aston's new measurement: four hydrogen atoms weigh about 0.7% more than one helium atom. If the Sun turned hydrogen into helium, that mass would be released as energy by E = mc², enough to keep it shining for some 10 billion years. "What is possible in the Cavendish Laboratory may not be too difficult in the Sun," he said. He had no mechanism, and physicists objected that the Sun's core, at tens of millions of kelvin by his own estimate, was far too cool to push protons together against their electrical repulsion.
:::

The objection was correct by classical physics. Quantum tunneling, applied to stars by Robert Atkinson and Fritz Houtermans in 1929, removed it; the next lesson covers how. What remained was to find the actual reactions.

## Bethe's reactions

::: history 1938–1939 · Washington and Ithaca
In March 1938 George Gamow and Edward Teller convened a small conference in Washington on stellar energy. Hans Bethe, a nuclear physicist at Cornell who knew little astrophysics, went reluctantly and came back with the problem. Within weeks, working through the light nuclei systematically, he had found the two reaction cycles that power stars, one of them with Charles Critchfield. Bethe's paper "Energy Production in Stars" appeared in March 1939. Carl Friedrich von Weizsäcker found the carbon cycle independently in Germany. Bethe received the 1967 Nobel Prize for this work.
:::

In the Sun, about 99% of the energy comes from the **proton–proton chain**. Its main branch has three steps.

::: equation The proton–proton chain
p + p &\to {}^{2}\mathrm{H} + e^+ + \nu_e \\ {}^{2}\mathrm{H} + p &\to {}^{3}\mathrm{He} + \gamma \\ {}^{3}\mathrm{He} + {}^{3}\mathrm{He} &\to {}^{4}\mathrm{He} + 2p
:::

In the first step two protons fuse into a deuteron (²H, a proton and neutron), and one proton turns into a neutron by emitting a positron and a neutrino. That conversion goes through the weak force, which makes it extraordinarily slow: an average proton in the Sun's core waits around 10 billion years before it takes part. This bottleneck is why the Sun burns steadily for billions of years instead of exploding. The deuteron then captures another proton within about a second, and two helium-3 nuclei combine in a few hundred thousand years more. The first two steps happen twice for each helium-4 produced.

The net result is four protons in, one helium-4 out.

::: steps Energy from the proton–proton chain
$ 4\,{}^{1}\mathrm{H} \to {}^{4}\mathrm{He} + 2\nu_e + \text{energy}
Using neutral atoms, the two positrons annihilate with two of the four electrons, and the other two electrons end up in the helium atom, so the bookkeeping works with atomic masses.
---
$ \Delta m = 4(1.007825\,\text{u}) - 4.002603\,\text{u} = 0.028697\,\text{u}
Four hydrogen atoms outweigh one helium atom by about 0.7% of their mass.
---
$ E = 0.028697\,\text{u} \times 931.5\,\text{MeV/u} \approx 26.7\,\text{MeV}
Converted with E = mc². About 2% is carried off by the neutrinos, which escape the Sun directly; the rest heats the core.
:::

::: model binding-curve
Pick hydrogen, then helium-4. That first step is the steepest climb on the whole curve, which is why hydrogen fusion is such a rich fuel.
:::

## How long the Sun lasts

::: steps The Sun's hydrogen-burning lifetime
$ E \approx 0.007 \times 0.1 \times M_{\mathrm{Sun}} c^2
Fusion releases 0.7% of the mass burned, and only the core, roughly a tenth of the Sun's mass, gets hot enough to burn.
---
$ E \approx 0.0007 \times 2.0 \times 10^{30}\,\text{kg} \times \left(3.0 \times 10^8\,\text{m/s}\right)^2 \approx 1.3 \times 10^{44}\,\text{J}
Insert the Sun's mass and the speed of light.
---
$ t \approx \frac{1.3 \times 10^{44}\,\text{J}}{3.8 \times 10^{26}\,\text{W}} \approx 3 \times 10^{17}\,\text{s} \approx 10^{10}\,\text{years}
Divide by the luminosity. The Sun is about halfway through.
:::

Every second the Sun converts about 600 million tonnes of hydrogen into helium and loses about 4 million tonnes of mass as energy.

::: note
Stars shine by fusing hydrogen into helium. Four protons become one helium nucleus, 0.7% of the mass becomes energy, and a slow weak-force step sets the pace so that a star like the Sun lasts about 10 billion years.
:::

## The CNO cycle

Bethe's other cycle uses carbon, nitrogen and oxygen nuclei as catalysts: a carbon-12 nucleus captures four protons one at a time, with two beta-plus decays along the way, and at the end throws off a helium-4 nucleus and is restored as carbon-12. The net reaction and energy are the same as the proton–proton chain. Because carbon has six protons, the electrical barrier is higher, so the cycle needs higher temperatures and is extremely sensitive to them. It supplies about 1% of the Sun's energy but dominates in stars more than about 1.3 times the Sun's mass. Neutrinos from the CNO cycle in the Sun were finally detected by the Borexino experiment in 2020.
