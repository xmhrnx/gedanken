The Sun gives off about 3.8 × 10²⁶ watts of power, and the rocks of the Earth and Moon show that it has been doing so, give or take, for about 4.6 billion years. For most of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries nobody could say where that energy came from. Every known source ran out far too soon. The answer, when it came, needed three new pieces of physics working together: relativity to say how much energy was locked in matter, nuclear physics to say which reactions could release it, and quantum mechanics to explain how those reactions could happen at all. This lesson follows how the puzzle was solved and works out the two numbers that matter most, the energy released when hydrogen becomes helium and how long that keeps a star like the Sun alive.

## What could keep the Sun shining?

In the middle of the nineteenth century physicists had just learned that energy is conserved: it can change form, but it cannot be made from nothing. So the Sun's light had to be paid for, and the question was what with. The obvious candidate was chemistry. But a Sun made entirely of coal and oxygen, burning fast enough to give off its observed power, would be reduced to ash in a few thousand years. Human history alone is longer than that.

The other known store of energy was gravity. Hermann von Helmholtz, and later William Thomson, Lord Kelvin, pointed out that if the Sun were slowly shrinking, every part of it would be falling a little closer to the centre. Falling releases energy, just as a dropped stone speeds up, and in a gas that energy turns into heat. A Sun contracting by a few tens of metres a year could keep shining at its present rate. The question was how long.

::: steps How long gravity could power the Sun
$ E_{\mathrm{grav}} \approx \frac{G M^2}{R}
The energy released by assembling a ball of mass M and radius R from scattered material is of order GM²/R, where G is Newton's gravitational constant. The exact number in front depends on how the mass is spread inside the ball, but for an estimate a factor of order one does not matter. This is the most energy the Sun could have released by contracting to its present size.
---
$ E_{\mathrm{grav}} \approx \frac{6.67 \times 10^{-11} \times \left(2.0 \times 10^{30}\right)^2}{7.0 \times 10^{8}}\,\text{J} \approx 4 \times 10^{41}\,\text{J}
Put in the Sun's mass, 2.0 × 10³⁰ kg, and its radius, 7.0 × 10⁸ m, with everything in SI units. The result is enormous by everyday standards, but the Sun spends energy at an enormous rate too, so the only meaningful test is to compare the two.
---
$ t \approx \frac{4 \times 10^{41}\,\text{J}}{3.8 \times 10^{26}\,\text{W}} \approx 10^{15}\,\text{s} \approx 3 \times 10^{7}\,\text{years}
Dividing the stored energy by the power output gives the time it would last, since a watt is a joule per second. About thirty million years: far longer than coal, but still a definite limit.
:::

Kelvin took this limit seriously and turned it on the geologists. If the Sun was at most a few tens of millions of years old, then so was the Earth, and he argued that the slow processes geologists described, and the long ages Charles Darwin needed for natural selection, simply had not had time to happen. The geologists were sure he was wrong but could not say why. The answer came from an unexpected direction. Radioactivity, discovered in 1896, revealed a new source of energy inside atoms, and it also gave a clock: by measuring how much of a radioactive element in a rock had decayed, physicists could date the rock. By the 1920s rocks were being dated at billions of years. The Sun had to be at least that old, so it had to run on something a hundred times richer than gravity.

## A clue from weighing atoms

::: history August 1920 · Cardiff, Wales
Arthur Eddington, the leading theorist of stellar interiors, gave the presidential address to the physics section of the British Association. He pointed to a measurement just made by Francis Aston at Cambridge, who had built an instrument precise enough to compare the masses of atoms. Four hydrogen atoms, Aston found, weigh slightly more than one helium atom, by a little under 1%. Eddington took Einstein's E = mc² at its word: if a star could somehow build helium out of hydrogen, that missing mass would come out as energy, enough to keep the Sun shining for something like 10 billion years. "What is possible in the Cavendish Laboratory may not be too difficult in the Sun," he said, thinking of Ernest Rutherford, who had just shown that nuclei could be changed artificially and had moved to Cambridge to lead the Cavendish Laboratory. He had no mechanism, only an argument from the numbers.
:::

Eddington's idea had a serious problem, and his critics saw it at once. A helium nucleus contains two protons, and protons are positively charged, so they repel each other. To fuse, two protons must be pushed close enough together for the short-range nuclear force to take over, and the electrical repulsion at that distance is fierce. Eddington's own models put the centre of the Sun at tens of millions of kelvin, which sounds hot but gives protons only a thousandth of the energy they would need to get that close by classical physics.

The objection was correct as far as classical physics went. What removed it was quantum mechanics: in 1929 Robert Atkinson and Fritz Houtermans showed that protons can sometimes pass through the electrical barrier without having enough energy to climb over it, a process called tunnelling, which the next lesson explains. With that settled, the question became a concrete one. Which nuclear reactions actually take place in the Sun, in what order, and how fast?

## Bethe's reactions

::: history 1938–1939 · Washington and Ithaca
In March 1938 George Gamow and Edward Teller organised a small conference in Washington to bring astrophysicists and nuclear physicists together on the problem of stellar energy. Hans Bethe, a nuclear physicist at Cornell, went reluctantly; he knew little about stars. But he came back with the problem in his head. Bethe knew the properties of light nuclei better than almost anyone, and he worked through the possible reactions one by one, asking which could proceed at the Sun's temperature and density. With his student Charles Critchfield he worked out the proton–proton chain, and on his own he found a second route that uses carbon as a catalyst. His paper "Energy Production in Stars" appeared in March 1939. Carl Friedrich von Weizsäcker found the carbon cycle independently in Germany at about the same time. Bethe received the Nobel Prize in Physics for this work in 1967.
:::

In the Sun, about 99% of the energy comes from the **proton–proton chain**. Its main branch has three steps.

::: equation The proton–proton chain
p + p &\to {}^{2}\mathrm{H} + e^+ + \nu_e \\ {}^{2}\mathrm{H} + p &\to {}^{3}\mathrm{He} + \gamma \\ {}^{3}\mathrm{He} + {}^{3}\mathrm{He} &\to {}^{4}\mathrm{He} + 2p
:::

The first step is the strangest. Two protons come together and produce a **deuteron**, the nucleus of heavy hydrogen (²H), which is one proton bound to one neutron. For that to happen, one of the two protons must turn into a neutron during the brief moment they are close, giving off a **positron** (the positively charged antiparticle of the electron) and a **neutrino** (a nearly massless, uncharged particle that hardly interacts with anything). Changing a proton into a neutron is the job of the weak nuclear force, and the weak force is extraordinarily reluctant. Almost every time two protons tunnel close together, they simply fly apart again. An average proton in the Sun's core waits around 10 billion years before it takes part in this step.

That slowness is not a flaw in the Sun; it is the reason the Sun is stable. If protons fused as readily as they collide, the core's fuel would be used up in moments and the star would blow itself apart. Instead the weak-force bottleneck rations the burning, so the Sun releases its energy steadily over billions of years.

Once a deuteron exists, the rest is quick by comparison. The deuteron captures another proton within about a second, making helium-3 (two protons and one neutron) and a gamma-ray photon. Two helium-3 nuclei then meet, typically after a few hundred thousand years, and combine into helium-4, handing back two protons. Since that last step uses two helium-3 nuclei, the first two steps must happen twice for each helium-4 made.

## How much energy comes out?

Add up the chain and the bookkeeping is simple: four protons go in, and one helium-4 nucleus, two positrons and two neutrinos come out. The energy released is the difference in mass, and the cleanest way to find it is to work with whole atoms, whose masses are measured very precisely.

::: steps Energy from the proton–proton chain
$ 4\,{}^{1}\mathrm{H} \to {}^{4}\mathrm{He} + 2\nu_e + \text{energy}
Write the reaction for neutral atoms rather than bare nuclei. Four hydrogen atoms bring four electrons. Two of them annihilate with the two positrons, turning their mass into energy that is part of the total, and the other two end up in the helium atom. So the mass balance can be done with tabulated atomic masses, with nothing left over except the neutrinos, whose mass is negligible.
---
$ \Delta m = 4(1.007825\,\text{u}) - 4.002603\,\text{u} = 0.028697\,\text{u}
Subtract the mass of one helium atom from the mass of four hydrogen atoms, both in atomic mass units (u, one twelfth of the mass of a carbon-12 atom). About 0.7% of the starting mass has gone missing. That missing mass is the binding energy of the helium nucleus: helium weighs less because its particles are tightly bound, and energy had to leave for them to become so.
---
$ E = 0.028697\,\text{u} \times 931.5\,\text{MeV/u} \approx 26.7\,\text{MeV}
Convert mass to energy with E = mc²; for atomic mass units the conversion factor is 931.5 MeV per u, where an MeV (mega-electronvolt) is the energy an electron gains crossing a million volts. About 2% of this is carried off by the neutrinos, which pass straight out of the Sun. The rest heats the core and, after a long journey outward, leaves the surface as sunlight.
:::

Twenty-seven MeV per helium nucleus may not sound like much, but compare it with chemistry. Burning a molecule of methane releases about 9 electronvolts. Per reaction, fusing hydrogen releases a few million times more energy than any chemical fuel, which is the factor that turns Kelvin's thirty million years into billions.

::: model binding-curve
Pick hydrogen, then helium-4. The climb between them is the steepest on the whole curve: helium's nucleons are bound far more tightly than hydrogen's, and that difference is the energy the Sun releases.
:::

## How long can the Sun last?

The same numbers give the Sun's lifetime. Not all of the Sun's hydrogen is available, though: only the core, where temperature and density are high enough, actually fuses, and the outer layers never mix down into it. Models of the Sun show that roughly a tenth of its mass will be burned before the core runs out of hydrogen.

::: steps The Sun's hydrogen-burning lifetime
$ E \approx 0.007 \times 0.1 \times M_{\mathrm{Sun}} c^2
Fusion turns 0.7% of the burned mass into energy, and about a tenth of the Sun's mass gets burned. Multiplying those two fractions by the Sun's total mass-energy Mc² gives the energy the Sun will release while burning hydrogen in its core.
---
$ E \approx 0.0007 \times 2.0 \times 10^{30}\,\text{kg} \times \left(3.0 \times 10^8\,\text{m/s}\right)^2 \approx 1.3 \times 10^{44}\,\text{J}
Put in the Sun's mass and the speed of light. This is about three hundred times the gravitational store calculated earlier, which shows at once why nuclear energy solved the age problem.
---
$ t \approx \frac{1.3 \times 10^{44}\,\text{J}}{3.8 \times 10^{26}\,\text{W}} \approx 3 \times 10^{17}\,\text{s} \approx 10^{10}\,\text{years}
Divide the energy by the power output, as before, and convert seconds to years. The Sun's hydrogen-burning life is about 10 billion years. At 4.6 billion years old, it is a little under halfway through.
:::

The same arithmetic run the other way shows how fast the Sun is changing. Dividing its power by c² gives the mass it turns into energy each second: about 4 million tonnes. Since that is 0.7% of the hydrogen burned, the Sun converts about 600 million tonnes of hydrogen into helium every second. That sounds alarming, but the Sun is so massive that over its whole life it loses less than a tenth of a percent of its mass this way.

## A second route: the CNO cycle

Bethe's other reaction sequence reaches the same end by a different path. A carbon-12 nucleus captures a proton, becoming nitrogen-13, which turns one of its protons into a neutron by emitting a positron. The resulting carbon-13 captures a second proton to become nitrogen-14, then a third to become oxygen-15, which again emits a positron and becomes nitrogen-15. When nitrogen-15 captures a fourth proton, it splits into a helium-4 nucleus and a carbon-12 nucleus, and the cycle can start again. The carbon is not used up. It acts as a **catalyst**, something that helps a reaction along and comes out unchanged, and the net result is once again four protons turned into one helium-4, with the same 26.7 MeV released. The cycle is named after the elements it passes through: carbon, nitrogen and oxygen.

Why does the Sun mostly use the slower-seeming proton–proton chain? Because a carbon nucleus has six protons, it repels an incoming proton six times more strongly than a single proton does, so the barrier is far higher and only much hotter cores can push protons over it often enough. Above that threshold the CNO cycle's rate climbs extremely steeply with temperature. In the Sun it supplies only about 1% of the energy, but in stars more than about 1.3 times the Sun's mass, whose cores are hotter, it takes over. The Sun's CNO cycle was long only a calculation; in 2020 the Borexino detector in Italy caught the neutrinos it produces, the first direct evidence that it runs.

::: note
Stars shine by fusing hydrogen into helium: four protons become one helium nucleus, and the 0.7% of mass that disappears is released as energy. The first step needs the weak force to turn a proton into a neutron, which is so rare that it rations the fuel, and that is why a star like the Sun burns steadily for about 10 billion years.
:::
