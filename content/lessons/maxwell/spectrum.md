Maxwell's equations allow electromagnetic waves of any wavelength. Visible light covers only a narrow band, from about 380 to 750 nanometres (billionths of a metre). Everything else, from radio waves longer than a football pitch to gamma rays smaller than an atomic nucleus, is the same phenomenon at a different scale.

## One equation for the whole range

All electromagnetic waves travel through vacuum at c. So wavelength and frequency are tied together: know one and you know the other.

::: equation Wavelength and frequency
c = f\lambda
:::

A wave with a longer wavelength has a lower frequency. An FM radio station at 100 MHz broadcasts waves 3 m long. A microwave oven at 2.45 GHz uses waves about 12 cm long. Green light at 550 nm oscillates about 5.5 × 10¹⁴ times a second.

::: model wave
Change the wavelength. For a wave of fixed speed, frequency rises as wavelength falls.
:::

## The bands

The bands are named by history and use, not by any sharp physical boundary. Wavelengths are approximate.

| Band | Wavelength | Typical sources |
|---|---|---|
| Radio | longer than about 1 m | broadcast antennas, pulsars |
| Microwave | about 1 mm to 1 m | ovens, radar, the cosmic background |
| Infrared | about 750 nm to 1 mm | warm objects, your skin |
| Visible | about 380 to 750 nm | the Sun, flames, LEDs |
| Ultraviolet | about 10 to 380 nm | the Sun, hot stars, sterilising lamps |
| X-rays | about 0.01 to 10 nm | X-ray tubes, gas around black holes |
| Gamma rays | shorter than about 0.01 nm | radioactive nuclei, supernovae |

X-rays and gamma rays overlap in wavelength. In practice the names often refer to the source: gamma rays come from atomic nuclei or particle processes, X-rays from electrons.

## Why the bands behave so differently

If radio and gamma rays are the same thing, why does one pass harmlessly through your body while the other damages DNA? Two reasons.

First, waves interact most strongly with things about their own size or with charges that naturally oscillate near their frequency. Radio waves are far larger than molecules and pass through walls; visible light matches the energy of electron transitions in molecules, which is why dyes have colours and why your retina can detect it; X-rays are small enough to slip between atoms and are stopped mainly by dense, heavy elements like the calcium in bone.

Second, light is absorbed and emitted in lumps, **photons**, each carrying energy proportional to frequency. The constant of proportionality is Planck's constant, h ≈ 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s. Why light comes in lumps belongs to the quantum chapters; here the formula is enough.

::: steps Photon energy from wavelength
$ E = hf
The energy of one photon of frequency f.
---
$ E = \frac{hc}{\lambda}
Substitute f = c/λ. Shorter wavelength means more energy per photon.
---
$ hc \approx 1240\,\text{eV} \cdot \text{nm}
Multiply h by c and convert units. An electronvolt (eV) is the energy an electron gains crossing one volt, 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J; chemical bonds are a few eV.
---
$ E \approx \frac{1240}{550}\,\text{eV} \approx 2.3\,\text{eV}
For green light at 550 nm. A photon of visible light carries a couple of electronvolts, comparable to a chemical bond. A 0.1 nm X-ray photon carries about 12,000 eV, enough to knock electrons out of any atom it hits.
:::

Above roughly 10 eV, in the far ultraviolet, single photons can ionise atoms: strip off electrons and break molecules. That, not intensity, is the line between radiation that heats and radiation that damages. A powerful radio transmitter only warms you; a weak X-ray beam can break chemical bonds.

## Windows in the sky

Earth's atmosphere absorbs most of the spectrum. Ozone stops most ultraviolet, water vapour and carbon dioxide absorb much of the infrared, and X-rays and gamma rays are stopped high up. Two broad windows remain: visible light and radio. That is why astronomy began with eyes, why radio telescopes came next, and why X-ray and gamma-ray astronomy had to wait for rockets and satellites.

::: note
Radio, microwaves, infrared, visible light, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays are all electromagnetic waves travelling at c. They differ only in wavelength, and so in frequency and photon energy.
:::

::: history 1800–1900 · Europe
The spectrum was found piece by piece. In 1800 William Herschel put a thermometer beyond the red end of a prism's spectrum and found it warmed fastest there: infrared. In 1801 Johann Ritter found that something beyond the violet blackened silver chloride faster than violet light: ultraviolet. Hertz made radio waves in 1887. Wilhelm Röntgen discovered X-rays in 1895 and Paul Villard gamma rays in 1900. Nobody knew at first that X-rays and gamma rays were light; that was confirmed when they were diffracted by crystals, X-rays in 1912 and gamma rays in 1914.
:::
