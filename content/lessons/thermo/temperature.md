In everyday speech "heat" and "temperature" mean much the same. In physics they are different quantities, and confusing them held up the subject for a century. A cup of boiling water is hotter than a bathtub of warm water, yet the bathtub can melt far more ice. The cup has the higher temperature; the bathtub can give up more heat.

## Temperature

*Temperature* is what a thermometer reads. Its defining property is about equilibrium: put two bodies in contact and energy flows from the one at higher temperature to the one at lower, until their temperatures are equal and the flow stops. If body A is in equilibrium with a thermometer, and body B gives the thermometer the same reading, then A and B will be in equilibrium with each other. That sounds obvious, but it is what makes thermometers meaningful at all, and it was eventually named the *zeroth law* of thermodynamics.

A thermometer needs some property that changes steadily with temperature: the length of a mercury thread, the pressure of a gas in a fixed bulb, the resistance of a wire. Scales are then fixed by reference points. On the Celsius scale, water freezes at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C at sea-level pressure. The **kelvin** scale has the same size of degree but starts at absolute zero, the lowest temperature possible, −273.15 °C. So T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15, and room temperature is about 293 K. Physical laws are simplest in kelvin, and most formulas in these lessons need it.

As the kinetic-theory lesson will show, temperature measures the average energy of random motion of a body's molecules.

## Heat

*Heat* is energy transferred from one body to another because of a temperature difference. It is energy on the move, like work, not something a body contains. What a body contains is *internal energy*: the total kinetic and potential energy of its molecules. Heating raises internal energy, but so can doing work: rub your hands and they warm with no hotter body involved.

The amount of heat Q needed to warm a body depends on what it is and how much there is:

::: equation Heat and temperature change
Q = mc\,\Delta T
:::

Here m is the mass, ΔT the temperature rise, and c the *specific heat capacity*, the energy needed to warm 1 kg by 1 K. For water c ≈ 4180 J/(kg·K), unusually large; for iron about 450, for copper about 385. The same heat that warms 1 kg of water by 1 K warms 1 kg of copper by about 11 K. This is why the bathtub wins: its mass is enormous, so it releases a great deal of heat for each degree it cools.

## Mixing

When bodies at different temperatures are brought together and insulated from everything else, the heat one loses is the heat the other gains.

::: steps Final temperature of a mixture
$ m_1 c_1 (T_1 - T_f) = m_2 c_2 (T_f - T_2)
Body 1 starts hotter, at T₁, and cools to the final temperature T_f; body 2 warms from T₂ to T_f. With no losses, heat out of one equals heat into the other.
---
$ m_1 c_1 T_1 + m_2 c_2 T_2 = (m_1 c_1 + m_2 c_2)\, T_f
Multiply out and collect the T_f terms on one side.
---
$ T_f = \frac{m_1 c_1 T_1 + m_2 c_2 T_2}{m_1 c_1 + m_2 c_2}
Divide. The final temperature is an average of the starting temperatures, each weighted by mc, the body's heat capacity.
:::

Pour 1 kg of water at 80 °C into 3 kg at 20 °C: T_f = (80 + 60)/4 = 35 °C. Drop a 1 kg iron block at 80 °C into the same 3 kg: its heat capacity is less than a ninth of the 1 kg of water's, and the water warms only to about 22 °C.

::: history 1760s–1850 · Glasgow, Munich, Manchester
Joseph Black in Glasgow was among the first to separate the two ideas clearly. He saw that equal masses of different substances needed different amounts of heat for the same warming, and that melting ice absorbs heat without getting warmer at all ("latent heat"). Black and most of his contemporaries thought heat was *caloric*, an invisible weightless fluid that flowed from hot to cold and was conserved. In 1798 Count Rumford, supervising cannon-boring in Munich, saw that a blunt drill could boil water for as long as the horses kept turning it: an apparently endless supply of caloric from friction. In the 1840s James Joule churned water with falling weights and measured the warming precisely, finding that a fixed amount of work always produced the same heat. His value was within about 1% of the modern 4.184 J per calorie. Heat was a form of energy transfer after all.
:::

::: note
Temperature says which way energy will flow; heat is the energy that flows. A body has a temperature and internal energy, but it does not contain heat.
:::
